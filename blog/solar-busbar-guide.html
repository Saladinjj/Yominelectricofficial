Solar Busbar: DC Interconnects for PV Arrays and Storage

ET
ET Engineering Team November 9, 2026 8 min read

A solar busbar does one job: it collects DC current from several strings or battery modules and delivers it to one device without the losses and the failure points of a chain of separate cables. What makes it different from a panel busbar is not the metal but the environment — direct current that will not self-extinguish an arc, clearance rules that change above 1000 V, and an enclosure that may sit in the sun for twenty years. The failures that matter are almost always at the joint, not in the bar.

Where the busbar sits in a PV system

On the array side, the busbar lives inside the combiner box. Strings land on fuse holders or breakers, and a common bar collects their outputs into one feeder to the inverter. On the storage side, the busbar lives inside the rack or the container, where it replaces a loom of cables between modules and the DC bus.

Both cases have the same economics. A single bar with several landing points is cheaper than several long cables, takes less space, and gives the installer one torque value to verify instead of ten terminations hidden behind each other. The cost is that a fault on the bar affects everything connected to it, which is why the isolation device upstream matters as much as the bar itself.

DC does not forgive the same mistakes as AC

The single most important difference is arc behaviour. An AC arc passes through zero twice per cycle and tends to extinguish; a DC arc does not. That changes two things in the enclosure: the clearance you must keep, and the isolation device you must provide.

Above 1000 V DC the spacing rules tighten noticeably, which is why a 1500 VDC design cannot simply reuse the layout of a 1000 V one. In the YOMIN range the difference is visible in the products themselves: 1000 V DC combiners for string aggregation, and separate 1500 VDC isolating switches for the higher-voltage architectures, rated IP66 for outdoor mounting. Match the busbar spacing and the barrier arrangement to the system voltage class, not to the current.

The second consequence is maintenance discipline. On a DC array, never open a joint under load on the assumption that the arc will stop on its own.

Aluminum or copper for the DC run

The same conductivity trade that governs panel bars governs array bars. Aluminum conducts about 61% as well as copper by volume, so it needs about 1.6 times the section for the same current — and then weighs about half as much.

That points to a simple split. Long runs along an array, and busbars that have to be carried up to a roof, favour aluminum. Crowded combiner boxes where every millimetre of section competes with fuse holders and cable glands favour copper, because the smaller section leaves room for everything else. In container storage the choice is usually made per interconnect: copper where the current is concentrated in a short link, aluminum where the bar spans the rack.

The insulation has to survive the weather, not just the voltage

An outdoor enclosure cycles through heating and cooling every day. That cycle drives two failure modes: differential expansion between the bar and its coating, and condensation on any surface that cools faster than the air around it.

Three finishes cover most of the range. Insulated hard bus gives a defined creepage path and mechanical protection in one part, which is what most combiner and distribution work needs. Spray coating on a tinned bar gives corrosion protection with less bulk, and suits bars that must be formed after coating. Nickel plating is the choice where the bar is handled repeatedly, where joints are opened for service, or where abrasion would wear through a softer finish.

String combining: the bar that replaces a terminal block

In a combiner box, the bar is doing the work a row of terminal blocks would do, with fewer parts and less voltage drop. The design decisions are unglamorous and decisive:

  • Landing pitch. The distance between landing points has to match the fuse holder spacing, not be chosen for convenience. A bar drilled to the wrong pitch forces cable dressing that puts strain on the fuse terminals.
  • Barrier arrangement. Positive and negative bars need separation that matches the DC voltage class, and that separation must survive a technician working with a tool inside the box.
  • One torque value, documented. Every landing point on the bar should use the same fastener and the same torque, printed inside the lid. Mixed hardware is how a box ends up with two joints nobody checked.

A combiner built this way is quicker to assemble, easier to inspect, and easier to fault-find than one wired point to point.

Container storage interconnects are a different shape of problem

In a rack or a container, the busbar has to absorb tolerance stack-up between modules that were never designed to be perfectly aligned, and it has to do it while carrying hundreds of amps in a space that vibrates during transport.

That is what soft busbars are for. A laminated flexible link takes up the misalignment, keeps the joint faces parallel, and moves the flexing away from the terminal. In the YOMIN energy-storage range these are quoted as storage connectors in copper or aluminum, from 100 pieces, for container and commercial storage applications.

The practical rule: use a rigid bar for the run, and a flexible link at every interface where two assemblies meet. A rigid bar bolted across a module interface will either preload the terminals or crack at the bend, and both outcomes appear after commissioning rather than during it.

Commissioning checks that actually catch a bad joint

Three checks, in this order, catch almost everything that will fail in the first year.

  • Torque audit. Verify every landing point with a torque wrench and mark it. Do it again after the first full-load thermal cycle, because aluminum joints settle.
  • Thermal imaging under load. Scan the bar and every joint with the array at working current. A joint a few degrees warmer than its neighbours is a joint that will fail; the absolute temperature matters less than the difference.
  • Insulation resistance and continuity. Confirm the barrier arrangement is intact after the lid has been closed and the cables dressed, because that is when clearance is usually lost.

A thermal scan at commissioning costs an hour and finds the joints that would otherwise be found by a string outage in the second summer.

What to send for a quotation

  • System voltage class: 1000 V DC or 1500 V DC.
  • Continuous current per bar, and the number of landing points with their pitch.
  • Indoor or outdoor, and the enclosure ingress rating the bar must live with.
  • Finish: tinned, nickel-plated, insulated, or a flexible laminated link.
  • Drawing or dimensioned sketch, plus quantity and whether the part is formed before or after coating.

With those five, the bar can be quoted against the actual installation rather than against a catalogue section. YOMIN produces aluminum and copper busbars for solar array and storage applications to customer drawings from 100 pieces.

Frequently asked questions

What is a solar busbar used for?

It collects DC current from several strings or battery modules and delivers it to one device, replacing a chain of separate cables. In a PV plant it usually sits inside the combiner box; in storage it sits inside the rack or container.

Why does 1500 V DC need a different busbar layout?

A DC arc does not pass through zero and will not self-extinguish, so clearance and creepage rules tighten above 1000 V. A 1500 VDC design needs wider spacing and a barrier arrangement built for that voltage class, not a reused 1000 V layout.

Should a solar busbar be aluminum or copper?

Aluminum for long runs and roof-mounted bars, because it needs about 1.6 times the copper section but weighs about half. Copper where the enclosure is crowded and the smaller section leaves room for fuse holders and glands.

Why use a flexible busbar in a battery container?

Because modules are never perfectly aligned and the container vibrates in transport. A laminated flexible link absorbs the misalignment, keeps joint faces parallel, and moves flexing away from the terminal.
