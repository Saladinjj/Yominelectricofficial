How Much Does a Current Transformer Cost? A Pricing Guide for Buyers

ET
ET Engineering Team 7 min read August 22, 2026

A current transformer (CT) typically costs between $3 and $150+ per unit, depending on type, accuracy class, ratio, and order volume. Small solid-core ring CTs for meter mounting are at the low end; high-accuracy instrument CTs for billing or MV installations sit at the top. This guide breaks down what drives the price so you can budget accurately — and avoid overpaying or buying the wrong class.

Typical price ranges by type

The figures below are typical FOB ranges for order volumes of 100–1,000 pieces (prices vary by market and specifications):

CT typeTypical unit priceTypical use
Solid-core ring CT (class 1.0)$3–$10DIN-rail meter mounting, monitoring
Solid-core ring CT (class 0.5s)$8–$20Submetering, billing-grade metering
Split-core CT$10–$40Retrofit monitoring without disconnecting cables
Bar-type / busbar CT$15–$60High-current feeders, switchgear
High-accuracy instrument CT (0.2s)$40–$150+Revenue metering, utility billing
MV / protection CTs (11–33 kV class)$100–$500+Substation protection and metering

What drives the price

  • Accuracy class: 0.2s and 0.5s CTs use better core materials and tighter winding control than class 1.0 — the biggest single cost factor.
  • Core material: silicon steel is standard; nanocrystalline cores cost more but reduce size and improve accuracy at low current.
  • Primary rating and window size: bigger windows (to fit large busbars) mean more core material and higher price.
  • Secondary rating: 5 A secondaries are common and cheaper; 1 A secondaries for long cable runs cost more.
  • Burden (VA): higher burden ratings require a larger core.
  • Insulation and certification: MID/IEC 61869 certified units with full test certificates cost more than uncertified equivalents.
  • Quantity: MOQ and volume discounts are significant — 1,000 pieces can cost 30–50% less per unit than 100.

Don't buy on price alone

Cheap CTs from unknown suppliers are the #1 source of metering accuracy problems. Three checks prevent bad buys:

  • Verify the ratio and class on the nameplate — and ask for the test report, not just the datasheet.
  • Confirm saturation behaviour: a CT that saturates below your fault or overload current will read wrong exactly when it matters.
  • Check certification: for billing use, require IEC 61869-2 / IEC 62053 compliance and factory test certificates.

Budgeting tips

  • For a submetering project, budget class 0.5s CTs — the accuracy pays for itself through fair billing.
  • For simple monitoring, class 1.0 is enough; don't pay for 0.5s you don't need.
  • Order spares (5–10%) — CT failures are rare but replacement lead times are long.
  • Buy CTs and meters from one supplier to guarantee matched ratios and burden ratings.

FAQ

How much does a current transformer cost?

Typical unit prices range from about $3–$15 for small solid-core ring CTs at volume, $10–$40 for split-core CTs, and $30–$150+ for high-accuracy (0.2s/0.5s) or high-voltage instrument CTs. Final price depends on ratio, accuracy class, window size, burden, and order volume.

Why do current transformer prices vary so much?

Cost drivers are accuracy class (0.2s costs more than 1.0), core material (silicon steel vs nanocrystalline), window/primary rating, insulation level (up to 0.66 kV typical vs MV), certification (MID, IEC), and quantity. A 5 A secondary CT also differs from a 1 A secondary.

What is the cheapest current transformer type?

Solid-core (ring-type) CTs for meter mounting are generally the lowest cost. Split-core CTs cost a bit more because of the hinged core and machining, but save installation labour because the conductor does not need to be disconnected.

Is it worth paying more for a higher accuracy class CT?

For revenue billing, yes — accuracy class 0.5 or better per IEC 62053/61869 is required and small errors compound over thousands of kWh. For basic monitoring, class 1.0 is usually sufficient and much cheaper.
