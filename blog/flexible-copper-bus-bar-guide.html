Flexible Copper Bus Bar: Stack, Current Rating and Termination

ET
ET Engineering Team November 13, 2026 8 min read

A flexible copper bus bar is a conductor built from many thin copper foils stacked and pressed into solid ferrules at each end. Between the ferrules the stack moves, so the bar carries a high current while absorbing the vibration, thermal expansion and assembly tolerance of the two things it connects. It is the standard answer wherever a rigid bar would have to be bent to fit, or would crack at a joint after a few thousand load cycles.

The specification has four decisions in it, in this order: total copper cross-section, stack construction, plating, and the hole pattern at each ferrule. Get the cross-section and the holes right and the part will fit; get the plating and insulation right and it will still be working in ten years.

Why foil instead of a solid bar

A solid copper bar is cheaper per kilogram and carries the same current in less space. It also has one serious weakness in battery and switchgear work: it transmits force. When a pack expands and contracts through a charge cycle, or when a cabinet door is slammed, a rigid bar transfers that movement into the terminal and the weld behind it. A laminated flexible bar turns the same movement into relative sliding between foils, which costs nothing.

  • Tolerance stack-up. Two terminals are never exactly where the drawing says. A flexible bar takes up several millimetres of misalignment without pre-load.
  • Vibration. Foil stacks survive the vibration duty of vehicle and marine packs that work-hardens and cracks a solid bar at a bend.
  • Thermal cycling. The stack accommodates differential expansion between aluminium cell housings and copper conductors.
  • Installation. No bending, no re-drilling, and the bar can be fitted after the modules are in place.

The foil stack is the default for cell-to-cell and module-to-busbar links because the ferrule can be formed into the exact pad geometry the pack needs while the middle stays free. Braid wins where the movement is rotational rather than axial.

Cross-section is the rating

Flexible bars are rated by copper area, exactly like rigid bars. The practical convention used across our busbar current carrying capacity guide is a current density between 2 and 4 A/mm², with the lower figure for enclosed or high-ambient installations and the upper figure for open, well-ventilated runs.

Two corrections matter more than the table. Insulation removes surface area, so a heat-shrink sleeve or a wrapped film pushes the design towards 2 A/mm². And a stack is not a solid bar: the foils are only as good as the weld into the ferrule, so the ferrule length and the weld quality set the real limit at the ends, not the middle of the bar.

Plating: bare, tin or nickel

Copper oxidises. On a bolted joint the oxide film is a resistive layer that heats under load, and heat accelerates the oxide growth — the failure is progressive and usually shows up as a hot joint at a routine thermographic survey.

  • Bare copper. Acceptable only if the joint is made immediately and protected, or if the bar is used in a sealed, dry enclosure.
  • Tin plating. Low contact resistance, good solderability, and the usual choice for busbars that will be bolted with plated hardware.
  • Nickel plating. Harder and more abrasion-resistant, and the preferred finish where the pad will be laser-welded to a cell terminal or where the bar sees repeated handling. The flexible bars we supply for LiFePO4 packs are nickel plated for this reason.

Match the plating to the mating surface. Nickel on nickel is a stable couple; nickel against bare aluminium is not, and the joint will need a transition washer or a different finish on one side.

Insulation

An insulated flexible bar is not simply a covered bar. The insulation has to survive the same flexing as the conductor, so the choice is between a loose sleeve, a heat-shrink tube that is allowed to slide, and a wrapped film that is bonded only at the ferrules.

OptionFlex behaviourBest fitCheck
Heat-shrink tubeSlides over the stack; must not be bonded mid-spanGeneral pack and cabinet linksWall thickness, shrink ratio, temperature rating
PET or PI film wrapThin, follows the foil movement closelyTight spaces, high-temperature zonesCut-through resistance at the ferrule edge
Silicone sleeveVery flexible, wide temperature rangeMarine and outdoor enclosuresMechanical protection, abrasion in routing

The ferrule edge is where insulation fails: it is the transition from a rigid welded block to a moving stack, and it is the point a wrapped film will chafe through first. Ask for the edge treatment to be described, not just the film grade.

Holes, bolts and torque

The pad geometry is the part that stops a project. A flexible bar is custom, so the hole pattern is free — which also means it is the buyer's responsibility.

  • Hole diameter from the bolt, not from the terminal: an M8 bolt needs clearance, and a slotted hole trades adjustment range for contact area.
  • Hole spacing must match the terminal pitch on both ends, measured on the actual hardware rather than taken from a datasheet.
  • Ferrule length must be long enough for the weld to be sound and for the washer to seat flat.
  • Anti-rotation — a two-hole pad or a keyed ferrule stops the bar turning while the bolt is torqued.

Send a dimensioned drawing or a photograph with a ruler in frame. A flexible bar quoted from a sketch is a guess; a bar quoted from a drawing is a part.

Frequently asked questions

How do I choose the cross-section of a flexible copper bus bar?

Start from the continuous current and apply a current density of 2 to 4 A/mm²: use 2 A/mm² for an enclosed or high-ambient run and 4 A/mm² for an open, ventilated one. A 200 mm² stack therefore covers roughly 400 A enclosed or 800 A open, and the ferrule weld must be rated for the same current.

Is nickel plating better than tin for battery bus bars?

Nickel is harder, more abrasion-resistant and the better choice where the pad will be laser-welded or handled repeatedly, which is why our LiFePO4 pack bars are nickel plated. Tin gives lower contact resistance and better solderability on bolted joints. Either way, match the plating to the mating surface.

Can a flexible bus bar be insulated after it is bent?

Yes, but the sleeve must be free to slide over the foil stack. Bonding insulation to the middle of a flexible bar restricts the movement the bar was bought for, and the film will fail at the ferrule edge. Specify the insulation with the bar so the edge treatment is designed in.

What should I send to get a quotation?

A dimensioned drawing of both pads (hole diameter, spacing and ferrule length), the total cross-section or the continuous current, the length between ferrules, plating and insulation requirements, and the annual quantity. The listed MOQ is 50 pieces on the standard builds.

Sourcing flexible copper bus bars for a battery or ESS build?

Send the pad drawing, cross-section, length and quantity. We will confirm the stack construction, plating and insulation against your pack layout and quote the nickel-plated or insulated build.
