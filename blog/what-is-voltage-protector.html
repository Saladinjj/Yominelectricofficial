What Is Voltage Protector

ET
ET Engineering Team November 23, 2026 10 min read

A voltage protector is a rail-mounted protection device that continuously measures the supply voltage and disconnects the load when that voltage moves outside a set window — too high, or too low — then reconnects automatically once the voltage has been stable inside the window for a set delay. It protects connected equipment from the slow, damaging excursions that fuses and circuit breakers are not designed to see: a neutral that drifts, a phase that drops out, a generator that comes back on at the wrong level, or a utility supply that sags every evening when the local network is loaded.

That description separates it from three devices it is routinely confused with. It is not a surge protective device, which clamps a microsecond transient; it is not a voltage stabiliser, which corrects the voltage continuously while passing the load; and it is not a circuit breaker, which responds to overcurrent rather than to voltage. Getting that boundary right is the difference between a device that solves a site's problem and one that sits in the board doing nothing relevant.

What a voltage protector actually protects against

The damaging events this device addresses last from a few hundred milliseconds to several seconds, which is exactly the window where nothing else in a typical board reacts. An overvoltage of 270 V on a 230 V supply will not trip a miniature circuit breaker; it will, however, stress power supplies, drivers and compressors until something fails. An undervoltage that pulls a motor's supply down during start will raise its current and heat it, and a contactor that chatters on a sagging supply will destroy its own coil.

  • Sustained overvoltage from a lost neutral, a tap changer fault, or a generator returning at high excitation.
  • Sustained undervoltage from an overloaded local network, an undersized cable run, or a phase imbalance on a three-phase supply.
  • Phase loss and phase sequence faults on three-phase models, which stop a motor instead of letting it run single-phased.
  • Repeated dips and recovery that make equipment restart repeatedly and shorten the life of compressors and pumps.

How the device works

The sequence is simple, and every adjustable model exposes the same three settings.

  1. Sense. A divider measures the line voltage continuously and compares it with the set thresholds.
  2. Decide. If the voltage stays outside the window for longer than the trip delay, the internal relay is released and the load is disconnected.
  3. Wait. The load stays off while the voltage remains out of range. Some models also enforce a fixed reconnection delay so that a supply which is flickering cannot cycle the load.
  4. Restore. Once the voltage has been inside the window for the recovery time, the relay closes and the load is re-energised without manual intervention.

The three settings that matter are the upper cut-off, the lower cut-off and the delay. A wide window protects less but trips rarely; a narrow window protects more but will disconnect a site on a supply that is merely poor. The recovery delay is the setting most often left at its default and most often the cause of a complaint that equipment restarts too quickly after an outage.

The four are complementary, and a board normally needs all of them in some form. A surge device at the incomer, a voltage protector on the sensitive feeder, a stabiliser where the supply varies continuously, and breakers on every circuit do different jobs and are not substitutes for one another.

Single-phase and three-phase models

Single-phase protectors are the volume product: a compact rail-mounted body that monitors one live-to-neutral voltage and switches a single load, commonly a domestic or small commercial circuit. Three-phase models monitor all three phases, and the useful ones also detect phase loss and phase sequence, because a three-phase load that loses one phase will run at reduced torque and draw excessive current on the remaining phases.

Single-phase or three-phase protector

The choice follows the load, not the board. A single-phase protector switches one live conductor and is the right device for a domestic board, a small commercial circuit or a single machine fed from one phase. A three-phase protector monitors all three phases and should be chosen wherever the load is a three-phase motor, because the fault that destroys those motors most often is not a voltage excursion at all — it is the loss of one phase, which leaves the motor running on the remaining two at reduced torque and excessive current.

Self-resetting or manual reset

A self-resetting protector restores the load by itself once the supply is healthy, which is what most domestic and commercial installations want: nobody is on site to press a button after a night-time outage. The trade-off is that the device will also reconnect a load into a supply that is still marginal, which is why the recovery delay matters more than the reset behaviour. Manual-reset models are chosen where an engineer should inspect the cause before the load is restored — typically on motors, pumps and equipment where a restart into a fault is worse than a stoppage.

Where voltage protectors are used

Domestic and small commercial boards use them to protect refrigeration, air conditioning and electronics from the excursions that follow a network fault. Agricultural and pumping installations use them because a phase loss on a three-phase pump motor is otherwise discovered only when the motor has burnt out. Workshops and machine shops use them ahead of CNC equipment and compressors, where a sag deep enough to drop a contactor causes a restart cycle that ruins work in progress. Generator-backed sites use them because the changeover back to mains is exactly the moment when the voltage can arrive at the wrong level.

Installation and wiring checks

  • Mount the protector downstream of the protective device for the circuit it controls, so the load is isolated when the relay opens.
  • Confirm the current rating against the actual load, not against the breaker rating alone.
  • Set the cut-off window against measured site voltage over a full day, not against the nominal figure.
  • Set the recovery delay against the equipment's own restart requirements, especially for compressors and pumps.
  • Where the device also protects against leakage or overcurrent, verify the trip thresholds against the upstream protection so the two coordinate.

Selection checklist

  • Single-phase or three-phase, and whether phase loss and sequence detection are required.
  • Current rating that covers the continuous load with margin.
  • Adjustable or fixed thresholds, and the range each threshold has to span.
  • Trip delay and recovery delay ranges, matched to the equipment being protected.
  • Self-resetting or manual reset, decided by whether anyone is on site after an outage.
  • Additional functions such as overcurrent or leakage protection where one device has to cover both.
  • Display and communication, if the site needs remote status rather than a local reading.
  • Module width and the rail space available next to the circuit it protects.

Frequently asked questions

What is a voltage protector used for?

It disconnects a load when the supply voltage stays above or below a set window for longer than a set delay, then reconnects automatically once the voltage is stable again. It protects equipment from sustained overvoltage and undervoltage, which circuit breakers and fuses are not designed to detect.

Is a voltage protector the same as a surge protector?

No. A surge protective device clamps a microsecond transient and keeps the load connected. A voltage protector responds to a sustained excursion lasting hundreds of milliseconds or longer by disconnecting the load. Most boards need both, at different points in the installation.

Can a voltage protector replace a voltage stabiliser?

No. A stabiliser corrects the voltage continuously and keeps supplying the load; a protector only disconnects and reconnects. Where the supply varies continuously and the load must stay energised, a stabiliser is the correct device, and the protector is a secondary safeguard.

What should the upper and lower cut-off be set to?

Set them from measured site voltage over a full day rather than from the nominal figure: the upper threshold above the highest normal level, the lower threshold below the deepest normal dip the equipment tolerates. A window that is too narrow will disconnect the site on a supply that is merely poor.

Why does equipment restart too quickly after an outage?

The recovery delay is usually left at its default. Extending it gives compressors time to equalise pressure and motors time to stop, which prevents the repeated restart cycle that shortens equipment life.

Does a voltage protector work on a generator supply?

Yes, and that is one of its stronger applications, because the changeover back to mains is the moment when voltage can arrive at the wrong level. The thresholds and delays have to be set for the generator's own regulation behaviour, not only for the utility supply.
