IEC 61869-2 Explained: Metering CT Accuracy Classes and Burden Limits

ET
ET Engineering Team November 4, 2026 11 min read

IEC 61869-2 is the part of the IEC 61869 family that specifies current transformers. It defines the accuracy classes a metering CT can be built to — 0.2S, 0.5S, 0.5 and 1.0 — and the protection classes 5P, 10P, PR and PX, together with the conditions under which those classes are actually guaranteed. The single most useful thing to understand about the standard is that a class is not a property of the transformer alone. It is a promise that holds only inside a declared burden, a declared current range and a declared frequency, and a CT ordered as “class 0.5” with no burden on the order can be perfectly compliant and still measure badly.

What the standard covers, and what it does not

The IEC 61869 family is organised by transformer type rather than by application. Part 1 carries the general requirements that apply to every instrument transformer, and part 2 carries the requirements specific to current transformers. The thermal ratings that decide whether a CT survives a fault, for example, are anchored in the general part, which is why a datasheet can be conforming on accuracy and still be under-specified on short-time current.

Older documents — and a great many datasheets still in circulation — cite IEC 60044-1 for the same transformers. That standard has been superseded, but the class designations it used are the same ones 61869-2 uses, so a 0.5 class CT described under either number means the same accuracy band. What changes is the detail of the type tests behind the claim.

What the standard does not do is choose the transformer for you. It will tell you whether a build is conforming at 0.5 class under 15 VA. It will not tell you that a 5 VA unit was the wrong purchase for a panel with 4 mm² secondary leads running twelve metres to a meter.

The metering classes

Four classes cover metering duty, and the choice between them is a commercial decision as much as a technical one, because it decides who absorbs the measurement error.

ClassWhat it is forWhere it is usually specified
0.2SThe tightest metering class, with the S extension to low currentUtility settlement points, inter-company transfer, large industrial billing
0.5SRevenue metering with the S extensionTenant billing between profit centres, utility revenue metering, substation feeder metering
0.5Standard commercial metering accuracyPanel meters, building management metering, internal cost allocation
1.0Indicating and monitoring accuracySwitchboard ammeters, drive and motor monitoring, alarm thresholds

There is no rule that a tenant meter must be 0.5S and no rule that a switchboard ammeter may be 1.0. The classes exist so that a buyer can state the commercial consequence of an error and let the supplier price the build that satisfies it. What is worth knowing is the direction of the cost: the step from 1.0 to 0.5 is small, and the step from 0.5 to 0.5S is where the price starts to move, because the S build is tested across a much wider current range.

What the S suffix actually buys

The S in 0.5S and 0.2S means the accuracy limits apply down to 1 % of rated primary current, instead of the 5 % floor used by the plain 0.5 and 1.0 classes. That sounds like a laboratory distinction until you look at a real load profile.

A 400/5 A CT on a feeder rated 400 A spends the night at 20 to 40 A. That is 5 % to 10 % of rated current — below or at the floor of a plain class 0.5 guarantee, and comfortably inside the range a 0.5S unit is tested over. The meter is not lying; it is being asked to hold an accuracy the purchaser never paid for. On a circuit with a wide day-night swing, the S suffix is the difference between a class that describes the installation and one that describes the test bench.

Our ym-0074 is a 250/5 A class 0.5 metering transformer with a 50-turn secondary winding, the build that suits a commercial feeder where the load stays in the upper part of the range. Where the same feeder has a deep night-time trough, the S class is the honest specification and the plain 0.5 unit should be recognised as a compromise.

Burden: the limit that voids the class

Burden is the total impedance the secondary has to drive, expressed in volt-amperes at rated secondary current. It is made up of the meter's own burden plus the resistance of the secondary leads, and the leads are usually the larger of the two by a wide margin.

A CT is tested at a stated burden. If the installed burden is higher, the core is driven harder, the ratio error and the phase displacement both move, and the class no longer holds — even though nothing on the nameplate is false. This is the most common way a conforming transformer ends up in a non-conforming measurement, and it is entirely avoidable at the ordering stage: state the burden, and state the lead length if it is unusual.

The arithmetic of lead resistance, and the point at which a 5 A secondary becomes the wrong choice against a 1 A secondary, is worked through in the guide to current transformer sizing calculation. The class definitions themselves, including how burden interacts with the accuracy class from the meter's side, are set out in the CT accuracy class guide.

Protection classes are a different animal

A protection CT is not asked to be accurate at rated current. It is asked to reproduce a fault current faithfully enough that a relay operates, and to do so without saturating. That is why protection classes are written differently.

DesignationWhat the number meansTypical duty
5P105 % composite error at 10 times rated primary currentFeeder overcurrent, general protection
5P205 % composite error at 20 times rated primary currentTransformer incomer, motor protection with high starting current
10P1010 % composite error at 10 times rated primary currentLess demanding protection where a wider error is tolerable
PRRemanence factor declared, for circuits where residual flux mattersProtection that must operate correctly after a previous fault
PXDefined by knee-point voltage and magnetising current rather than a classDifferential protection, where the relay setting depends on the actual magnetising curve

The distinction matters at the ordering stage because a metering class and a protection class cannot be substituted for one another. A 0.5 class CT used in a protection circuit will saturate at the fault current it was never designed to see; a 5P20 CT used for billing will be inaccurate at the low currents where most of the energy is actually consumed. Where a panel needs both functions, the usual answer is two cores in one housing, each built and tested to its own class.

Thermal ratings that decide whether the unit survives

Accuracy is the part of the datasheet that gets read. The thermal ratings are the part that decides whether the transformer is still in service after a fault.

Two figures matter. The continuous thermal current rating sets the primary current the unit can carry indefinitely, and it is normally at least the rated primary current — sometimes higher, which matters on a feeder that runs overloaded. The short-time thermal current rating, usually written Ith, sets what it survives for a stated duration; the general requirements of the family anchor this at a multiple of rated primary current for one second or three seconds, so a unit declared at eighty to one hundred times its primary rating for one second is describing a fault-withstand capability, not an accuracy capability.

A buyer comparing two quotations on price alone will often find that the cheaper unit has the same accuracy class and a lower short-time rating. On a feeder with a high prospective fault current, that difference is the whole specification.

What to put on the purchase order

Seven lines remove almost every ambiguity a CT order can carry.

Line on the orderWhat it prevents
Primary and secondary current, e.g. 400/5 A“400 A CT” leaves the secondary open to interpretation
Accuracy class, with or without SA class 0.5 unit is not a 0.5S unit, and the difference shows at night
Burden in VAWithout it the class is a claim with no test condition attached
Short-time thermal current Ith and durationThe rating that decides survivability after a fault
Insulation levelDecides whether the unit belongs on a 690 V system at all
Mounting: DIN rail, busbar clamp or bothDecides whether it fits the panel you have already built
Sealable terminal coverRequired wherever the measurement is the basis of a payment

Send those seven lines and the quotation can be compared line by line. Send “class 0.5 CT, 400 A” and two suppliers will quote two different products that both satisfy the sentence and neither of which necessarily satisfies the installation.

The one check before you approve

Take the burden figure from the quotation and add the lead resistance of the run you are actually installing. If the total is above the declared burden, the class in the quotation does not describe your installation, and no amount of certificate checking will fix it. Either shorten the run, increase the conductor size, or buy the next burden rating up. It is a five-minute calculation at the desk and a very expensive discovery afterwards.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between class 0.5 and class 0.5S on a current transformer?

Both are metering classes with a 0.5 % accuracy band, but the S version holds its limits down to 1 % of rated primary current, while the plain class 0.5 guarantee starts at 5 %. On a load that swings widely, the S class is the one that still describes the measurement at night.

What does burden mean on a CT datasheet?

Burden is the total impedance the secondary can drive, expressed in VA at rated secondary current. It is the meter burden plus the resistance of the secondary leads. A CT only holds its accuracy class at or below its declared burden, so a long secondary run can silently void the class.

Can I use a protection class CT for metering?

No. A 5P or 10P core is designed to reproduce fault current without saturating, and its accuracy at the low currents where energy is actually consumed is not specified to a metering class. Metering and protection need separate cores, even when they share one housing.

Does IEC 61869-2 replace IEC 60044-1?

Yes. IEC 60044-1 has been superseded by the IEC 61869 family, with part 2 covering current transformers. The class designations carried over, so a 0.5 class unit means the same accuracy band under either reference; what differs is the detail of the type tests behind the claim.

Specifying a metering current transformer?

Send the primary current, the secondary rating, the accuracy class, the burden in VA and the panel it goes into. We will confirm the class that actually holds at your load, and quote the matching transformer from the MSQ range.
