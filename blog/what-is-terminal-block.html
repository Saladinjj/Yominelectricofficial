What Is Terminal Block: Electrical Terminal Block Types and How to Choose

ET
ET Engineering Team November 21, 2026 12 min read

A terminal block is a reusable, insulated junction for electrical conductors. It holds one or more wires or bar ends in a fixed position, keeps live metal away from the enclosure, and lets a technician disconnect, test or re-terminate a circuit without cutting it. The part is passive: it carries current and clamps mechanically, but it does not switch, protect or measure. That is why a terminal block appears at almost every point in a panel where a wire arrives, changes size, splits, or leaves again.

Most people asking the question are specifying a panel or repairing one, and the answer changes with the clamp. A screw terminal block and a spring terminal block are both terminal blocks and behave very differently on a machine that vibrates. This guide covers what the part does, how it is built, which types exist in practice, and how to choose one without over- or under-specifying it.

What a terminal block does in a circuit

Every terminal block performs the same four jobs, and a part that does any of them badly will fail long before the conductor does. It makes a stable, low-resistance connection between conductors that may differ in material or cross-section. It holds that joint at a defined distance from neighbouring poles and from the enclosure, so clearance and creepage stay predictable. It keeps the joint accessible, so a circuit can be isolated or re-routed during maintenance rather than cut and re-made. And it gives the installer a place to label, which is why terminal blocks and wiring schedules are designed together.

  • Electrical: clamps the conductor so contact resistance stays stable through thermal cycling.
  • Mechanical: fixes the joint so vibration or a pulled cable does not migrate the wire out of the clamp.
  • Insulating: separates poles and phases and keeps live parts behind a finger-safe barrier.
  • Serviceable: provides a labelled, testable point instead of a taped or twisted joint.

Because the block is the meeting point of two different worlds — the panel builder's copper and the field electrician's cable — most terminal block failures are not material failures. They are interface failures: the wrong wire preparation, a clamp that was never tightened to its own specification, or a block chosen for the nominal wire and not for the wire someone will actually land in it five years later.

How an electrical terminal block is built

Strip away the catalogue photography and a terminal block is five decisions. The insulating body sets the dielectric behaviour and the colour code. The current bar and clamp set contact resistance and the wire range. The clamp element decides how the conductor is held and how many times the joint can be re-made. The mounting foot decides how fast the panel goes together. The accessory slot decides whether the finished row can be labelled and grouped.

The body material is where buyers most often try to save money and where the saving is least visible. A brass current path with a plastic body is a perfectly serviceable industrial part; the same brass path in a body that softens near a heat source is a warranty claim. If the enclosure runs warm, that is a body-material question, not a clamp question.

Screw terminal block vs spring clamp

This is the comparison that decides most projects, and it is not settled by preference. Screw clamps win where the joint will be opened again by someone with a screwdriver and a wiring schedule. Spring clamps win where nobody will ever re-check the joint and vibration is continuous.

How to choose a terminal block for a panel

Selection starts from the wiring schedule, not from the block. Work out what conductor actually arrives at each point — including the repair conductor, which is often a size larger or a different material — and then choose the clamp that accepts that range with margin.

DIN rail terminal blocks and the space they really take

A DIN rail terminal block is an assembly decision as much as an electrical one. The rail is a shared resource: every pole consumes width, every group needs an end stop, and every labelled row needs a marker strip that does not foul the duct cover. Panels that run out of room usually ran out of rail, not of enclosure. Plan the rail length from the pole count plus end stops plus spare capacity for the circuits that will be added after commissioning, because they always are.

The rail itself matters too. A block that clips securely on one rail profile may sit loosely on a deeper profile, and a loose block is a joint that moves. When a row is mixed — different clamp types, different widths — keep the end stops and the busbar or jumper arrangement consistent, otherwise the row will not stay aligned under a wire pull.

Materials: brass, plastic bodies and what the choice changes

Brass is the default current-carrying material in this product family because it machines well, holds a thread and keeps contact resistance low. Aluminium appears in lug-style and single-wire-hole blocks where large conductors and weight matter more than thread strength, and it needs the joint protection that any aluminium termination needs. Plastic, PC and PA bodies cover the ordinary industrial case; where a specification calls for a flame-retardant grade or a higher tracking index, that is a body-material decision made at the drawing stage, not something a buyer can add later.

Colour is not decoration. Colour-coded bodies let a technician confirm at a glance that the protective conductor, neutral and phase rows are separated, and the marking strip above the row is what makes a panel maintainable two years after the installer has left.

Installation and inspection points that actually matter

  • Prepare the conductor the clamp was designed for: correct strip length, correct ferrule where one is specified, no nicked strands.
  • Land one conductor per clamp unless the block is explicitly a multi-conductor type; doubled wires hide a loose joint.
  • Tighten to the clamp's own specification, then check a sample after the first thermal cycle rather than trusting the first pass.
  • Bond the earth row to the rail or earth bar as its own connection; the block does not create the earth path by being mounted.
  • Label the row before the ducts go on. After they are on, nobody will.
  • Re-inspect after transport or a long idle period: a joint that was correct in the workshop can be loose after a lorry and a winter.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a terminal block and a terminal?

A terminal is the single conductive point where a conductor is attached. A terminal block is the insulated assembly that contains one or more of those points, mounts them together and separates them from neighbouring poles. In practice the words are used loosely: what is sold as a terminal block is a housing plus clamp plus mounting, and the terminal is the metal inside it.

Is a terminal block the same as a connector?

Not quite. A connector is usually a two-part device designed to be mated and unmated, such as a plug and socket pair. A terminal block is a single part that accepts bare or ferruled conductors one at a time. Some terminal blocks accept plugs and some connectors are mounted on rails, which is where the two categories blur, but the intended use is different: connectors join cables, terminal blocks organise a panel.

Do terminal blocks need ferrules?

A screw clamp will accept a bare stranded conductor, but a ferrule keeps the strands together and gives the clamp a defined surface. Spring and push-in clamps are generally designed around a prepared conductor, and the block's own specification will say what preparation it expects. Use the preparation the clamp was designed for, not the preparation that is quickest.

Can a terminal block be used for earth or ground?

Yes, but it must be a grounding type bonded to the rail or to an earth bar, and that bond is part of the protective path. Mounting an ordinary block on a rail does not create an earth connection. Where several protective conductors land together, a neutral and ground bar assembly is the usual solution.

How many times can a screw terminal block be re-terminated?

There is no universal number, and any figure quoted without naming the clamp and screw is a guess. Repeated re-termination wears the thread and the saddle, and the practical check is inspection after the first few cycles: if the screw no longer holds torque smoothly, replace the block rather than adding more torque.

Are DIN rail terminal blocks required in control panels?

They are not a legal requirement, but they are the standard because they mount, group and label quickly on a shared rail. Panel and machine builders specify them for maintainability more than for electrical reasons. The exception is high-current work, where bolted busbar joints or dedicated power blocks do the job better.

Why do terminal blocks overheat?

Almost always at the clamp, and almost always for one of three reasons: the conductor was not prepared correctly, the clamp was not tightened to its own specification, or the block was chosen for a smaller conductor than the one that was landed. Body material rarely causes it. Check the joint first and the current path second.

Specifying terminal blocks for a panel build?

Send the wiring schedule — conductor sizes, pole count, working voltage and rail type — and we will match the clamp, grouping and accessories to it. Brass, plastic/PC and DIN rail versions are all available from one factory.
