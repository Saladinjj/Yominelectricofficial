Screw Terminal Block: How the Clamp Works and How to Specify One

ET
ET Engineering Team November 20, 2026 6 min read

A screw terminal block holds a conductor with a screw-driven clamp. Specify it by clamp opening and wire range first, then by body material, and only then by the printed current rating, because that rating is quoted at a reference ambient a real panel rarely reaches.

Buyers who order by current alone are the ones who discover at installation that a 6 mm conductor will not enter the block they bought. The clamp opening, not the amp figure, is the hard constraint, and it is the one number that cannot be corrected with a torque wrench.

What a screw terminal block is doing

The job is narrower than it looks: maintain a stable, low-resistance contact between a prepared conductor and a brass or copper current bar, for years, through thermal cycling, without maintenance. Every part of the design serves that one requirement.

The body insulates and locates. The metal insert carries the current. The screw generates the clamping force, and the clamp itself decides how that force is applied to the conductor. Most field failures trace back to the third and fourth of those, not to the first.

Three clamp builds and where each one fails

BuildHow it gripsStrengthWhere it fails
Rising cage clampA moving cage lifts as the screw turns, so the screw does not rotate against the conductorNo twisting of stranded wire; consistent contact on fine-stranded cableCage can be over-driven, and the screw thread strips before the operator feels resistance
Wire protector clampA curved plate sits between the screw tip and the conductorCheap, compact, and adequate for solid and coarse-stranded wireThe plate can bite through fine strands, and it hides a conductor that is only partly inserted
Stud and nutA threaded stud with a nut and washer compresses a lugHighest clamping force, easy to inspect, suited to large conductorsNeeds a proper lug and two tools; loosening under vibration if not restrained

The choice is usually made by conductor type rather than by current. Fine-stranded flexible cable belongs in a rising cage or a ferrule; solid building wire is fine under a wire protector; anything above the largest cage size moves to a stud.

Wire range is the constraint that cannot be argued with

A terminal block is sized by the conductor it must accept. The rated current tells you what the block can carry once a conductor is in it; the wire range tells you whether a conductor will fit at all.

Two practical rules follow. First, order by the largest conductor on the drawing, not the average, because one undersized block stops a whole row. Second, if the conductor is fine-stranded, allow for the ferrule: a ferrule adds a wall thickness on every strand bundle and can push a nominal size past the clamp opening.

Torque: both directions are a failure

Torque is set by screw size and clamp design, and the figure that matters is the one marked on the part, not a generic value from a catalogue table. Under-tightening leaves a joint with higher resistance than the circuit around it; the joint runs hot, the plastic softens, and resistance climbs further. Over-tightening splits the cage or strips the thread, which produces the same hot joint but without the warning.

The practical discipline is a calibrated driver set to the marked value, applied to every terminal, and re-checked once after the first thermal cycle. A terminal row where one screw was tightened by feel is a row with one unknown joint in it.

Body material: PC, PA, and when neither is right

Polycarbonate gives good impact strength and dimensional stability, and holds its shape well in a machined or moulded body. Polyamide, usually glass-filled, tolerates higher temperatures and resists oils and fuels better, at the cost of taking up moisture and moving slightly with it.

Neither is the right answer in a genuinely hot location. Where the ambient or the conductor temperature is beyond what a thermoplastic will hold, the block becomes a ceramic or a porcelain body — which is why ceramic blocks still exist in fuse and heating circuits despite being brittle and expensive.

Barrier, DIN rail, PCB or comb?

FormatMounted whereBest forLimitation
Barrier blockScrewed to a panel or chassisMixed voltages in one row, with a wall between each circuitBulkier than a DIN block for the same pole count
DIN rail blockClipped to 35 mm rail inside an enclosurePanel building and machine rows that change during commissioningNeeds rail, end stops and a plan for the rail itself
PCB terminal blockSoldered to a boardBringing field wiring onto a control boardCurrent is limited by the board, not by the block
Comb or fork busbarPlugged across a row of blocksDistributing one supply across many outletsThe comb, not the blocks, becomes the current limit

A comb or fork busbar is the item most often left out of a first order. It is also the part that decides the row's real current capability, because a row of blocks rated well above the load is still limited by the bar feeding it.

Derating is the number nobody applies

The printed current rating assumes a reference ambient and a single conductor at full size. A panel at elevated internal temperature, a row of blocks mounted side by side, and a bundle of conductors all reduce what the block can actually carry.

In practice, treat the printed rating as an upper bound for a well-ventilated enclosure and derate from there. Where several blocks sit adjacent and all carry load, the row's capability is set by the hottest block in it, not by the average.

Selection checklist

  • Wire range from the largest conductor on the drawing, allowing for ferrules on fine-stranded cable.
  • Clamp build matched to conductor type: rising cage for flexible, wire protector for solid, stud for large.
  • Body material chosen for the real ambient, with ceramic where thermoplastics are marginal.
  • Format matched to the mounting: barrier for chassis, DIN rail for enclosures, PCB for boards.
  • Busbar or jumper specified with the row, not after it.
  • Torque value taken from the marking and applied with a calibrated driver.
  • Marking and colour agreed if the row has to be identifiable at a glance.

What actually fails in service

Three failure patterns cover most of what comes back. The first is a conductor that was never fully inserted: the clamp closed on insulation, the joint looked correct from outside, and it failed on load. A wire protector clamp makes this easier to miss than a rising cage, which is one reason to prefer the cage where access is poor.

The second is thermal cycling on a joint that was tight enough at installation and not tight enough a year later. The third is a row whose comb busbar was sized for the block count rather than the actual load. None of the three is a manufacturing defect, and all three are prevented at specification stage.

Frequently asked questions

Can a screw terminal block take fine-stranded cable without a ferrule?

It will accept it, but the strands spread under the clamp and the joint becomes unpredictable. A ferrule consolidates the bundle into one solid section, which is what the clamp was designed to grip. Use one wherever the conductor is fine-stranded.

Does a higher current rating mean a better terminal block?

No. The rating is quoted at a reference ambient with a single conductor at full size. A block with a generous clamp opening and a sound cage can outperform a higher-rated block whose opening will not accept the cable you actually have.

Why do terminal blocks loosen over time?

Thermal cycling. Every load cycle expands and contracts the joint, and a joint that was correct cold can relax slightly when hot. Re-checking torque once after the first thermal cycle catches most of it, and periodic inspection catches the rest.

When should I use a barrier block instead of a DIN rail block?

Use a barrier block when different voltages share one row and the walls between circuits are doing real segregation work. Use DIN rail blocks when the row will be built and modified on site, because they clip in without a screw per pole.
