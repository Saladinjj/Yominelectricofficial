Miniature Power Relay Guide: Reading the Nameplate and Specifying a Sealed Relay

ET
ET Engineering Team September 15, 2026 7 min read

A miniature power relay is a small electromechanical switch that lets a low-power control signal operate a higher-power load. It is the standard way a controller — a PLC, a timer, a metering board — switches something it could not drive directly.

Most selection mistakes with relays are not sophisticated. They come from misreading the nameplate. A relay has two completely separate ratings printed on it, and confusing them is how a relay ends up welded shut in the field.

The two ratings on the case

Take the YOMIN miniature power relay, model MPA-S-112-C. Its case carries two contact ratings and one coil rating, and all three matter:

MarkingMeaning
10 A 250 V ACContact rating for an alternating-current load
10 A 30 V DCContact rating for a direct-current load
12 VCoil voltage — what the control circuit must supply

The critical point is that the current rating is not a single number. 10 A at 250 V AC and 10 A at 30 V DC are different jobs. Direct current does not cross zero twice per cycle, so an arc drawn when the contacts open has nothing to extinguish it. That is exactly why the DC rating sits at 30 V while the AC rating reaches 250 V. A relay rated 10 A AC is not automatically a 10 A DC relay.

So the first question when reading any relay nameplate is: what is my load, AC or DC, and at what voltage? Match to the correct figure on the case, not to the larger one.

Choosing the coil voltage

The coil is the control side, and it is electrically separate from the contacts. In the MPA-S-112-C the coil is 12 V, which makes it a natural fit for 12 V control and battery-backed systems.

Practical notes that do not appear on the nameplate:

  • Coil power has to come from somewhere. A relay coil is a standing load whenever it is energised. On a battery-backed panel with many relays, that adds up.
  • Do not drive a coil directly from a logic pin. Use a transistor or driver stage, and fit a flyback diode across the coil to absorb the collapsing field. Without it, the switching transient will eventually damage the driver, not the relay.
  • Hold-in versus pull-in. A coil needs more voltage to pull in than to stay in. Designs that sit near the threshold will drop out intermittently under brown-out conditions.

Why "sealed" matters more than it sounds

The published construction for this relay is sealed, protective. In a control panel that is not marketing language — it is the difference between a relay that works for a decade and one that fails in a year.

An unsealed relay breathes. Every thermal cycle draws air in and pushes it out, and with the air comes dust, moisture and the airborne contamination that exists in every industrial environment. Inside the contact gap, that contamination becomes contact resistance, and contact resistance becomes heat.

A sealed relay is the right choice where:

  • the panel is in a workshop, a plant room, or anywhere with airborne dust;
  • humidity or condensation is possible, including outdoor and containerised installations;
  • the enclosure is washed down or cleaned;
  • the relay will be in service for years between maintenance windows.

Sealing also matters for the board itself, because it allows the relay to be cleaned with the assembly rather than removed first.

Check the approvals, not just the rating

The MPA-S-112-C carries RoHS and is listed under cURus and CQC. Those three tell you different things, and for an export product all of them can matter:

  • RoHS — restricted hazardous substances. Increasingly a hard requirement for anything entering the EU.
  • cURus — recognition under the UL system for the North American market.
  • CQC — the Chinese certification mark.

If your product is exported, the approval set is often what decides the part, not the electrical rating. Confirm the approvals on the specific part number you are ordering — they are not always identical across a product family.

Miniature relays and relay accessories

A miniature power relay is rarely used bare. The usual companion parts are sockets and DIN-rail bases that let the relay be replaced without disturbing the wiring, retaining clips for high-vibration installations, and identification labels for maintenance. YOMIN supplies multi-specification relay accessories alongside the relay range.

Specifying the socket at the same time as the relay is worth doing. It fixes the pin arrangement, the terminal type and the footprint in one decision, and it makes future replacement a plug-in operation rather than a re-termination.
