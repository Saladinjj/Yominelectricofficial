Aluminum Busbar: Grade, Temper and Finish on the Drawing

ET
ET Engineering Team November 9, 2026 9 min read

Aluminum busbar is the right choice when weight and cost matter more than section size. Aluminum conducts about 61% as well as copper by volume, so a bar carrying the same current needs roughly 1.6 times the copper cross-section — and still ends up at about half the weight. The engineering effort is not in choosing the metal. It is in three fields on the drawing: grade and temper, surface finish, and the point where aluminum meets copper.

Why aluminum wins on weight and loses on section

The comparison that decides a project is not conductivity on its own, but conductivity per kilogram. Aluminum conducts about 61% as well as copper by volume and has a density of about 2.70 g/cm³ against copper's 8.96 g/cm³. Multiply those two ratios and you get the practical result every panel designer works with: about 1.6 times the copper cross-section for the same current at the same temperature rise, at about half the weight.

That trade is worth making when the run is long, when the assembly has to be lifted into place, or when the enclosure has a weight limit. It is a poor trade when the bar is short and the terminal area is crowded, because the extra section has to fit somewhere, and the space it needs is usually the space the terminals needed.

Grade and temper decide whether the part can be made

Two bars cut from the same cross-section can behave completely differently on the press brake. A soft temper forms easily and work-hardens as it goes. A harder temper springs back, and in a tight bend it will crack at the outside radius. Neither is wrong — but the drawing has to say which one you want, because a mill will otherwise supply what suits its own rolling schedule rather than your forming operation.

Put four things on the drawing: the grade designation, the temper, the bend radius measured to the inside of the bend, and the grain direction relative to the bend line. A bend across the grain behaves differently from a bend along it, and the difference only shows up when the first article cracks.

One procurement habit is worth adopting: specify temper by designation, never by adjective. Words like “soft” and “half hard” mean different things to different mills, and the argument happens after the parts have been cut.

If the part is formed after plating, say so explicitly. Forming a tinned bar can open the coating at the bend and expose bare aluminum exactly where the material is most highly stressed — which is the worst possible place for an oxide layer to appear.

Surface finish is what decides joint life

Aluminum forms a tenacious oxide within minutes of being cleaned, and that oxide is an insulator. Every finish in a busbar catalogue exists to deal with that one fact.

  • Bare aluminum works inside a dry, sealed enclosure where the joint is made once, with abrasive preparation and a joint compound.
  • Tin plating gives a stable, low-resistance surface that holds its contact resistance through thermal cycling. It is the usual choice when the bar terminates on tinned lugs or tin-plated terminals, because the joint becomes tin-to-tin rather than aluminum-to-copper.
  • Nickel plating is harder and more abrasion-resistant. It suits bars that are handled during assembly, joints that are opened for maintenance, and environments where the tin would wear through.
  • Insulation — epoxy, spray coating or sleeving — is a separate decision from plating, and the two are often combined on the same bar.

The aluminum-to-copper joint is the real design problem

Almost every aluminum busbar in a panel ends at a copper terminal, a copper lug or a copper bar. That transition is where failures concentrate, for two reasons: the metals are galvanically dissimilar, and aluminum creeps under sustained clamp load, so a joint that was tight in the workshop is loose two summers later.

Three measures do most of the work. Plate the aluminum where it will be bolted, so the contact is tin-to-tin or nickel-to-tin rather than aluminum-to-copper. Use a bimetallic transition or a plated lug instead of bolting bare aluminum to bare copper. And specify the torque, then verify it with a torque wrench at commissioning, because a joint tightened by feel is a joint nobody can audit.

Where the connection is outdoors, in a humid room or in a washdown area, treat it as a corrosion detail rather than an electrical one: contact paste on the faces, a sealed boot over the joint, and a drain path so water cannot sit in the lowest point of the connection.

One more habit: re-check torque after the first thermal cycle on any aluminum joint that carries a significant load. Aluminum settles, and the first heat cycle is when it settles most.

Insulated, coated or bare

Insulation is chosen by clearance, not by preference. A coated bar lets phase-to-phase spacing come down inside a tight enclosure. A bare bar relies on air and on the creepage distance that has been designed in. Coating also reduces the consequence of accidental contact during maintenance, which matters more in equipment that is worked on live than in equipment that is always isolated first.

Two cautions. A coating is not a substitute for a barrier where the standard calls for one. And a coating that is damaged at a bend or a bolt hole is worse than no coating at all, because it hides the damage from inspection — so specify whether the coating must survive forming, and inspect the bend radius at goods-in rather than at first power-up.

Flatness and hole position: the tolerances that decide fit

Two busbars can meet the same current rating and still fail to assemble, because the joint faces do not sit flat or the holes do not line up. Flatness matters most at the joint: a bar that bows pulls the connection into line contact instead of face contact, and resistance rises even though every torque value on the sheet was correct.

Give the hole diameter and its position tolerance, the edge distance from hole centre to bar edge, and a flatness callout over the joint zone rather than over the whole length. On a long bar, state whether it must be straight or whether a controlled bow is acceptable — because “straight” without a number is an argument waiting to happen at inspection.

What to send for a quotation

  • Drawing, or bar size plus a dimensioned sketch of bends and holes.
  • Continuous current per bar, and the ambient temperature inside the enclosure.
  • Fault current and duration, if the bar sits inside switchgear.
  • Finish: bare, tinned, nickel-plated, insulated, or a combination.
  • Quantity, and whether the part is formed before or after plating.

With those five, a manufacturer can quote a bar rather than a catalogue number. Frequently asked questions

Is an aluminum busbar as good as copper?

For the same current it needs about 1.6 times the cross-section, because aluminum conducts roughly 61% as well as copper by volume. It then weighs about half as much. So it is better where weight and cost dominate, and worse where space is the binding constraint.

Why tin plate an aluminum busbar?

Plating replaces the insulating oxide with a stable, low-resistance surface and keeps contact resistance predictable under thermal cycling. It also turns the joint into tin-to-copper instead of aluminum-to-copper, which is far less prone to galvanic trouble.

Can aluminum busbar be bolted directly to copper?

It is done, but it is the joint most likely to give trouble later. Plate one side, use a bimetallic transition or a plated lug, and specify the torque rather than tightening by feel.

What temper should I specify?

Specify by designation for the forming you actually do. A soft temper forms easily but work-hardens; a harder temper springs back and can crack at a tight bend. If the part is bent after plating, put that on the order as well.

Sourcing aluminum busbars to a drawing?

Send the drawing, the continuous current, the ambient inside the enclosure and the finish you need. We produce tinned, nickel-plated and insulated aluminum busbars to customer drawings from 100 pieces.
