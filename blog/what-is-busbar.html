What Is Busbar

ET
ET Engineering Team November 17, 2026 10 min read

A busbar is a solid metal bar that carries current through a panel, a switchboard or a battery pack in place of a cable. It is normally copper or aluminium, usually rectangular in section, and it is bolted or clamped rather than crimped. The reason it exists is simple: above roughly a hundred amps, a bar carries the same current in less space, with less voltage drop, with better heat rejection and with a joint that can be inspected and re-torqued.

That definition drives everything else on this page. Because a busbar is a structural part as much as an electrical one, its size is set by three separate limits — current density, temperature rise and short-circuit withstand — and the largest of the three wins. A bar sized only on amps is the most common specification error we see.

Why panels use bars instead of cable

Cable is a conductor wrapped in insulation; a busbar is a conductor with air and standoff insulation around it. Once current rises, that difference compounds in four ways.

  • Space. A 1000 A cable bundle is thick, stiff and needs bending radius. The equivalent bar is a flat section that turns in one plane and needs a fraction of the volume.
  • Heat. A bar has a high surface-area-to-volume ratio, so it rejects heat to the enclosure air instead of trapping it inside insulation.
  • Voltage drop. A solid bar has no stranded-conductor contact resistance and a shorter run, so drop across the same length is lower.
  • Serviceability. A bolted joint can be opened, inspected and re-torqued. A crimped lug is a permanent decision.

The four forms you will be quoted

Busbar is not one product. The forms differ in how the conductor moves, and the choice follows the mechanical duty rather than the current.

How the size is actually set

Three limits apply, and each can govern a different design. Check all three before you accept a section.

LimitWhat it protectsWhat fixes the size
Current densityContinuous temperature riseCross-section against the panel ambient and the enclosure's ability to shed heat
Temperature riseInsulation and touch safetySurface area, mounting orientation, whether the bar is painted or bare
Short-circuit withstandMechanical survival of the bar and its supportsPeak fault current and the duration it lasts; supports must resist the magnetic force

The third limit is the one buyers forget. A bar that carries its continuous current comfortably can still be destroyed by a fault, because the force between parallel bars during a short circuit is enormous and the temperature spike is adiabatic. That is why a bar is often specified two sizes above its continuous duty, and why the support spacing matters as much as the section.

For the arithmetic behind these three limits, see the current-carrying capacity guide, which works through cross-section, derating and fault duration.

Copper or aluminium

The choice is a trade between conductivity, weight and the joint, and the joint usually decides.

CopperAluminium
ConductivityHigher, so a smaller section carries the same currentLower, so roughly one size up for the same duty
WeightHeavy; matters in battery packs and on floating structuresAbout a third of copper for the same volume
JointTolerant, well understood, easy to boltOxidises, creeps under torque, needs bimetallic transition to copper
CostHigher and volatileLower and more stable

A common compromise is aluminium for the long run and copper for the terminal interface, joined through a bimetallic plate. Without that transition, the aluminium-to-copper joint is where the failure happens two years later.

Specification checklist

  • Continuous current at the real panel ambient, not the nominal rating.
  • Peak fault current and duration, so the bar and its supports can be checked.
  • Form — rigid for fixed distribution, flexible wherever vibration or thermal expansion exists.
  • Material and finish, including whether the joint is copper-to-copper or needs a bimetallic plate.
  • Drilling and centres, because a bar is made to a drawing and arrives wrong if the drawing is wrong.
  • Insulation and standoff, including the rated voltage of any support insulator.

Send the fault level with the current. A quotation based on amps alone cannot confirm that the bar survives a fault.

Frequently asked questions

What is busbar in simple terms?

A busbar is a metal bar that carries current where a cable would otherwise be used. It is bolted rather than crimped, takes less space at high current, and is used in panels, switchboards and battery packs.

Is busbar copper or aluminium?

Both are standard. Copper conducts better and takes a smaller section; aluminium is about a third of the weight and cheaper, but it needs a bimetallic transition where it meets copper or the joint corrodes.

How is busbar size calculated?

Three limits apply together: continuous current density against ambient temperature, permissible temperature rise given the surface area and mounting, and short-circuit withstand for the peak fault current and its duration. Whichever demands the largest section governs.

When should busbar be flexible instead of rigid?

Wherever the connection moves. Battery packs expand and contract with state of charge, EV modules vibrate, and long runs grow with temperature. A rigid bar in those positions transfers the movement into the joint and eventually cracks it.

Specifying busbar for a panel or a battery pack?

Send the continuous current, the peak fault current, the form you need and the joint drawing. We will confirm the section, the material and the drilling before anything is cut.
