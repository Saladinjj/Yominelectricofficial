What Is Screw Machine: Screw Tightening and Metal Processing

ET
ET Engineering Team November 22, 2026 9 min read

A screw machine is an automatic production machine built around a screw: either a machine that cuts a screw thread or turns a small part from bar stock, or a machine that drives screws into an assembly at a controlled torque. Both meanings are current in manufacturing, and the one that applies to electrical busbar and terminal production is the second. In that setting a screw machine is the station that drills, taps and tightens the fasteners holding a busbar row or a terminal together, and an integrated screw machine line performs those steps plus the finishing work in a single sequence.

The distinction matters because the two machine types are sold to different buyers, priced on different logic and cannot be substituted for one another. Ordering the wrong one is an expensive way to learn the difference.

Two machines share the name

The older use of the term comes from screw machining: an automatic lathe feeds bar stock through a guide bushing and performs turning, drilling, threading and cut-off in one cycle, producing small cylindrical parts at high volume. The modern use in electrical assembly describes something closer to a special-purpose assembly line, where the screw is the fastening method rather than the product.

AspectScrew machining latheScrew tightening and processing line
What it producesSmall turned parts: screws, pins, spacers, connectorsAssembled busbar rows and terminals with fasteners set
Material removalContinuous, by cutting toolDrilling and tapping at defined positions on a formed part
InputBar stock on a coil or magazinePre-cut brass or aluminium busbar rows, terminals
Output logicParts per hour of a single geometryCompleted assemblies per drawing or sample
Typical buyerComponent manufacturerSwitchboard, meter and terminal block manufacturer

If the requirement is a finished assembly with holes in the right places and screws at the right torque, the lathe is irrelevant regardless of how the phrase is searched. What is needed is the processing line, and that is the machine covered by the rest of this article.

Where the electrical industry uses the term

Busbar rows and terminal assemblies arrive at the screw station already cut and formed. Three operations then happen in sequence, and each of them carries a quality consequence that shows up later in the field rather than at the machine.

  • Drilling and tapping. A hole in the wrong position, or a thread cut shallow, produces a joint that cannot be tightened to specification no matter what torque is applied afterwards.
  • Screw tightening. The fastener is what maintains contact pressure across the joint for twenty years. Uneven torque across a row means uneven current sharing between parallel paths.
  • Rotary finishing. Deburring and surface work remove the raised metal that would otherwise hold two faces apart and reduce the real contact area.

A joint that is mechanically tight but electrically poor behaves in a very specific way: it runs slightly warm at commissioning, warmer under load, and eventually hot enough to discolour the surrounding insulation. That progression starts at the screw station, which is why the station is specified rather than improvised.

Inside an integrated line: three stages

YOMIN builds its metal deep processing machine as an integrated line with three production stages in sequence: deep processing, screw tightening and rotary finishing. Each station carries its own touch screen and PLC control, and the line is built to work brass and aluminium busbar rows and terminals rather than a single fixed part.

StageWhat happensWhy it is a separate station
Deep processingDrilling, tapping and compound machining of the busbar row or terminalTool wear changes hole quality, so the stage needs its own control and its own inspection point
Screw tighteningFasteners driven to a set condition, station by stationTorque consistency is the variable that decides joint resistance, and it cannot be corrected downstream
Rotary finishingRotary metalworking and surface finishing of the completed partRemoves burrs and raised edges left by the earlier stages

The reason for splitting the work into three controlled stations rather than one continuous pass is diagnosability. When a batch fails a pull test or runs hot, a line with per-station control tells you which stage produced the defect. A single-pass machine tells you only that the batch is bad.

Manual, semi-automatic or integrated

Most workshops move through three stages of automation, and each stage solves a different problem. The choice is not about being modern; it is about which failure mode is currently costing the most money.

ApproachWhat it is good atWhere it breaks downChoose it when
Hand tools and drill pressOne-off prototypes, repair work, very low volumeOperator-dependent hole position and torqueVolume is measured in single pieces
Semi-automatic stationsEstablished product families with modest variationHandling between stations; quality depends on the hand-offSeveral hundred identical assemblies per batch
Integrated lineRepeatable position, torque and finish across a full batchChangeover time on a new geometry; needs drawings or samples up frontVolume justifies tooling and the customer audits the joint

The step that usually forces the decision is not throughput but auditability. Once a customer asks for evidence that every joint in a shipment was tightened to the same condition, hand assembly stops being defensible regardless of how fast the operators are.

Torque consistency is a quality variable, not a setting

On a busbar joint, the screw is a spring. It stores the clamp force that keeps two metal faces in contact, and that force relaxes over time as the joint beds in and as thermal cycling works on it. Two consequences follow.

The first is that under-tightening and over-tightening fail differently. Under-tightening leaves a high-resistance joint that heats under load. Over-tightening strips the thread or yields the busbar around the hole, which produces a joint that was correct at assembly and loose six months later. Neither is visible at dispatch.

The second is that consistency matters more than the absolute figure. A row of ten joints where nine are at one torque and one is at half of it shares current unevenly, and the odd joint carries the thermal penalty for the whole row. A line that repeats the same condition on every fastener removes that variable, which is the entire argument for automating the station.

What to send for a quotation

These machines are built against a part, not against a catalogue number, so the specification is the drawing set. YOMIN customises the line per drawings or samples, and the faster those arrive the faster the configuration can be confirmed.

  • Drawings or physical samples of each busbar row or terminal to be processed, including hole positions and thread sizes.
  • Material and thickness — brass and aluminium behave differently under drilling and tapping, and tooling follows the material.
  • Fastener specification: thread, head type and the torque condition the joint is designed for.
  • Station sequence required, if the finishing step differs from the standard rotary arrangement.
  • Batch pattern — how many variants share the line, because changeover drives the real cost per part.

Send the drawings through the contact form or on WhatsApp and the configuration discussion starts from the actual geometry rather than from a generic machine size.

Where the line sits in production

An integrated screw machine is one link in a chain, and it performs best when the links either side are stable. Upstream, the busbar must be cut and formed to a repeatable dimension, which is the job covered in the busbar bending machine guide. Downstream, the completed assembly is inspected, packed and installed, and the joint behaviour that matters at installation is described in what is busbar.

YOMIN builds three machines in this family, each aimed at a different part of the sequence: the deep processing machine for brass and aluminium rows, the automatic screw tightening machine, and the circulating rotary metalworking machine. All three are listed under screw machines.

Selection checklist

  • Part family rather than a single part: a line is justified by the variants it can absorb.
  • Control architecture — per-station PLC and touch screen, so a defect can be traced to a stage.
  • Material handling, because brass rows and aluminium terminals do not present the same way to a fixture.
  • Changeover, measured in the time it takes to switch a variant, not in the speed of the fastest cycle.
  • Inspection points at each stage, since a defect found after finishing is a scrapped part rather than a reworkable one.

Machines in this class are specified once and used for years, so the cost of a wrong configuration is not the purchase price but the months spent working around it.

How joint quality is verified after assembly

Three checks catch almost every failure the screw station can introduce, and none of them needs destructive testing. A visual pass finds missing fasteners and raised burrs. A torque re-check on a sample confirms the station still delivers the condition it was set to. A millivolt drop measurement across the joint measures what actually matters electrically, because resistance at the interface is where a shallow thread or a trapped burr shows up long before the joint runs hot.

Thermal imaging closes the loop on a finished assembly under load. A joint running warmer than its neighbours on the same row is evidence that clamp force is not uniform, and the cause sits upstream of the panel rather than in service.

Frequently asked questions

What is a screw machine used for?

In electrical manufacturing it drives the fasteners that hold busbar rows and terminals together, after drilling and tapping the holes they pass through. In metalworking the same phrase describes an automatic lathe that turns small parts from bar stock, which is a different machine sold to a different buyer.

What are the three stages of an integrated screw machine line?

Deep processing, screw tightening and rotary finishing. YOMIN builds the line with a touch screen and PLC at each station, working brass and aluminium busbar rows and terminals, so a defect can be traced to the stage that produced it.

Why does torque consistency matter more than the torque figure?

Because the screw is a spring that holds the joint closed for years. Under-tightening leaves a high-resistance joint that heats under load, over-tightening strips the thread or yields the metal around the hole, and uneven torque across a row shares current unequally between parallel paths.

What do I need to send for a quotation?

Drawings or physical samples of each busbar row or terminal, the material and thickness, the fastener specification including thread and head type, and the number of variants that will share the line. These machines are built against a part, so the drawing set is the specification.

Specifying a screw machine for busbar or terminal production?

Send the drawings or samples, the material and thickness, and the fastener specification. We build integrated metal deep processing lines for brass and aluminium busbar rows and terminals, with per-station PLC control across deep processing, screw tightening and rotary finishing.
