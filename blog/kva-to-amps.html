kVA to Amps: The Conversion Formula, Worked Charts and CT Selection

ET
ET Engineering Team November 3, 2026 11 min read

To convert kVA to amps, divide the apparent power in kilovolt-amperes by the voltage, and by the square root of three as well on a three-phase supply. Single phase: amps = kVA × 1000 ÷ volts. Three phase: amps = kVA × 1000 ÷ (1.732 × volts). At 400 V three-phase, one kVA is 1.44 A, so 100 kVA is 144 A. At 230 V single phase the same 100 kVA is 435 A — three times the current for the same apparent power, which is why the two formulas must never be mixed.

Where the square root of three comes from

On a three-phase supply the three line currents are separated by 120 electrical degrees, and their vector sum is 1.732 times the current in any one line. The formula is not a rule of thumb to be memorised; it is the geometric consequence of sharing the same apparent power across three conductors.

That is also why a three-phase circuit needs only one third of the copper of a single-phase circuit for the same kVA. The current per conductor falls by 1.732 and the number of conductors rises from two to four, so the total conductor mass still drops.

The practical consequence for a buyer is the opposite of a saving: if you apply the single-phase formula to a three-phase nameplate, the current you calculate is 1.732 times too high, and you will oversize the meter, the current transformer and the cable. Apply it the other way round — a three-phase formula on a single-phase circuit — and the calculated current is 1.732 times too low, which is the expensive direction, because the metering then runs into overload and the CT saturates.

Note how little the 400 V and 415 V columns differ. The 3.75% gap in voltage produces the same 3.75% gap in current, so the choice between them rarely changes the equipment selection. What does change it is the difference between single and three phase, which is a factor of 1.73 and moves the answer across whole product families.

From amps to the meter: the 80 A ceiling

Direct-connected meters are limited by the current that can pass through their terminal block. YOMIN's ym-0058, a three-phase four-wire DIN rail meter with a 3×5(80 A) rating, WiFi communication and a digital display, carries the load current through the meter itself. At 400 V three-phase that 80 A ceiling corresponds to 1.732 × 400 × 80 ÷ 1000, or about 55 kVA.

Above roughly 55 kVA the circuit moves to a current transformer, and the meter becomes a 5 A device reading a scaled signal. This is the point where a kVA-to-amps calculation stops being an academic exercise: the answer decides whether the installation needs a direct meter or a CT-operated one, and the two are not interchangeable after the panel is built.

The full wiring difference between those two arrangements is set out in the energy meter wiring diagram guide.

kVA is not kW, and the difference changes the current

Everything above converts apparent power. A load's real power in kW is the kVA multiplied by the power factor, so a 100 kVA transformer feeding a plant at 0.8 power factor delivers 80 kW. The current, however, is set by the kVA, not the kW — the cable and the meter carry the apparent current whether or not the load converts all of it into useful work.

This catches out buyers who start from a motor list in kW. A 90 kW motor at 0.85 power factor and 0.9 efficiency draws about 106 kVA, not 90, and the feeder must be sized on the larger figure. The kVA versus kW comparison works through that correction.

Frequently asked questions

How many amps is 100 kVA?

It depends on the voltage and the phase count. At 400 V three-phase, 100 kVA is 144.3 A. At 415 V three-phase it is 139.1 A. At 230 V single phase it is 434.8 A.

Should I use 400 V or 415 V in the formula?

Use the nominal voltage your supply actually declares. The difference between the two is 3.75% in both voltage and current, which rarely changes a product selection but does change the displayed current on a report.

Why does my calculated current not match the meter reading?

Three usual causes: the load is not balanced across the phases, so one line carries more than the average; the meter is CT-operated and the reading is the secondary current; or the calculation used kW where the nameplate gave kVA.

Can I use the single-phase formula on a three-phase supply?

No. It omits the square root of three, so it overstates the current by 73%, which leads to an oversized meter, an oversized CT and an oversized cable.

Sizing a meter or a current transformer?

Send the system voltage, the phase count and either the kVA or the maximum demand in amps. We will confirm whether the circuit needs a direct-connected meter or a CT-operated one, and quote the matching meter and transformer from the production range.
