What Is Multi Tariff Energy Meter: Time-of-Use Registers and Billing

ET
ET Engineering Team November 25, 2026 8 min read

A multi tariff energy meter is an electricity meter that keeps separate energy registers for separate time-of-use windows — usually peak, off-peak and night — and moves between them on its own clock and calendar rather than on a command from the network. Consumption recorded inside the peak window is billed at the peak rate, consumption recorded at night is billed at the night rate, and the bill is assembled afterwards from the registers instead of from a single running total.

That single design decision is what separates a multi-tariff meter from a plain kWh meter, and it is also where most specification mistakes happen. A meter that switches windows correctly bills correctly even when the communication link is down for a month; a meter whose clock drifts, or whose calendar was never loaded, keeps perfect energy totals and still sends every kilowatt-hour to the wrong register.

What the meter actually counts

Energy is not accumulated into one number that is later split up. Each register is an independent counter with its own identifier, its own unit and its own billing meaning. The display scrolls through them, and the meter’s job is to decide, at every instant, which counter an incoming pulse belongs to.

This matters commercially because the registers are read separately. A discrepancy between the total register and the sum of the time-of-use registers is one of the first things a utility checks when a customer disputes a bill, and it is the reason the total register is never replaced by the window registers.

How a tariff calendar becomes a register

The meter carries a real-time clock and a calendar stored in non-volatile memory. The calendar is not a list of prices — the meter does not know what a kilowatt-hour costs — it is a map from date and time to register. A typical residential map has a weekday pattern, a weekend pattern, a summer and a winter season, and a small list of exception days for public holidays.

At each boundary the meter closes one register and opens the next. Nothing else happens: no data is transmitted, no server is consulted, and the energy already accumulated stays where it is. That is exactly why the calendar has to be loaded and verified at commissioning, and why a meter installed with the factory default map will keep working flawlessly while billing the wrong windows.

Multi tariff, multi rate and prepaid are three different things

These three terms are used interchangeably in sales conversations and they describe three independent properties of a meter. Multi-tariff is about when energy is priced differently. Multi-rate is about how many separate counters exist. Prepaid is about how the customer pays. A meter can hold all three, any two, or none.

Confusing them produces orders for the wrong device. A buyer who needs night charging to be cheaper does not need a prepaid meter; a buyer who needs to stop supply at zero credit does not need a multi-tariff meter, although the two are frequently combined because both are driven by the same clock and the same communications port.

ConfigurationWhat changes for the customerWhat does not change
Single-rate postpaidNothing — one price, one registerMetering accuracy and installation

Maximum demandPart of the bill depends on peak power, not energyEnergy registers continue unchanged

The clock is the weakest link

Every multi-tariff design inherits one vulnerability: the register assignment is only as correct as the clock. A crystal that drifts by a few minutes a month is harmless in a single-rate meter and expensive in a multi-tariff one, because the error accumulates at every boundary and never corrects itself.

Three details deserve attention in a specification. The clock should be backed by a battery or a supercapacitor so that it keeps time through an outage. The drift should be stated in the datasheet, not assumed. And a change to the clock or the calendar should be visible as an event in the meter log, because it is the one configuration change that can move money between registers without moving a single kilowatt-hour.

Reading the meter without a communication link

A multi-tariff meter is fully functional as a standalone device. The registers can be read from the display, from an optical probe, from a serial bus, or as pulses, and each path gives a different amount of detail.

Reading pathWhat it gives youWhen it is enough
Display scrollRegister identifier, window energy, total energyCommissioning and site visits
Optical infrared probeAll registers and configuration, no wiringUtility handheld reading
RS485 serial busAll registers and events over Modbus or a similar protocolBuilding-level automatic meter reading
Pulse outputEnergy pulses only, no register identityThird-party logging where the meter is the reference

Where multi-tariff installations fail an audit

Three failures account for most disputed bills, and none of them is a metering fault. The first is a calendar left at the factory default, which produces perfectly accurate totals and a completely wrong split between windows. The second is a clock set from a phone rather than a reference, which shifts every boundary after commissioning. The third is a demand interval that does not match the billing rule, so the recorded maximum is higher than the one the tariff expects.

All three are checkable on the day of installation, and all three are invisible afterwards unless someone compares the total register against the sum of the tariff registers. That comparison is worth writing into the commissioning sheet: if the two agree to within rounding, the register allocation is at least self-consistent, and if they drift apart the meter is telling you that a window boundary or a clock has moved.

What to specify before you order

  • Phase and connection. Single-phase direct-connected, three-phase four-wire, or transformer-operated through current transformers. The three are not interchangeable and the wiring diagram differs.
  • Number of tariff windows. Two is enough for a simple peak and off-peak split; three or four is normal where a night window and a weekend pattern exist.
  • Calendar capability. Weekday and weekend patterns, seasons, and exception days. A meter with only a daily timer cannot express a real residential tariff.
  • Register set. Total, each time-of-use register, maximum demand, and export if the site generates.
  • Clock backup and drift. Battery or supercapacitor backed, with a stated accuracy.
  • Communication. RS485 is the default for building installations; optical infrared should be present for utility reading even when a bus is fitted.
  • Sealing and tamper evidence. Terminal cover seal, and a clock-change or cover-open event in the log.

Frequently asked questions

What is a multi tariff energy meter?

It is an electricity meter that records energy into separate registers for separate time-of-use windows, switching between them on its own clock and calendar, so that each window can be billed at a different rate.

Does a multi-tariff meter need a network connection to switch tariffs?

No. The switching is done locally from the meter’s own clock and stored calendar. Communication is only needed to read the registers remotely or to change the calendar afterwards.

What happens to the registers during a power cut?

The accumulated energy stays in non-volatile memory and remains readable. What matters is the clock: with a battery or supercapacitor backup it keeps time through the outage, and without one it restarts and can assign the following hours to the wrong window.

Can a multi-tariff meter be prepaid?

Yes, and the two are often combined. The tariff registers decide how consumption is priced, while the prepayment function decides whether supply continues; they share the same clock and the same display.

How many tariff windows can a meter hold?

The limit is a configuration property of the model, not a universal number, so it belongs on the datasheet you are ordering against. Two windows cover a simple peak and off-peak split, and four is a common residential pattern.

What is the difference between multi-rate and multi-tariff?

Multi-rate describes the number of separate energy counters in the meter; multi-tariff describes prices being attached to time windows. In practice one device provides both, which is why the terms are used interchangeably.
