Three-Phase Voltage Stabilizer on an Unbalanced Load: Neutral Shift and Per-Phase Sizing

ET
ET Engineering Team November 28, 2026 11 min read

Most three-phase voltage stabilizers are bought on a single number: total kVA. On a balanced load that is a reasonable shortcut. On the load most industrial sites actually have — a four-wire system where single-phase loads are spread unevenly across the three phases — it is the wrong number, and it produces the specific failure where two phases sit comfortably in band while the third one sags under the equipment that matters.

The direct answer is this: on an unbalanced load, what decides whether a stabilizer works is not its total capacity but whether it regulates each phase independently against neutral. A unit that senses and corrects a single average, or that corrects only one reference phase, cannot hold three different phase-to-neutral voltages at once. Sizing then has to be done per phase, on the worst-case imbalance, not on the sum.

What an unbalanced load actually does

In a three-phase four-wire system the neutral carries the difference between the phase currents. With a perfectly balanced load that difference is zero and the neutral point sits exactly at the centre of the voltage triangle. As soon as the load is unbalanced, current flows in the neutral, and the voltage drop along the neutral conductor — plus any impedance in the neutral path — displaces that centre point.

The consequence is easy to state and easy to underestimate: the heavily loaded phase sees a lower phase-to-neutral voltage, and the lightly loaded phases see a higher one. Nothing about the phase-to-phase voltages looks obviously wrong, which is why the problem is often missed during commissioning.

  • Undervoltage on the loaded phase causes contactors to chatter, motors to draw more current for the same output, and switch-mode supplies to drop out.
  • Overvoltage on the light phases is the more expensive half. It stresses insulation, shortens capacitor and power-supply life, and trips overvoltage protection on equipment that has no fault of its own.
  • Neutral current itself becomes a design item: harmonics from electronic loads add in the neutral rather than cancelling, so a neutral sized only for fundamental balance can be overloaded.

A stabilizer that regulates only the average of the three phases makes this worse in a subtle way. It will happily hold the average in band while the loaded phase stays low and the light phases go higher — the regulation looks correct on the display and the equipment still trips.

Which loads actually see the neutral shift

Not every load cares, and knowing which ones do is what turns a vague concern into a specification.

LoadResponds toSensitivity to neutral shiftWhat the stabilizer must do
Three-phase motorPositive-sequence voltageLow — it is largely indifferent to phase-to-neutral spreadHold phase-to-phase voltage; per-phase correction is not the deciding factor
Single-phase electronics, PLC, instrumentationPhase-to-neutral voltageHigh — both sag and rise are damagingRegulate each phase independently against neutral
Lighting and heatingPhase-to-neutral voltageModerate — output changes with voltage, life changes with overvoltageHold each phase in band to keep output and life predictable
Capacitor banks and filtersPhase-to-neutral voltageHigh — reactive output rises with the square of voltagePrevent the overvoltage phase from being pushed higher
Mixed panel, unknown splitBothUnknown until measuredMeasure the actual per-phase load before choosing a topology

The practical rule that follows: if the panel is dominated by three-phase motors, a three-phase stabilizer regulating phase-to-phase voltage is adequate. If the panel feeds single-phase electronics, controls or lighting from a four-wire supply, per-phase regulation against neutral is not a luxury.

The three regulation topologies and how they handle imbalance

All three families are sold as "three-phase AVR", and they behave very differently once the load stops being symmetrical.

TopologyHow it correctsUnbalanced-load behaviourWhere it fits
Relay type Switches transformer taps in steps as voltage crosses thresholds Corrects in coarse steps; three independent channels are possible but the steps are wide Appliances and small single-phase or light three-phase loads where a few volts of error is acceptable
Servo type (motor-driven variac) Motor drives a variable transformer continuously to the required ratio Genuinely per-phase when built as three independent servo chains referenced to neutral Mixed four-wire panels with significant single-phase load; the usual choice for industrial distribution
Static type (thyristor or IGBT with tapped transformer) Electronic switching selects or synthesises the correction, with no moving parts Fast per-phase correction, well suited to rapidly changing or highly unbalanced load Sensitive processes, sites where correction speed matters more than purchase price

The distinction that matters commercially is inside the servo family, not between families. A three-phase servo stabilizer can be built as three independent single-phase regulators sharing a frame, or as one three-phase regulator with a single error signal. Both are described as "three-phase servo stabilizer" in catalogues, and only the first one solves an unbalanced load. Ask which one you are being quoted, and ask what the unit references each phase to.

One three-phase unit or three single-phase units?

This is the decision that most often separates a working installation from an expensive one, and it is a genuine trade-off rather than a rule.

ApproachAdvantagesDisadvantages
Three independent single-phase units Each phase regulated on its own error against neutral; a fault takes out one phase only; each unit sized to its own phase load Three enclosures, three maintenance items, more panel space and more wiring
One three-phase unit with per-phase correction Single enclosure, one commissioning step, shared control and protection, per-phase regulation retained Higher unit cost than a single-error-signal design; repair affects all three phases
One three-phase unit with a single error signal Lowest cost, smallest footprint, adequate for balanced three-phase motor load Cannot hold three different phase-to-neutral voltages; the failure mode this article is about

For a panel with a handful of three-phase motors, the third row is often correct and honest. For a building distribution board feeding single-phase circuits, it usually is not, however attractive the price looks.

Sizing: use the phase, not the total

Sizing on total kVA assumes the load divides equally. On an unbalanced panel it does not, and a unit that is nominally large enough will saturate its correction range on the worst-loaded phase. A defensible sequence looks like this:

  1. Measure the actual per-phase load at representative times, not from a nameplate schedule. Load diversity across a day is often more unbalanced than the design intent.
  2. Size the correction for the worst-loaded phase, then check that the same capacity is available to the other phases. Per-phase capacity is what limits correction, not the total.
  3. Take the lowest sustained input voltage from a week of logging, not from a single reading. The correction range has to cover the brownout that actually happens, and a unit that runs out of range behaves like no stabilizer at all — it simply passes the sag through.
  4. Add the starting load of motors and compressors, which is far above running current for a short period, and decide whether the stabilizer must ride through it or whether the start is staged.
  5. Apply an ambient derating. A stabilizer in a hot panel room is not the same product as the same unit on a test bench, and the correction range shrinks before the capacity does.

Then check the neutral. The neutral conductor, the neutral terminal and any neutral link must be rated for the highest neutral current the panel can produce, including harmonic content, because that is the path the imbalance returns on.

Installation points that decide whether it works

  • Never switch or fuse the neutral in a four-wire system feeding single-phase loads. An open neutral turns a phase-to-neutral load into a phase-to-phase load and destroys it.
  • Reference each phase to neutral, not to the other phases, if per-phase regulation is the requirement.
  • Provide a maintenance bypass so the unit can be serviced without dropping the load. It also allows a before-and-after measurement, which is the only honest way to prove the stabilizer is doing anything.
  • Check phase rotation on three-phase servo units before energising the load.
  • Ventilate according to the actual ambient, and keep the unit's intake clear; servo types dissipate real heat in the variac and the motor drive.

Frequently asked questions

Why does one phase stay low when my three-phase stabilizer is working?

Because the unit is most likely correcting a single error signal or an average of the three phases. On an unbalanced load that average can sit in band while the loaded phase remains low and the light phases rise. Per-phase regulation against neutral is what fixes it.

Can I size a three-phase stabilizer on total kVA?

Only if the load is genuinely balanced. On a four-wire panel with single-phase loads, size each phase for its own worst-case load, because the correction range is consumed by the heaviest phase first.

Is a three-phase stabilizer needed if the loads are mostly motors?

Usually a phase-to-phase regulating unit is adequate, because motors respond to positive-sequence voltage and tolerate phase-to-neutral spread. Add per-phase regulation when the same panel also feeds single-phase electronics, controls or lighting.

What happens if the neutral opens on a four-wire system?

Phase-to-neutral voltages are no longer held by the neutral point and can rise far above nominal on lightly loaded phases, damaging connected equipment. That is why the neutral is never switched or fused in such a system and why its current rating matters.
