Duct Rodder Guide: Fibreglass Rods for Underground Duct and Draw-Pit Pulling

ET
ET Engineering Team September 18, 2026 8 min read

Underground ducts fail silently

An installed buried duct looks identical whether it is clear or blocked. Ducting collapses, couplings separate, and silt and mortar find their way in during the build. The first indication is usually a cable that will not pull — at which point excavation is the only remaining option. A duct rodder exists to move that discovery forward, to the day the duct is handed over rather than the day the cable is installed.

What a duct rodder is

A duct rodder is a rigid, non-conductive rod that is pushed through a buried duct from one access point to the next. Where a fish tape works by coiling and steering through accessible conduit, a rod works by being stiff enough to be pushed several tens of metres along a straight buried route and by being visible and controllable at both ends. Rods are usually supplied as coupled sections so the working length can be built up on site.

Rod diameter and stiffness

Diameter is the compromise between pushability and the ability to round a bend.

Rod diameterBehaviourSuitable duct
4 mmMost flexible, least pushSmall ducts and tight radius bends
5 mmGeneral purposeStandard duct runs between draw pits
6 mmStiffest, best push on long runsLonger straight runs and larger ducts

The published YM-CP002 rod covers 4 / 5 / 6 mm diameters with 1 m sections and other lengths to order, which is the arrangement that lets a crew start flexible at a bend and stiffen up for the long straight that follows.

Why fibreglass, and why it must be non-conductive

Ducts are not always empty and their contents are not always documented. On street-lighting circuits, in mixed-service trenches and on any route where a live cable may share the duct, a conductive rod introduces a fault path that did not exist before. The YM-CP002 rod is non-conductive, with a hook, an eye and a flexible extension among its published end fittings — so the duct can be proved without putting a person or a circuit at risk.

Proving a duct properly

Rodding a duct is a sequence, not a single action:

  1. Rod the route. Push through from the far end where possible, so a blockage is found early rather than mid-way.
  2. Pull a draw cord. Once through, the rod carries a cord back — the cord, not the rod, is what the cable pull uses.
  3. Prove the bore. Pull a mandrel or swab of a diameter matched to the duct to confirm the full bore is clear, not just the top of the duct.
  4. Record the result. Note the duct reference, the length rodded and the condition found, so the next crew does not repeat the work.

The diameter of the proving tool should track the duct it is proving: a 110 mm duct needs a substantially larger swab than a 50 mm one to be meaningful.

End fittings

  • Hook — for reaching into a pit, catching a draw cord or retrieving an existing cable end.
  • Eye — for carrying the draw cord straight through the rod assembly.
  • Flexible extension — for the last awkward metre where a stiff rod cannot follow the duct into the pit.

Handling and life

Fibreglass rod is strong in compression and weak against a sharp bend. Two habits double its service life: never force a rod through a radius it is fighting, and never leave a rod coiled tighter than it was supplied. The brass ferrules at the joints should be kept clean and dry — grit in a coupling is what eventually spreads the joint and lets water into the rod.

Frequently asked questions

How far can a duct rodder reach?

It depends on duct diameter, straightness and how many bends the route contains. Rods are built up in sections precisely so the working length can be matched to the run rather than fixed in advance.

Is a duct rodder the same as a fish tape?

No. A fish tape is a coiled tape for short, accessible conduit work. A duct rodder is a rigid non-conductive rod for long buried ducts between draw pits.

Can I pull the cable with the rod itself?

The normal practice is to rod a draw cord through first and pull on the cord. Pulling directly on a coupling loads a joint that was not designed for it.

What about very long runs?

For long routes, an electric cable puller takes over once the cord is through. The rod's job is to establish the route; the machine does the heavy pulling.

Need duct rodders for an underground route?

Give us the duct diameter, the pit-to-pit distances and whether any duct may be live. YOMIN supplies non-conductive fibreglass rods with hook, eye and flexible extension ends.
