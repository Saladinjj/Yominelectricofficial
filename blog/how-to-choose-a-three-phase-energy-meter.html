How to Choose a Three-Phase Energy Meter: Selection Guide

ET
ET Engineering Team 9 min read August 28, 2026

Choosing a three-phase energy meter comes down to six decisions: phase configuration (3P4W vs 3P3W), connection mode (direct vs CT), accuracy class, tariff support, communication, and certification. Get those six right and the meter will serve your project for a decade; get them wrong and you will pay twice.

This guide walks through each decision in the order an engineer should make it, with the numbers that matter. It is written for electrical contractors, panel builders, EPC firms, utilities and wholesalers specifying meters for commercial and industrial sites.

Step 1: Know Your System — 3P4W or 3P3W

The first thing to establish is how your supply is configured:

  • 3P4W (three-phase, four-wire) — three phases plus neutral, common in 230/400 V and 127/220 V systems. Most commercial and industrial buildings use this.
  • 3P3W (three-phase, three-wire) — no neutral, used in some delta-connected industrial systems and on CT metering of large consumers.

Almost every DIN-rail three-phase meter on the market handles 3P4W with 3P3W selectable. Still, confirm the nominal voltage range matches your network — for example 3×230/400 V or 3×127/220 V — before ordering. If you need the fundamentals first, our three-phase meters explained article covers how these meters work.

Step 2: Direct Connection or CT-Operated?

This is the highest-impact decision because it determines the meter's current rating and whether you need current transformers:

Load currentConnectionMeter rating
Up to ~60 ADirect5(60) A, 5(80) A
~60–100 ADirect (heavy duty)10(100) A
Above 100 ACT-operated1.5(6) A or 5(6) A secondary with CTs

For a main incomer on a commercial building, CT-operated is the norm. For individual panel feeders up to 100 A, direct-connected DIN-rail meters are the most economical. For the full rules on pairing meters with CTs, see our power meter with CT guide.

Step 3: Accuracy Class

Accuracy class defines the maximum permitted error, per IEC 62053-21 (active energy):

ClassMax errorUse it for
Class 2.0±2%Basic residential-level monitoring
Class 1.0±1%Standard commercial/industrial metering, most billing
Class 0.5S±0.5%Cost allocation between tenants/profit centres, high-value billing

For commercial projects, Class 1.0 is the sensible default. Step up to 0.5S only where you allocate costs between parties.

Step 4: Tariffs and Demand

Utilities increasingly bill commercial and industrial customers on time-of-use (TOU) rates. If your customer pays different rates per time band, the meter must store energy per tariff and record maximum demand:

  • Number of tariffs — look for 4–6 programmable tariff schedules with seasonal and weekday/weekend settings.
  • Maximum demand — demand charges are often 30–50% of the bill; a meter that logs peak demand (with date/time stamp) is essential for verifying the utility's demand charge.

Our time-of-use metering guide explains how TOU tariffs work in practice.

Step 5: Communication

For anything beyond a single standalone meter, communication is what turns the meter into a data source:

  • RS485 / Modbus RTU — the workhorse for building management systems and energy dashboards. Meters daisy-chain on a two-wire bus; hundreds can share one loop. See the Modbus RS485 guide for addressing and wiring.
  • Pulse output — a simple kWh pulse (e.g. 1000 imp/kWh) counted by a PLC or building controller.
  • Wi-Fi / GPRS / 4G — for retrofits without cabling or for remote/multi-site monitoring.

Step 6: Certification for Your Market

Certification decides whether the meter is legal to install and bill with:

MarketStandard
International (IEC)IEC 62053-21 (active energy), IEC 62052-11 (general)
European UnionMID (2014/32/EU) for billing meters; CE marking
North AmericaANSI C12.20 for revenue metering
Utility projectsOften requires type approval per utility specification

If the meter is for your own energy monitoring (not utility billing), CE + IEC compliance is usually sufficient — and much cheaper than MID-certified units. See what MID certification actually means for the distinction.

Applications for Three-Phase Meters

  • Commercial buildings — main incomer and tenant sub-metering.
  • Industrial plants — feeder and machine metering; read the industrial energy meter buyer's guide.
  • Solar and storage — bidirectional metering for net metering; see what is a bidirectional meter.
  • EV charging — per-charger energy accounting on three-phase supplies; learn why the meter is critical in EV charging.
  • Utilities and distribution — feeder metering and loss analysis.

Selection Checklist (Download Mentally Before Calling Suppliers)

  1. System: 3P4W or 3P3W; nominal voltage confirmed.
  2. Connection: direct (5(60)A–10(100)A) or CT-operated (5 A secondary).
  3. Accuracy: Class 1.0 standard; 0.5S for cost allocation.
  4. Tariffs: 4–6 TOU tariffs + max demand logging if billed on demand.
  5. Communication: RS485/Modbus as a minimum; add pulse or wireless as needed.
  6. Certification: IEC 62053-21, CE; MID/ANSI only if legally required.
  7. Environment: DIN rail 35 mm, operating temperature range, display readability.
  8. Supply: sample testing, warranty, technical support response time.

FAQ

What is the difference between a 3-phase energy meter and a 3-phase power meter?

An energy meter registers energy (kWh) over time and is used for billing and monitoring. A power meter typically measures instantaneous electrical parameters (V, A, kW, power factor) and often includes energy registers as well. Many modern multifunction meters do both.

Can I use a 3P4W meter on a 3P3W system?

Usually yes — most meters have a configurable connection mode. Set the mode correctly before energizing, and match the voltage range to the system.

Do I need MID certification for a three-phase meter?

Only if the meter performs legal billing in the EU (or a market that requires MID). For internal monitoring, sub-metering and cost allocation, IEC 62053-21 compliance with CE marking is typically sufficient and significantly cheaper.

What CT ratio do I need for CT-operated three-phase metering?

Choose the CT ratio so the feeder's expected full-load current falls at 60–100% of the CT primary rating, and the meter's secondary matches the CT output (5 A or 1 A). Our CT selection guide covers this in detail.

Conclusion

A three-phase energy meter is a long-life investment: choose the connection mode and accuracy first, then tariffs, communication and certification. When in doubt, buy the meter with RS485 and a few extra tariffs — the cost difference is small and the flexibility is permanent.
