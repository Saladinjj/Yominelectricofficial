Understanding kWh: What You Are Actually Paying For

ET
ET Engineering Team 5 min read August 5, 2026

Your electricity bill is measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh), but most people never think about what a kWh actually represents. Once you understand it, you can read bills correctly, size solar systems, explain prepaid credits to tenants, and spot meter problems before they cost you money.

What Exactly Is a Kilowatt-Hour?

A kilowatt-hour is a unit of energy: the amount of energy used by a 1,000-watt appliance running for one hour. A 100 W light bulb left on for 10 hours consumes 1 kWh. A 2 kW heater running for 30 minutes consumes 1 kWh. It is simply power × time.

  • 1 kWh = 1,000 watt-hours
  • 1 kWh = 3.6 megajoules
  • 1 kWh is roughly what a modern fridge uses in a day
  • An electric shower uses about 1 kWh per 10–15 minutes of use

Watts vs Kilowatt-Hours: Power vs Energy

Watts measure the rate of energy use at an instant; kilowatt-hours measure the total energy used over time. This distinction explains why a 3 kW kettle uses the same energy as a 300 W TV over 10 hours — same kWh, different power and duration. Meters always track the total, which is why the register on a meter reads kWh, not kW.

How the Meter Measures kWh

An electricity meter multiplies instantaneous voltage by current to get power, then integrates power over time. Modern electronic meters do this digitally with a metering chip, while traditional electromechanical meters use a spinning disc. Both end up with the same register: the number of kWh consumed since installation.

Meters can be single-phase (2 wires) or three-phase (3 or 4 wires). Three-phase installations meter each phase and sum the total — important in factories and larger buildings where loads are spread across phases. For the full breakdown, read our guide to three-phase meters.

Reading Your Bill Like a Pro

  • Units used — the difference between this month's and last month's meter reading.
  • Tariff rate — price per kWh; may differ by time of day (TOU) or usage tier.
  • Fixed charges — connection and service fees billed regardless of usage.
  • Demand charges — for commercial accounts, a charge based on peak kW in the billing period.

If the units used suddenly double with no change in activity, check for a faulty appliance, a leaking immersion heater, or possible tampering — a meter that spins too fast should be verified by the utility.

Prepaid Meters and kWh Credits

Prepaid (STS) meters convert purchased credits into kWh at the current tariff. Because the consumer pays before use, prepaid metering removes billing disputes and debt — which is why it is standard for rentals and utilities in many markets. See our prepaid vs postpaid comparison and the guide to prepaid metering for landlords if you manage rental properties.

Using kWh to Size Solar and Batteries

Your daily kWh usage is the starting point for sizing any solar or storage system. Divide monthly kWh by 30 for a daily average, then size the solar array to generate that amount in your location's average sun hours. A battery bank should cover your essential evening loads — usually 30–60% of daily usage for whole-home backup.

FAQ

How many kWh does a typical home use?

It varies enormously: 150–400 kWh per month is common for small households in mild climates, while homes with electric heating or air conditioning can pass 1,000 kWh per month.

Is 1 kWh the same as 1 unit on my bill?

Yes — most utilities call one kWh a "unit". If your tariff is 0.15 per unit and you used 400 units, the energy charge is 60.

Why does my meter show kVAh as well?

Reactive energy (kvarh) and apparent energy (kVAh) registers appear on commercial meters with power-factor billing. They measure reactive power drawn by inductive loads like motors. If you see them on a residential meter, they are simply being recorded, not necessarily billed.

Conclusion

A kWh is power multiplied by time — nothing more, nothing less. Understanding it lets you read bills, explain prepaid credits, size solar and catch faults early.
