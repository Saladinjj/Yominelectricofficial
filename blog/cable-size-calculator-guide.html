Cable Size Calculator Guide: Current, Voltage Drop and Derating

ET
ET Engineering Team November 12, 2026 9 min read

A cable is correctly sized when three checks pass at the same time. First, the conductor's tabulated current-carrying capacity — corrected for ambient temperature, grouping and installation method — must be at least the design current of the circuit. Second, the rating of the protective device must not exceed that corrected capacity. Third, the voltage drop at full load must stay inside the limit you are designing to. Every cable size calculator is automating that sequence, and nothing else. Feed it the wrong design current, or let it ignore the correction factors, and it will return a confident, wrong number.

The three quantities a calculator is actually solving

Most online tools ask for a load in amps and return a conductor size. That shortcut hides the parts that decide real installations, so it is worth naming them before looking at any table.

QuantityWhat it meansWhere the number comes from
Ib — design currentThe current the circuit must carry continuously in normal serviceLoad schedule, not the nameplate of one machine
In — device ratingNominal rating of the breaker or fuse protecting the cableSelected so that Ib ≤ In ≤ Iz
Iz — corrected capacityTabulated capacity of the conductor multiplied by every applicable correction factorAmpacity table for the installation method, then derated
ΔU — voltage dropLoss along the run at full load, usually expressed as a percentageConductor resistance and reactance, length, current, power factor

Written as an inequality, the whole exercise is Ib ≤ In ≤ Iz, followed by a voltage-drop check. A calculator that skips the middle term has quietly assumed your breaker is the right size.

Why the tabulated ampacity is never the answer on its own

A conductor's published capacity assumes a defined set of conditions: a stated ambient temperature, a stated number of loaded circuits, and one installation method. Change any of them and the same copper carries less current.

  • Ambient temperature. Cable in a metal enclosure in a hot plant room is not in 30 °C air. Correction factors fall away quickly above 40 °C.
  • Grouping. Six loaded circuits sharing one tray heat each other. The factor is applied to every conductor in the group, not just the newest one.
  • Installation method. Free air, conduit in a wall, buried duct and thermal insulation are four different thermal environments for the same cable.
  • Harmonics. In circuits feeding switch-mode loads, triplen harmonics add in the neutral. A neutral sized as a fraction of the phase conductor can become the hottest conductor in the run.

This is the step where cheap calculators and real design diverge. Two identical 4 mm² cables in the same tray do not have the same capacity if one is loaded and the other is a spare.

Voltage drop is usually what decides the size on long runs

On short runs, ampacity decides. On long runs, drop decides, and it decides much earlier than most people expect — a cable can be thermally comfortable and still deliver an unacceptable voltage at the far end.

For a three-phase circuit the working form is straightforward:

ΔU = √3 × I × R × L, where R is the conductor resistance per metre and L the one-way length. Using copper at 0.0175 Ω·mm²/m at 20 °C and ignoring reactance, a 25 mm² conductor has R = 0.0007 Ω/m.

A worked three-phase example

Take a 63 A load at 400 V, a 45 m run, and a 25 mm² copper conductor.

ΔU = 1.732 × 63 × 0.0007 × 45 ≈ 3.4 V, which is about 0.9 % of 400 V. That comfortably passes a 5 % limit.

Now change only the length:

Run lengthVoltage dropAs % of 400 VVerdict against a 5 % limit
45 m3.4 V0.9 %Passes with margin
120 m9.2 V2.3 %Passes
250 m19.1 V4.8 %Borderline — go up a size

The instructive part is the reverse check. Dropping to 16 mm² at 45 m raises the drop to roughly 5.4 V, still only 1.3 %. So on this run, voltage drop does not decide anything — ampacity and derating do. Designers who always size on drop alone end up over-spending; designers who never check it end up with dim lights at the end of a long feeder.

Where calculators quietly go wrong

  • Using the nameplate of the largest machine instead of the diversified design current of the circuit.
  • Ignoring enclosure temperature. A panel that runs at 45–50 °C inside is a derated environment even when the room is comfortable.
  • Counting parallel runs as free capacity without checking that the load shares equally between them.
  • Sizing the conductor, then ordering a lug that does not fit the strand class — fine-stranded flexible cable and solid building wire need different terminations.
  • Forgetting the return path. The neutral and the protective conductor are part of the circuit, not an afterthought.

The cable is only as good as its termination

Cable sizing stops at the lug. A conductor chosen to carry 63 A loses that capacity at a joint that is undersized, over-torqued, or made between dissimilar metals without a bimetallic interface. Aluminium-to-copper joints in particular need a termination designed for the pair, because the joint — not the cable — is where the heat appears.

YOMIN supplies mechanical cable lugs in the terminals and connectors range that are specified alongside the conductor rather than after it: the dual-conductor aluminium screw lug, and the multi-hole aluminium alloy lug range. Both are mechanical designs, so the termination is made with a hex key and a torque value rather than a crimp die.

A short checklist before you order cable

  • Design current from the load schedule, with diversity applied.
  • Ambient temperature inside the enclosure, not the room.
  • Number of loaded circuits sharing the route.
  • Installation method, and whether any part runs in thermal insulation.
  • Voltage-drop limit for the load type, then the length of the actual route including verticals.
  • Termination: lug type, material pair, stud pattern, torque.

If two candidate sizes both pass the thermal check, choose on voltage drop and on what the terminals can accept. Those two constraints usually point at the same answer.

Frequently asked questions

What is the formula for cable size?

There is no single formula. The size is the smallest conductor whose corrected current-carrying capacity is at least the design current, whose protective device rating does not exceed that capacity, and whose voltage drop at full load stays within the limit. Capacity comes from a table, corrected by ambient temperature, grouping and installation method; drop comes from resistance, length and current.

Does voltage drop or ampacity decide the cable size?

Ampacity decides on short runs and voltage drop decides on long ones. In the worked example in this article, a 63 A load over 45 m is limited by thermal capacity, while the same load over 250 m is limited by drop. Always run both checks; neither one alone is a design.

How much current can a 25 mm² copper cable carry?

It depends on the installation. The same conductor carries noticeably less current in a hot enclosure with grouped circuits than in free air, so the tabulated figure must be corrected before it is compared with the design current. Quoting a single number for a cross-section without stating the installation method is the most common source of undersized cable.

Why does my calculator disagree with the electrician?

Almost always because of an input, not the arithmetic: a design current taken from a nameplate instead of a load schedule, an ambient temperature assumed to be the room rather than the enclosure, or grouping ignored. Ask which correction factors were applied and the disagreement usually resolves itself.

Do I need a different lug for flexible cable?

Yes. Fine-stranded flexible cable and solid or stranded building wire need different termination designs, and a lug rated for the cross-section can still be wrong for the strand class. Specify the conductor construction together with the cross-section.

Sizing a cable and ordering the termination in one go?

Send the design current, the route length and the conductor type. We will confirm the termination to match — mechanical aluminium lugs and connectors built for the conductor you have specified, not a size that happens to be in stock.
