Fish Tape Guide: Steel, Nylon or Fibreglass for Conduit Cable Pulling

ET
ET Engineering Team September 18, 2026 9 min read

The pull is decided before the cable goes in

Most difficult cable pulls are lost before anyone touches the cable. The conduit is installed, the bends are fixed, and the only remaining variable is the tool that has to travel the route first. A fish tape that is too stiff will not take the bend; one that is too soft will fold back on itself in the third sweep. Getting the material right is cheaper than cutting an access opening later.

What the tool has to do

A fish tape is doing four jobs at once: it has to push through the duct without buckling, follow bends without kinking, transmit enough pulling force back to the cable, and come out again. No single material wins all four, which is why working electricians carry more than one.

  • Pushability — resistance to buckling in an empty duct.
  • Trackability — ability to follow a bend instead of stopping at it.
  • Tensile strength — how much pull it survives before it yields.
  • Recoverability — whether it returns to a coil you can store and re-use.

The three materials

MaterialBehaviourSuitsWatch out for
Spring steelHigh stiffness, high pushability, keeps a coilLong straight runs, large-diameter conduit, masonry wallsConductive — never into a duct that may be live; inspect for kinks after a heavy pull
NylonFlexible, follows tight bends, lightShort runs, many bends, conduit already populated with other circuitsLess push — needs a helper at the far end on long runs
Fibreglass rodRigid but non-conductive, extends in sectionsUnderground ducts, draw pits, long buried routesDo not over-bend; keep the rod ends dry and clean

The three models also cover the same job from different directions. Where a steel tape is used to prove the route, the rod pushes through silted or long underground ducts, and the nylon tape handles the bends the steel refuses.

Matching the tool to the conduit

Three questions narrow the choice faster than any table: how long is the run, how many bends, and how much space is left in the duct.

  • Run length. As a working rule, one person can manage a tape up to roughly 15 m of straight conduit; beyond that, either add a rod extension or post someone at the far end to catch and pull.
  • Bend count. Two 90° sweeps are comfortable for steel tape. From three sweeps onward, switch to nylon or rod the duct in sections.
  • Fill. A common planning figure is around 40% conduit fill for the final cable bundle. If the duct is already near that, the route must be proved with a smaller tool and the pull lubricated.

Conduit diameter sets the floor: a 20 mm conduit takes a 3–4 mm tape, while 50 mm and above will carry a 6 mm rod comfortably.

Working near live ducts

The one rule that is not negotiable: never introduce a conductive tape into a duct that could be energised. Spring steel is an excellent tape and a very poor idea inside a panel that has not been isolated. In substations, around street-lighting circuits and anywhere the layout is not fully documented, the non-conductive fibreglass rod (YM-CP002) is the correct tool even though it is slower.

Rod ends do as much work as the rod

A rod without the right termination is a stick. The published YM-CP002 fittings cover the three normal duties:

  • Hook — engages a draw cord or an existing cable and drags it back.
  • Eye — carries the draw cord through for a straight-through pull.
  • Flexible extension — takes the lead through a tight sweep where the rod body cannot follow.

Storage, inspection and life

Steel tape is the only one of the three that degrades in a way you cannot see. Water inside the coil, a kink left by an overloaded pull, or a burr on the leading end will all shorten its life and score the inside of the conduit. Wipe it down, inspect the length after a heavy pull, and re-coil it to the diameter it was supplied at rather than to whatever the last user left behind. Nylon tape should be stored out of direct sun; fibreglass rod should be stored straight or in a large loop and never forced into a tight coil.

Frequently asked questions

How long can a fish tape run be?

Coils of 10 m and 15 m cover most domestic and commercial conduit work; longer runs are handled by coupling rod sections rather than by carrying a longer tape.

Can I use a steel fish tape in a live panel?

No. Use a non-conductive fibreglass rod, isolate first, and treat an undocumented duct as live until proved otherwise.

What is the difference between a fish tape and a duct rodder?

A fish tape is a coiled tape that is pushed and steered through a route that is mostly accessible. A duct rodder is a rigid, non-conductive rod used on buried ducts and draw pits where the route is long and the entry points are fixed.

Do I need lubricant?

On a route that is already near its fill limit, or on any pull over about 20 m, a purpose-made cable lubricant reduces the pulling force noticeably and lowers the risk of damaging the cable sheath.
