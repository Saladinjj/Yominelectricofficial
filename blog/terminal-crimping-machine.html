Terminal Crimping Machine: Which Machine You Actually Need

ET
ET Engineering Team November 10, 2026 10 min read

Two unrelated machines are sold under the name terminal crimping machine, and buying the wrong one is a five-figure mistake. A wire crimping press closes a terminal onto a stripped conductor and is judged on crimp height and pull-out force. A terminal production machine machines brass terminals, links and busbar lugs out of bar stock and is judged on cycle time and tool life. They share a search term, they are often shown next to each other in the same catalogue, and they solve completely different problems.

Two machines, one search term

The confusion is not the buyer's fault. Both families are legitimately described as terminal machines, and a supplier listing a production line will often include the word crimping because the line ends in a crimping or pressing station. What separates them is what goes in and what comes out.

Read that table once more with your own output in mind. If you buy terminals and crimp them onto cable, you are in the first row. If you make terminals and sell them, you are in the second. If you assemble blocks, you are in the third, and a crimping press will not help you at all.

What a wire crimping press actually does

A crimping press is a vertical press with an applicator. The applicator feeds a terminal from a strip into the anvil, the press closes the crimper down, and the terminal is formed around the conductor and the insulation in two separate crimps. Conductor crimp carries the current. Insulation crimp carries the mechanical relief, and a machine that gets the second one wrong will pass an electrical test and still fail in the field after a few hundred flex cycles.

Three variables decide whether the result is acceptable:

  • Crimp height. The single number that is measured with a micrometer on every setup and at intervals during the run. Too high and the joint is loose; too low and the conductor is damaged and the resistance rises.
  • Terminal and wire combination. Barrel size must match the conductor class and the insulation diameter. A terminal that suits a fine-strand flexible cable will not close correctly on a solid conductor of the same nominal area.
  • Tooling condition. Crimpers wear, and a worn crimper produces joints that look right and pull out early. Tooling is a consumable with a life, not a permanent part.

Verification is mechanical, not visual. A pull test on a sample from each setup, at the value the applicable standard requires, is what turns a crimp into a documented joint. For crimped connections that standard is IEC 60352-2, and the buyer's own quality plan usually adds a sampling frequency on top of it.

Within this family there is a wide range of automation, from a hand tool through a bench press with a foot switch to a fully automatic stripper-crimper that measures, strips, crimps and pull-tests without an operator. The right level is set by annual volume and by how many terminal families you handle, not by what looks modern.

What a terminal production machine actually does

A production machine starts from bar or wire stock and makes the terminal itself. The sequence is cutting to length, drilling or punching the wire hole, tapping the screw thread, and chamfering the entry so the conductor does not catch. Machines differ in how many of those operations they combine and how the part is transferred between them.

Two architectures dominate. A transfer machine moves the part through dedicated stations, one operation per station, and is fast on a single part family that rarely changes. An integrated machining centre performs several operations at one position with a tool changer, and is slower per part but far quicker to change over. Neither is better in the abstract: a shop making one lug size in millions wants the transfer machine, and a shop quoting twenty variants a month wants the flexible one.

The economics are also different from what buyers expect. The machine does not compete with the price of a bought terminal on material cost. It competes on lead time, on the ability to hold a drawing that no catalogue covers, and on the margin between bar stock and a finished part. That is why the buyers of these machines are usually panel builders and terminal manufacturers with a steady family of parts, not workshops solving a one-off shortage.

Why the catalogue blurs the two

Product titles are written for search engines, not for engineers. A production line that ends in a crimping station will legitimately carry the phrase terminal crimping machine in its title, and a photograph of a machine with a control screen tells you nothing about which family it belongs to. Three things resolve it in under a minute:

  • Look at the input. Reels of terminals and cut cable mean a crimping press. Bar stock, coils or a vibratory bowl of blanks mean a production machine.
  • Look for a die or an applicator. A crimping press always has interchangeable tooling sized to the terminal. A production machine has drills, taps and fixtures instead.
  • Ask what the output is measured in. Crimped assemblies per hour is a crimping press. Parts per minute from bar is a production machine.

Five questions that decide the machine

  • What is the annual output? Below a few hundred thousand crimps a year, a bench press with good tooling is usually the honest answer, and a fully automatic line will sit idle.
  • How many terminal families? One family justifies dedicated stations. Twenty families justify quick-change tooling, and the changeover time becomes the number that matters.
  • What is the conductor range? A press sized for 0.5–2.5 mm² will not close a 50 mm² lug, and buying the larger press does not automatically give you the tooling for the smaller wire.
  • Who maintains the tooling? Crimpers, drills and taps all wear. If nobody on site can set a crimp height or replace a tap, the machine choice has to account for that.
  • Is the part on a drawing? If a catalogue terminal fits, buy it. A production machine only earns its place when the part genuinely does not exist off the shelf.

What to put in the RFQ

Machines in this price class are quoted against a specification, not a model number, and the specification is what the supplier will be held to at acceptance. Send:

  • The terminal or part drawing, with the tolerance that actually matters marked, not the whole title block.
  • Conductor range and insulation diameter if the requirement is crimping; bar size, alloy and temper if it is production.
  • Required output per hour, and the number of shifts it must sustain.
  • Changeover expectation, stated as a time in minutes for a defined change.
  • Power supply, compressed air availability, and the footprint you actually have.
  • Which acceptance test will be run, and who provides the gauge or the sample.

The last item is the one that prevents arguments. A machine that meets its cycle time but cannot hold the crimp height tolerance is not a working machine, and if the acceptance test was never named, that conversation happens after delivery.

Commissioning checks

Three checks catch most of the problems in the first week. Measure crimp height on the first article and record it, rather than judging by eye. Pull-test a sample and keep the result with the machine record. And run the machine for an hour at production rate before signing, because a machine that cycles correctly for ten minutes and drifts after an hour is a tooling or thermal problem that a short trial will not reveal.
