Cartridge Fuse: Matching Size, Curve and Breaking Capacity

ET
ET Engineering Team November 25, 2026 9 min read

A cartridge fuse is a cylindrical fuse in which the element is enclosed in a tube filled with silica sand and closed by metal end caps. The sand quenches the arc when the element melts, which is what allows a body only a few centimetres long to clear a short circuit of many kiloamps.

It is also the easiest protective device in a panel to get wrong, because a cartridge fuse is never a part on its own: it is a system of fuse, holder and base. A fuse of the correct current rating in the wrong body length will not clip in, a fuse with the wrong curve will nuisance-trip a motor starter, and an AC-rated fuse fitted to a DC string can fail to extinguish the arc at all. This guide covers the three things that decide whether the part works: size, curve and breaking capacity.

Size comes first, because it is what the holder accepts

Cartridge fuses are identified by body diameter and length, and the holder is built around exactly one of those combinations. The common cylindrical families are 10 × 38 mm, 14 × 51 mm and 22 × 58 mm; larger industrial circuits use blade-terminal cartridge forms such as BS88 and the NH knife-blade family, which is a different mechanical system again.

Two fuses can share a current and voltage rating and still be mechanically incompatible. Before anything else, confirm the body size and the terminal style against the holder already on the panel or the holder you intend to order. It is also worth checking the holder's own rating: a 10 × 38 mm holder rated 32 A will not become a 63 A circuit because a larger fuse is forced into it.

Reading the marking: five values, one line of text

A compliant cartridge fuse carries five pieces of information on a body a few millimetres across. Reading them in the right order prevents most mis-orders.

  • Rated current, in amps. This is the current the fuse carries continuously without melting, not the current at which it blows.
  • Rated voltage, AC or DC. A fuse rated 500 V AC is not automatically suitable for 500 V DC; the two are separate ratings.
  • Breaking capacity, in kiloamps, often printed as a small number with a kA suffix. This is the fault current the fuse can safely interrupt without rupturing its body.
  • Operating class, the letter code such as gG, aM, aR or gPV. The letter is the single most useful item on the marking because it tells you what the fuse is intended to protect.
  • Standard reference, normally IEC 60269 for low voltage fuses, and the manufacturer's type code.

If a supplier cannot tell you the operating class and the breaking capacity of a part, the part is not specified, however good the price looks.

The curve class decides what the fuse protects

Two fuses with identical current and voltage ratings but different class letters behave completely differently under overload. The class is the difference between protecting cable and protecting a motor.

The practical symptom of a wrong class is a fuse that blows on every start. That is not a faulty fuse; it is an aM duty being asked of a gG part, or the reverse.

AC and DC are not interchangeable

Extinguishing a DC arc is harder than extinguishing an AC arc, because an AC fault current passes through zero twice per cycle and gives the fuse a natural moment to stop conducting. A DC fault current does not, so a DC-rated fuse is built differently inside and is tested to a DC breaking capacity.

In a photovoltaic string the consequence is serious: an under-rated or AC-only fuse may fail to clear a fault, and the arc can persist inside the body. This is why the gPV class exists and why the voltage rating has to be checked against the array's maximum system voltage, not its nominal operating voltage. The same logic applies to battery storage and to the DC side of any inverter.

Breaking capacity and the fault level of the board

The breaking capacity has to exceed the prospective short-circuit current at the point of installation. In a small distribution board fed by a modest transformer that is rarely a constraint; on the load side of a large transformer, or in a panel close to the origin of the installation, the available fault current can exceed the rating of a small cartridge fuse by a wide margin.

Two answers are used in practice: fit a fuse with an adequate breaking capacity, or rely on documented back-up protection from an upstream device, which is only valid if the manufacturer's coordination tables cover that exact pair. Assuming back-up protection without the table is the same as having none.

A selection sequence that avoids the usual mistakes

  1. Measure the holder. Body diameter and length, or blade style, before anything else.
  2. Fix the class from the load. gG for cable and general distribution, aM for motor circuits with a separate overload relay, aR for drives, gPV for PV strings.
  3. Choose the current rating from the protected conductor or the load, then check that the holder and the fuse agree on the maximum.
  4. Check the voltage rating in the right currency — AC or DC — against the system's maximum, not its nominal, voltage.
  5. Confirm the breaking capacity against the prospective fault current, or against a documented coordination table.

One further habit is worth the effort: record the class, size and breaking capacity of every fuse type used in a panel on the door schedule. Fuse substitution by size alone is the most common cause of a protection failure that nobody notices until it matters.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a cartridge fuse and an HRC fuse?

An HRC fuse is a cartridge fuse built for high breaking capacity, with a ceramic body and a sand filler. The terms overlap: most industrial cartridge fuses are HRC types, while small glass-body cartridge fuses are general-purpose parts with much lower breaking capacity.

Can I replace a 10 x 38 mm fuse with a 14 x 51 mm one of the same rating?

No. The body size is fixed by the holder and the contact spacing. A larger body will not make proper contact, and forcing it defeats the spring pressure that the fuse relies on to keep resistance low.

Why does my motor starter fuse blow every time the motor starts?

Almost always because a gG general-purpose fuse has been fitted where an aM motor-rated fuse belongs. An aM part is designed to ride through starting current and to leave overload protection to the relay, so the two must be used together.

Do I need a DC-rated fuse for a solar array?

Yes. PV strings require a gPV fuse rated for the array's maximum system voltage in DC. An AC-rated cartridge fuse is not a substitute, because a DC arc has no natural current zero to help extinguish it.
