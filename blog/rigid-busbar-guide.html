Rigid Busbar Guide: Choosing Copper Bar for Power Distribution

ET
ET Engineering Team September 15, 2026 8 min read

A rigid busbar is a solid conductor — a flat bar, or a bar bent into a shape — that carries current and holds its own position without support along its length. It is the oldest and still the most common way to move heavy current around a panel, a battery rack or a piece of power distribution equipment.

Specifying one is mostly a geometry exercise. The current rating tells you how much metal, but the bends and the hole positions decide whether the part fits and whether the joint survives.

Rigid or flexible?

This is the first decision, and it is not about current — both types carry the same current for the same cross-section.

 Rigid busbarFlexible busbar
Load pathDirect, short, stiffAbsorbs movement along the route
Best whereFixed geometry, short runs, firm mountingVibration, thermal expansion, tolerance between two assemblies
FittingMust be bent and drilled to match exactlyTolerates a small misalignment
Typical usePanelboards, switchgear, distribution equipmentBattery modules, inverters, moving assemblies

A rigid bar is the better answer when the two things it connects are bolted down and will not move relative to each other. As soon as one end can move — a battery module that swells, a door that opens, an inverter on resilient mounts — a rigid bar turns that movement into stress at the joint. That is where a flexible laminated link belongs instead.

In practice many designs use both: rigid bars for the main distribution spine, flexible links at the points where a module or a subassembly has to be isolated from that spine.

Bends and holes decide the design

Two rigid busbars can have identical cross-section and completely different cost, because the manufacturing work sits in the forming and drilling:

  • Bend count and direction. Every bend is an operation. The offset direction also sets how much bar the bend consumes.
  • Bend radius. Tight radii risk cracking, particularly on harder tempers.
  • Hole pattern. Hole diameter, pitch and edge distance all matter. A hole placed too close to the edge reduces the effective cross-section right where the current crowds.
  • Plating. Contact areas are usually plated separately from the insulated or coated run, so the drawing has to show which areas stay bare.

One detail that gets missed: the joint is often the limiting point, not the bar. A bar rated well above the load still overheats if the contact area is too small or the mating surfaces are not flat. Give the contact pads enough area and keep them clean.

Copper or aluminium?

The published material field on the YOMIN rigid busbar range is Copper / aluminium, and the trade-off is the usual one. Copper gives more current per unit of cross-section, which matters when the envelope is fixed. Aluminium is lighter and cheaper per kilogram and is a reasonable choice for larger, longer bars — provided the terminations are designed for it.

Do not mix the two at a joint without thinking about it. Copper and aluminium in direct contact under moisture is a known corrosion path, and the joint is exactly where you cannot afford it.

Where rigid busbars are used

The published application is low-impedance power distribution in switchgear, panelboards and battery packs, which covers:

  • Switchgear and panelboards — main and distribution bars, phase bars and neutral links.
  • Battery packs and energy storage — module interconnects and DC bus links, including LiFePO4 packs where the bar material has to suit the terminal.
  • New energy and EV assemblies — custom hard busbars for power distribution inside a vehicle or a charging unit.
  • General power distribution equipment — anywhere a rigid bar replaces a heavy cable run.

What to send with an enquiry

  1. A dimensioned drawing — bar width, thickness, overall length, every bend angle and the developed length.
  2. The hole schedule — diameter, pitch, and which holes are contact points.
  3. The current rating, continuous or short-time.
  4. The material and plating, including which areas must remain unplated.
  5. Any coating requirement — such as epoxy coating over the non-contact run.
  6. The quantity and whether it is a one-off or a repeat production item.

A rigid busbar is a made-to-order part. If a supplier quotes without seeing a drawing, the number is provisional.

Production and lead time

YOMIN manufactures rigid busbars in custom lengths and bends under ISO 9001 and CE certified manufacturing.
