Ring Terminal Guide: Stud Size, Wire Gauge and the Crimp Die

ET
ET Engineering Team November 11, 2026 8 min read

A ring terminal is a crimp lug whose tongue is a complete circle, so the fixing screw passes through metal on every side. That closed shape is the whole point: unlike a fork terminal, a ring terminal cannot slide out from under a loosened screw, and it cannot be pulled sideways off the stud by vibration or by a cable tug.

Specify one with three numbers, not one: the conductor range the barrel accepts, the stud size the tongue clears, and the barrel style you can actually crimp on site. Get those three right and the joint will outlast the panel. Get the barrel-to-wire match wrong and no amount of torque on the screw will save it.

What each number on the label actually controls

Catalogues list ring terminals by wire range and stud size, and buyers often treat the second number as the important one. It is not. Each figure controls a different part of the assembly, and each one produces a different failure when it is wrong.

Figure on the labelWhat it decidesWhat happens when it is wrong
Conductor range, mm² or AWGThe bore of the barrel and therefore the crimp dieToo small cuts strands at the barrel mouth; too large leaves a loose bundle that pulls out under load
Stud size, M3 to M12The clearance hole in the tongue and the contact patch on the padUndersized will not fit; oversized reduces contact area and lets a washer bridge the joint
Barrel and insulation styleDie profile, crimp height and the temperature the joint survivesInsulated terminal crimped with a bare-terminal die crushes the sleeve and leaves the conductor barrel under-crimped

The rule that follows is simple: the barrel must match the conductor, and the tongue must match the stud. A terminal with a large tongue and a small barrel is a legitimate part, but it is not interchangeable with the reverse.

Insulated, non-insulated or heat-shrink

All three are ring terminals. The difference is what protects the transition from cable insulation to bare conductor, which is where moisture and strand fatigue start.

TypeChoose it whenThe trade-off
Non-insulatedThe joint sits in a control cabinet, is protected by a cover, and space is tightNothing covers the barrel, so a stray strand or a slipped tool can bridge to the next terminal
Pre-insulated, PVC or nylon sleevePanel wiring, appliance and automotive harnesses where the joint is touched and inspectedThe sleeve sets the maximum temperature and the die must be the insulated type; a universal die often crimps the sleeve and not the barrel
Heat-shrink or sealedOutdoor, washdown, or where condensation cycles the jointSlowest to install and needs a heat source, but it is the only version that keeps water out of the barrel

RoHS matters here because the plating is what carries the current long term. Tin-plated copper is the default for a reason: it does not form the brittle oxide that bare copper does, and it stays solderable and crimpable after months in stores.

The barrel, not the tongue, decides whether the joint holds

Two ring terminals can look identical from the top and behave completely differently. The difference is inside the barrel.

  • Seam. A brazed or welded seam stays closed when the die compresses the barrel. A butted seam can open under a heavy crimp and let the bundle escape.
  • Entry chamfer. A chamfered mouth guides the strands in without catching them. A sharp mouth shaves strands, and a shaved bundle loses cross-section exactly where the current concentrates.
  • Inspection window or sight hole. It lets the operator confirm the conductor reached the end of the barrel before the crimp, which is the single most common assembly mistake.
  • Separate insulation support. The rear crimp should grip the cable insulation, not the conductor. It carries no current; it only relieves strain.

Match the die to the barrel, not to the tool brand

Crimp quality is set by the die cavity, and the die is matched to a barrel geometry, not to a brand of pliers. A tool that is sold as universal will produce an acceptable joint on one barrel style and a marginal one on the next.

The practical check is a pull test on a sample from each reel, not a visual check on the finished panel. Crimp height is the process variable to control: too little compression and the strands are not cold-welded together, too much and the barrel cracks or the strands are severed at the mouth. A go/no-go gauge on the crimp height catches drift from a worn die before it reaches a shipment.

Hand crimping with pliers is not a controlled process. It flattens the barrel into an oval, leaves the seam unsupported and gives no repeatable compression, which is why it is the first thing an inspector looks for on a returned harness.

The four failures that come back from the field

SymptomUsual causeDesign or process fix
Strands cut at the barrel mouthDie too small for the conductor class, or strip length too short so the bundle sits proud of the barrelMatch the die to the actual wire class, including fine-strand flexible cable, and set strip length so the conductor ends flush with the barrel end
Terminal pulls off at low forceUnder-crimp, insulation trapped inside the conductor barrel, or a barrel sized for a larger conductorRe-die, re-strip, and check the conductor range against the printed cable size rather than the nominal one
Green deposit inside the jointMoisture reaching bare copper at the crimp, often with a dissimilar metal nearbyTin-plated terminals, sealed or heat-shrink version outdoors, and a joint that drains rather than traps water
Loose screw and a warm terminalContact pressure lost to thermal cycling, no spring washer, or a tongue that is too thin for the stud torqueSpecify tongue thickness with the stud size, use a spring or serrated washer, and torque to the stud specification rather than to feel

Copper, brass or aluminium

Copper carries the current with the least cross-section and crimps predictably, which is why it dominates control and battery work. Brass is stiffer and cheaper and is common on small stud sizes where mechanical strength matters more than conductivity. Aluminium is chosen for weight and cost on large conductors, but it needs a bimetallic transition: a plain copper tongue bolted to an aluminium bar is a galvanic couple, and the corrosion products that form at that interface add resistance until the joint runs hot.

For aluminium cable, use a bimetallic lug and finish the joint with a barrier compound. For copper cable on an aluminium busbar, the same rule applies in reverse. The terminal is rarely the weak point; the interface between two metals is.

Specification checklist

  • Conductor range written as a range, not a single size, and matched to the cable class actually used — fine-strand flexible cable needs a different die from solid conductor.
  • Stud size stated as M-number plus the tongue thickness the torque requires.
  • Barrel and insulation type, with the temperature rating if the joint sits near a heater, a brake resistor or an engine.
  • Plating and, where the environment demands it, the RoHS or automotive compliance reference.
  • Crimp specification on the drawing: die part number, crimp height and pull-force acceptance, so the joint does not depend on who is holding the tool.

Send the conductor range, stud size and the environment the joint works in, and a supplier can confirm the correct barrel and die combination before the harness is built rather than after the first pull test fails.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a ring terminal and a fork terminal?

A ring terminal has a closed circular tongue, so the screw must be removed to fit or remove it, but the joint cannot slip out. A fork terminal is open at one end, which makes it faster to fit and allows removal with the screw loosened rather than removed. Where vibration or a safety circuit is involved, the closed ring is the safer choice.

How do I choose between stud size and wire size?

They answer different questions and both are needed. The wire range sets the barrel bore and the crimp die; the stud size sets the tongue hole and the contact patch. Choose the barrel from the conductor and the tongue from the stud, then check that a terminal exists with that combination.

Can I crimp a ring terminal with pliers?

You can deform one, but the result is not a controlled crimp. Pliers flatten the barrel into an oval, leave the seam unsupported and give no repeatable compression, so the joint depends on operator strength. Use a die matched to the barrel and verify with a pull test.

Do insulated terminals need a different die?

Yes. An insulated terminal has a conductor crimp and an insulation crimp at different heights, and the die has to form both without cutting the sleeve. A bare-terminal die used on an insulated part typically crushes the sleeve and under-crimps the conductor barrel.
