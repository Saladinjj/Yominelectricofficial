Current Transformer Cabinet: What Goes Inside and How to Specify It

ET
ET Engineering Team November 20, 2026 6 min read

A current transformer cabinet is the enclosure that holds the metering current transformers, the shorting terminal block and the secondary wiring that carries a proportional current to the meter. Specify it by primary current first, then ratio and accuracy class, then burden, and only then by cabinet dimensions and access.

The order of those four decisions is not a formality. A current transformer cabinet is built around devices that cannot be re-rated on site: a 400/5 A transformer will not become a 600/5 A transformer, and a Class 1.0 unit cannot be relabelled Class 0.5. The first two lines of the specification lock in everything that follows.

What a current transformer cabinet contains

The enclosure itself is normally sheet steel with a sealable door and a metering window. Inside, the metering CTs sit on the primary conductors, either as busbar or window types that the conductor passes through, or as split-core units that close around a live bar without disconnection.

  • The metering CTs, one per phase, plus a fourth on the neutral where the tariff requires neutral measurement.
  • A shorting or test terminal block, which is the single most important accessory in the cabinet.
  • Secondary wiring in a cross-section sized for the burden, routed away from the primary conductors.
  • Fuses and links on any voltage circuit that shares the enclosure.
  • A seal point, because the cabinet is the boundary between the utility's measurement and the consumer's installation.

Cabinet, combined metering unit or panel-mounted CT?

These three arrangements answer different questions, and confusing them is the most common source of a cabinet that has to be rebuilt after delivery.

A cabinet is chosen when the utility needs a sealable, accessible measurement point independent of the switchgear. A combined unit is chosen when the site is outdoors and a separate enclosure would need its own foundation and primary connections.

Ratio and class: specify from the meter backwards

The ratio is written as primary over secondary, for example 400/5 A. On IEC equipment the secondary is almost always 5 A, so the ratio decision is really a decision about the primary rating.

Choose the primary so the real load sits in the upper part of the range without ever exceeding it. A CT run at a small fraction of its rating loses accuracy, and one run above its rating loses accuracy faster and permanently.

Burden is what decides whether the class survives

An accuracy class is meaningless on its own because it is quoted at a burden, expressed in volt-amperes. The burden is the total impedance of the secondary circuit: the meter, the terminal block, every joint, and the resistance of the wire itself.

A CT ordered as Class 0.5 at 5 VA will not hold that class in a loop that presents 10 VA. The ratio stays correct, so the meter still shows a plausible number, which is exactly what makes the error hard to find. Keep the secondary run short, keep the cross-section generous, and write the burden on the order instead of accepting a supplier default.

The shorting terminal block is not optional

A CT secondary must never be open-circuited while the primary is energised. With no burden the transformer tries to drive its whole magnetising current into a rising impedance, and the terminal voltage can climb high enough to break down the insulation and endanger anyone standing near the cabinet.

A shorting terminal block gives the technician somewhere to make the secondary safe before the meter is removed. It is also the part most often quietly dropped from a cheap cabinet, which is why it belongs on the specification as its own line item rather than as an assumption.

Selection checklist

  • Primary current taken from the real load profile, not from the rating of the incoming breaker.
  • Ratio and secondary written as a single figure, for example 400/5 A.
  • Accuracy class matched to the tariff: Class 0.5 where the reading is billed, Class 1.0 where it only drives monitoring.
  • Burden in VA, calculated from the meter, the wiring and the terminal block together.
  • Build: busbar, toroidal or split-core, depending on whether the primary can be isolated during installation.
  • Cabinet access: door swing, sealing point, and the cable route back to the meter.
  • Test facilities: a shorting block, plus test links if the utility requires them.

Where cabinets go wrong in practice

Three failures account for most of the rework we see. The first is a ratio chosen from the breaker rating rather than the load, which leaves the meter reading a small fraction of full scale for the life of the installation. The second is a burden that was never calculated, so a Class 0.5 transformer delivers Class 1.0 behaviour and nobody notices until the bill is disputed.

The third is mechanical: the secondary cable was run before the cabinet position was fixed, so the loop is longer than the burden allows. Fixing that means new cable, not a new transformer, and it is the most expensive of the three to correct after commissioning.

Frequently asked questions

Does a current transformer cabinet need its own earth connection?

Yes. The enclosure must be bonded to the installation earth, and the CT secondary circuit is earthed at one point only. Earthing the secondary at both ends creates a loop that carries stray current through the measuring circuit and corrupts the reading.

Can a split-core CT be installed without disconnecting the primary?

That is what a split-core unit is for. It closes around an existing bar or cable, so metering can be added to a live installation during a planned window rather than a full isolation. The trade-off is that the core joint must close squarely, because an uneven gap changes the magnetic path and therefore the accuracy.

Why does the meter read a plausible but wrong value?

Nearly always ratio or burden. A ratio programmed wrongly in the meter gives a reading that scales consistently; an excessive burden gives a reading that drifts with load. Compare the ratio programmed into the meter against the ratio printed on the transformer before suspecting the transformer itself.

Which accuracy class should a tenant meter use?

Class 0.5 is the usual choice where the reading is billed. Class 1.0 is adequate where the reading informs monitoring and the tenant is billed from a different source.
