Energy Meter Pulse Output: What It Is, How It Is Counted and How to Wire It

ET
ET Engineering Team November 21, 2026 9 min read

Short answer: a pulse output is a switching contact or transistor inside the meter that closes once for every fixed amount of energy counted. The meter does the measuring; the pulse output only reports "one more unit has passed". That makes it the cheapest way to bring energy into a PLC, a BMS or a legacy data logger - no protocol, no register map, no polling. It is also the output that fails most quietly, because a missing resistor or a filter setting does not produce an error, only a total that is too low.

The buyer's question is rarely "does it have a pulse output". It is "which pulse output, how many pulses per kWh, and what do I have to add to make it count correctly". Those three answers decide whether the installation works.

What a pulse output physically is

Inside the meter, the metering circuit increments an energy register. At a fixed increment - the pulse constant - it operates a small output stage. In one meter that stage is a relay contact; in another it is an opto-isolated transistor. Either way, the outside world sees two terminals that change state, and a counter that adds up the transitions.

Nothing about the pulse carries the voltage, current or power factor. A pulse output reports energy only, and usually cumulative energy in one direction. If you need power, voltage, current, power factor or per-phase values, a pulse output cannot supply them, no matter how fast you count.

The type is not a preference, it is an interface decision. A dry contact will drive almost anything slowly; an open collector is fast and cheap but needs the pull-up; a passive output assumes the counter provides the current. Ask for the output type in writing, not just "pulse output".

kWh per pulse is the only number that matters

The pulse constant is quoted either as impulses per kWh (imp/kWh) or as kWh per pulse. They are reciprocals, and mixing them up is the classic scaling error.

kWh per pulse = 1 / (imp/kWh). A meter marked 1000 imp/kWh therefore produces one pulse per 0.001 kWh.

The last column is the one that catches people out. At 3 kW, a 2,000 imp/kWh meter produces almost 1.7 pulses per second. A relay output rated for a few operations per second will lose counts at that rate; a counter input with a 10 ms filter will lose them too. High pulse constants are useful for resolution and hostile to slow inputs.

Many meters carry both a pulse output and an RS485 port. That combination is worth having: RS485 for the detail, pulses for a totaliser that keeps working when the network or the polling master does not.

Two practical points. Use a high-speed or interrupt-capable input rather than a general-purpose digital input with a long filter, and keep the pulse cable away from contactor and VFD wiring. A pulse train is a low-energy signal and it behaves like one.

Five reasons counts go missing

  • No pull-up on an open-collector output. The input stays in one state and the total never moves - the most common single fault.
  • Input filter longer than the pulse. Fine at light load, lossy at full load, and almost impossible to spot from a daily total.
  • Both edges counted. The total reads exactly double, which looks like a scaling error and is not.
  • Wrong pulse constant applied. The error is proportional, so it hides at one load and grows at another.
  • Noise on a long unshielded run. Contactors inject pulses that were never generated.

Commissioning check: run a known load for a measured time, compare the counted energy against the meter display, and confirm the two agree to within the meter's accuracy class. Do that before the panel is signed off, not after a month of billing data is suspect.

Frequently asked questions

Does a pulse output measure power or energy?

Energy only, and normally cumulative energy in one direction. Power, voltage, current and power factor require a communicating meter or a separate transducer.

Can I connect a pulse output directly to a PLC input?

Only if the input suits the output type. An open-collector output needs a pull-up resistor; a dry contact needs a wetting voltage; a passive two-wire output needs the counter to supply current. Check all three before wiring.

How do I convert pulses to kWh?

Divide one by the pulse constant in imp/kWh, then multiply by the pulse count. At 1,000 imp/kWh each pulse is 0.001 kWh, so 250,000 pulses equal 250 kWh.

Why does my total read exactly double the meter display?

The counter is almost certainly counting both edges of the pulse. Set it to count one edge only, then re-check the scaling.

Is a pulse output enough for tenant billing?

It is enough for a total, but not for time-of-use tariffs, reverse energy or per-phase data. For billing, specify RS485 as well and use the pulse output as an independent cross-check.

Integrating an energy meter into a PLC or BMS?

Tell us the counter input, the pulse constant you need and whether RS485 is also required. We will confirm the right meter and the terminal arrangement before the order.
