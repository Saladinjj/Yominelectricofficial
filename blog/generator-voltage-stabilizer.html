Generator Voltage Stabilizer: What It Fixes and How to Size It

ET
ET Engineering Team November 7, 2026 11 min read

A generator voltage stabilizer is a load-side device that holds the voltage delivered to your equipment inside a defined band when the supply comes from a generator instead of the grid. It sits between the generator output or the changeover switch and the load, measures the output voltage continuously, and corrects it by switching transformer taps or by driving a motorised variac.

The word that causes most of the confusion is AVR. Two different devices carry that name. The excitation AVR lives inside the alternator and regulates field current so the machine's own terminal voltage stays near its set point. The voltage stabilizer is a separate enclosure on the load side. An excitation AVR cannot correct a voltage drop that happens inside your installation, and a stabilizer cannot repair an alternator that is regulating badly. Buying one while expecting the other is the most common way this decision goes wrong.

This guide is about the load-side device. It covers why a generator supply behaves differently from a transformer supply, how to size a stabilizer when the load includes motors, which technology suits which load, and the four things a stabilizer will never fix.

Why a generator supply needs different treatment

A distribution transformer is a stiff source. Its impedance is low relative to the load, a fault on the secondary can pull thousands of amps, and the voltage at the terminals barely moves when a motor starts. A generator is a soft source by comparison. Its ability to hold voltage under a sudden load step is limited by the subtransient reactance of the machine and by how fast the excitation system can push field current back in.

That produces three behaviours you will not see on a healthy grid connection:

  • Voltage dip on load application. Starting a direct-on-line motor draws several times its running current for a second or two. On a generator that current flows through a much higher source impedance, so the terminal voltage drops visibly — lights dim, contactor coils chatter, a drive may trip on undervoltage.
  • Voltage rise at light load. A generator running well below its rating can sit above nominal, particularly if the excitation is set for full-load compensation. The stabilizer has to accept that high input as well as the sag.
  • Frequency that moves with the load. The governor responds to load changes more slowly than the excitation system. A frequency excursion changes the volts-per-hertz ratio seen by motors and drives, and no voltage stabilizer can correct it.

The practical consequence is that the stabilizer and the generator have to be specified together. A unit chosen from a catalogue page on load kVA alone will either be too small to pass starting current or too narrow in input range to accept the generator's light-load voltage.

If your generator is hunting, surging or dropping voltage so far that equipment resets, the first question is not which stabilizer to buy. It is whether the excitation system and the governor are set up for the load. A stabilizer downstream of a misbehaving generator hides the symptom on the load side while the machine keeps doing damage to itself.

Sizing: start from the load, not the generator

The single most common sizing mistake is to match the stabilizer to the generator's kVA rating. A 100 kVA generator supplying a 25 kVA load needs roughly 30 kVA of stabilization, not 100. Oversizing is not free: a servo unit running at a small fraction of its rating spends its life at the bottom of its travel, and a relay unit steps in coarser increments than the load needs.

Work through the load instead:

StepWhat to establishWhere it goes wrong
1. List the loadsEvery load that must ride through a generator start: motors, drives, resistive heating, electronics, lightingUsing the design load instead of the load that will actually run on generator
2. Sum running kVARunning kVA at the load power factor, with a realistic diversity factor for the siteAdding nameplate kVA of every load as if all run together at full load
3. Add starting allowanceThe largest motor's starting current for the seconds it lasts, and whether starts overlapIgnoring inrush, then discovering the stabilizer limits on motor start
4. Set the input windowThe lowest and highest voltage the generator will present: sag at full load, rise at light loadSpecifying a narrow window (for example ±15%) and losing the load every time a big motor starts

6. Confirm output accuracyThe band the load actually tolerates, not the tightest number availablePaying for ±1% where ±3% would do

Two numbers do most of the work: the largest motor's starting current and the generator's voltage at light load. Get those from the site, not from the datasheet of a similar installation.

Relay or servo: which one suits a generator supply

Relay typeServo type (motor-driven variac)

Best forLighting, heating, small single-phase loads, cost-sensitive jobsMotors, drives, CNC, medical, printing, whole plant feeders

On a generator, fluctuation is rarely gentle. Load steps arrive in jumps, so a relay unit will step repeatedly during a shift, and each step is a voltage transient the load sees. Where the load is electronic or motor-driven, the servo type is the safer choice and the reason most generator-backed industrial installations use it.

Electronic stabilizers using SCR or IGBT switching also exist and correct faster than either type above. They are a different product family from the units described here, and they are worth asking about specifically if the load cannot tolerate even a short correction interval.

What a voltage stabilizer will not fix

  • Frequency. If the governor lets frequency wander, motors run at the wrong speed and drives see the wrong volts-per-hertz. Voltage correction does not touch this.
  • Waveform and harmonics. A generator feeding rectifier or inverter loads produces a distorted waveform. A stabilizer corrects magnitude, not shape.
  • Phase imbalance. Single-phase loads spread unevenly across a three-phase generator produce negative-sequence voltage. The fix is load distribution, not a stabilizer.
  • Capacity. No stabilizer makes a small generator start a large motor. If the set cannot supply the starting current, the answer is a bigger set, a soft starter, or a different starting method.
  • Earthing and neutral integrity. A lost neutral or a poor earth produces voltage problems that a stabilizer will faithfully pass through, or make worse by correcting the wrong quantity.

Specification checklist

Take these to a supplier and the conversation becomes short:

  • Source details — generator kVA, voltage, phases, frequency, and whether the stabilizer sits before or after the changeover switch.
  • Load list — running kVA, largest motor and its starting method, and any load that must not see a step.
  • Input window — the lowest and highest supply voltage the unit must accept before it limits or bypasses.
  • Output accuracy — expressed as a band at nominal voltage, not as a marketing claim.
  • Environment — ambient temperature, altitude, dust, and whether the enclosure is indoor, outdoor or in a container.
  • Neutral and earthing regime — TN-S, TN-C-S, TT or IT, and whether the stabilizer must switch the neutral.
  • Maintenance access — a manual bypass so the load can run while brushes or contacts are serviced.
  • Signalling — a volt-free contact for “on bypass” or “out of range” wired to the plant alarm, not just a front-panel lamp.

Frequently asked questions

Can I run a voltage stabilizer from a generator?

Yes. A load-side stabilizer works from a generator supply provided its input window covers the generator's voltage at both light load and full load, and its rating covers the starting current of the largest motor. Size it from the load list, not from the generator's kVA rating.

Is a generator's AVR the same as a voltage stabilizer?

No. The excitation AVR is inside the alternator and regulates field current to hold the machine's terminal voltage. A voltage stabilizer is a separate load-side device that corrects the voltage delivered to equipment. One does not replace the other, and a stabilizer cannot compensate for an alternator that is regulating badly.

Why do my lights dim when a pump starts on the generator?

Because the starting current of the pump flows through the generator's source impedance and pulls the terminal voltage down for a second or two. A stabilizer sized to pass that current will hold the voltage for the rest of the installation; one sized only for running load will limit or trip instead.

What size stabilizer for a 10 kVA generator?

It depends on the load, not the generator. If the generator runs a 4 kVA load with one 1.5 kW motor, the stabilizer is sized on that load plus the motor's starting current and 20–30% headroom. Matching the stabilizer to 10 kVA would oversize it and, on a servo unit, waste its resolution.

Can a stabilizer fix unstable generator frequency?

No. Frequency is set by the prime mover and its governor. A stabilizer corrects voltage magnitude only, so a frequency problem has to be solved at the engine and governor, not at the stabilizer.
