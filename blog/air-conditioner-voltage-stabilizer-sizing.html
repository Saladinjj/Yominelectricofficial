Voltage Stabilizer for Air Conditioners: Sizing, Types and Installation

ET
ET Engineering Team October 23, 2026 10 min read

What a stabilizer actually protects

An air conditioner compressor is an induction motor that expects a stable supply. When line voltage drops, the motor draws more current for the same output, windings run hotter, and the internal overload eventually opens. When voltage rises, control boards and capacitors see stresses they were not designed for. In both directions the failure is rarely immediate — it shows up as a compressor that trips on hot afternoons, then as a replacement.

A voltage stabilizer sits between the wall supply and the air conditioner and holds the output within a working band while the input moves. It does not replace surge protection, and it does not fix a supply that is simply too weak to start the compressor. It buys the motor a stable voltage so that the current stays where the designer intended.

The mistake buyers make is choosing by horsepower alone. A 1.5 HP unit in a hot climate with a long cable run and 160 V evenings needs a different stabilizer from the same unit on a stiff urban supply.

Sizing in four numbers

Everything you need is on the unit nameplate and a multimeter:

  • Running current (A): the rated current on the nameplate, not the cooling capacity in BTU.
  • Supply voltage (V): single-phase 220–240 V for most domestic splits, three-phase for larger cassette and ducted systems.
  • Starting current: compressors draw several times running current for a second or two at start-up. A stabilizer that is only marginally larger than the running load will trip or sag on every compressor start.
  • Worst-case input voltage: measure at the socket during the evening peak, not at midday. The stabilizer must cover the lowest voltage your area actually delivers, not the nominal value.

A practical method: required capacity (VA) = running current × nominal voltage × a starting margin. A margin of 1.3 to 1.5 covers normal compressor starting and ageing; go higher where the supply is very weak or the cable run is long. Round up to the next standard size — never down.

Worked example: a single-phase split with a nameplate running current of 6 A at 230 V gives 6 × 230 = 1,380 VA; with a 1.4 margin the requirement is about 1,930 VA, so the next standard stabilizer size is chosen, not the 1,500 VA unit that looks “close enough”.

Relay type or servo type?

Both are automatic voltage regulators; they correct in different ways.

CharacteristicRelay (tap-switching) typeServo (motor-driven) type
CorrectionSteps between transformer tapsContinuous, by moving a carbon brush over a winding
Output qualitySmall step changes as taps switchSmooth output, better for sensitive electronics
Speed of correctionFast, stepwiseSmoother but mechanically slower on large swings
Input rangeSuited to moderate fluctuationBetter where the grid swings widely
Wear partsRelay contactsBrush and motor
Typical useDomestic splits, budget installationsLarge splits, clinics, shops, sensitive loads

For a house with two splits on a supply that moves between roughly 190 V and 240 V, a relay-type wall unit is usually the economical answer. For a clinic, a shop with refrigeration, or an area where voltage collapses in the evening, the servo type earns its price difference.

Installation: where it goes and what it needs

Position

Mount the stabilizer on a wall close to the air conditioner, in a ventilated position out of direct sun. It dissipates heat; a unit boxed into a closed cabinet will derate or trip. Keep at least the clearance stated in its manual around the ventilation slots, and mount it at a height where the display can be read and the breaker reached.

Wiring

Feed the stabilizer from a dedicated circuit with a breaker rated for the air conditioner, then take the output to the unit. Use a conductor size appropriate to the circuit current, connect the earth continuously, and do not use the stabilizer as a junction box for other loads. Every connection should be torqued and tug-tested; loose terminals at a stabilizer are a common cause of nuisance tripping.

Restart delay

Compressors must not be restarted immediately after stopping: the refrigerant pressures need time to equalise, and a short-cycling compressor overheats and fails. Many stabilizers include a restart delay of a few minutes for exactly this reason. Where the stabilizer does not provide one, the air conditioner’s own control board usually does — but check, because stacking two long delays makes the unit feel unresponsive.

Symptoms of an undersized or wrong unit

SymptomLikely cause
Trips every time the compressor startsCapacity below starting demand, or the input voltage is under the unit’s range
Output voltage still low under loadStabilizer at its correction limit; the supply is weaker than assumed
Audible buzzing and a warm caseOperation at the edge of its range or an overloaded unit
Display flickers between valuesUnstable input or a loose incoming connection
Works in winter, trips in summerThermal derating in a hot position, or demand above capacity at peak ambient

What to specify when you buy

For a distributor or contractor order, specify: phase, capacity in VA, input voltage range, output accuracy band, correction type, restart delay, mounting format (wall or floor), display, protection functions and the ambient temperature rating. Ask for the input range explicitly — capacity alone tells you nothing about whether the unit will hold output at 150 V.

A note on three-phase air conditioning

Cassette and ducted systems in shops, offices and clinics are often three-phase. A three-phase stabilizer must correct all three phases and protect against phase loss and phase sequence faults, because a compressor running on two phases will fail quickly. Specify the phase-failure behaviour at order time; it is not something to discover on commissioning.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size stabilizer do I need for a 1.5 HP air conditioner?

Size from the nameplate running current, not the horsepower. Multiply the running current by the nominal voltage, apply a 1.3 to 1.5 margin for compressor starting, and round up to the next standard capacity. A unit that sits exactly at the calculated figure will trip on hot days.

Is a relay-type stabilizer enough, or do I need a servo unit?

A relay type is adequate for domestic splits on a supply that fluctuates moderately. Choose servo where the voltage swings widely, where the load includes refrigeration or medical equipment, or where the output must be smooth rather than stepped.

Why does the stabilizer trip when the compressor starts?

Almost always because the unit is sized to running current only, ignoring the starting surge. It can also be the input voltage falling below the stabilizer range at that moment, or a loose incoming terminal. Check the nameplate, the measured voltage at start-up and the terminal torque in that order.

Can one stabilizer supply two air conditioners?

Only if its capacity covers the combined running load plus the starting surge of the largest unit, and only if both units are unlikely to start together. Separate units per air conditioner are easier to protect and to diagnose.
