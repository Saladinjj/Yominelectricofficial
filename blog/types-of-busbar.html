Types of Busbar: Rigid, Flexible, Laminated, Aluminum and Integrated Forms

ET
ET Engineering Team November 2, 2026 10 min read

A busbar type answers four questions: does the run move, is the joint bolted or welded, does the bar need insulation, and which metal carries the current at the weight and cost the project can accept. Plating, hole pattern and current density are detail added on top of those four.

What actually separates one busbar type from another

Copper or aluminum is the question buyers ask first and the least useful one to start with. Two bars of the same metal can be a rigid switchgear bus and a flexible battery link, and they are not interchangeable in either direction. The structural difference is the joint: a rigid bar transfers current through a bolted lap, while a laminated flexible stack transfers it through many thin foils that move with the connection.

The families below are therefore grouped by construction, and the material trade-off is dealt with inside them.

Rigid busbar: the fixed-installation default

A rigid busbar is a solid flat bar, drilled at the laps and bolted to insulators or stands. It is the right answer when nothing moves: switchgear internals, panel power distribution, fixed battery racks. Capacity comes from cross-section, and the practical constraints are the hole pattern, the bend radius and the clearance to earthed metal.

YOMIN supplies rigid flat bar in copper and aluminum, including insulated high-current aluminum bar for fixed installations, battery packs and switchgear (ym-0443) and flat rigid copper bus for new-energy battery power distribution (ym-0444). Current density, joint pressure and plating rules are set out in busbar design basics.

Flexible laminated busbar: where the joint has to move

A flexible busbar is not a solid bar bent by hand. It is a stack of thin copper foils, welded or pressed solid at each end so the terminals stay rigid while the middle flexes. That construction absorbs the vibration of a vehicle, the thermal expansion of a rack and the assembly tolerance that a drilled solid bar cannot.

The range covers nickel-plated copper links for LiFePO4 cells and EV modules (ym-0432, ym-0433) and stamped copper flexible links for lithium battery packs (ym-0442). Nickel plating is there for the weld and for corrosion resistance at the cell terminal, not for conductivity.

The trade-off is honest: for the same width a laminated stack carries less current than a solid bar, because the foils are thin and the stack is not fully dense. Where space is tight and the run is fixed, a rigid bar wins.

Aluminum and copper-aluminum composite

Aluminum is chosen for weight and cost, and it brings two constraints: lower conductivity per unit of cross-section, and a terminal that needs proper preparation to avoid galvanic problems against copper. A bimetallic joint or a plated finish is not optional detail.

The aluminum range includes insulated aluminum bus for energy-storage batteries (ym-0435) and drilled DC copper bus supplied in aluminum to drawing (ym-0436), both positioned for ESS, solar arrays and lightweight power links. Where the design needs both metals, the composite laminated range bonds copper and aluminum into a single laminate for rail and high-voltage distribution (ym-0486, ym-0507).

CCS integrated busbar

A CCS, or cell contact system, is the point where a busbar stops being a conductor and becomes an assembly. The bar is bonded to an insulating film, the voltage and temperature sensing circuit is laminated in, and the whole part is fixture-aligned to the module. It removes a separate harness, a separate insulator and a set of manual connections from the line.

The catalogue carries PET-film integrated units for portable and mobile storage (ym-0487), FPC-based CCS schemes with an ABS cover for industrial storage (ym-0577) and integrated busbar wire harnesses for vehicle packs (ym-0630). Because the sensing geometry is part-specific, these are drawing-driven items rather than catalogue picks.

Energy-storage busbars and connectors

Container-scale storage has its own family: short, high-current links terminated in a connector that must be rated for the whole string. The bar is the easy part; the connector is the qualified component.

The range includes DC single-pole battery power connectors rated 120 to 500 A at 1500 V (ym-0434), screw-bolt connectors for high-current storage systems (ym-0440) and stackable 50/60/70 A connectors with 3+10 pins for storage and motor-drive interfaces (ym-0451). In this family the connector rating, not the bar cross-section, sets the limit of the link.

Insulation and support decide the voltage ceiling

An uninsulated bar is limited by clearance and creepage to earthed metal; an insulated bar is limited by the dielectric of its covering. Heat-shrink gives mechanical protection and a modest dielectric margin, film and epoxy give more, and standoff insulators set the physical distance that the clearance calculation assumes.

Support hardware belongs to the same decision. The EL-3M busbar with EL-600 insulators (ym-0316) is rated 10 kV, and it exists precisely because the insulator, not the bar, defines what the assembly can withstand.

Five questions that pick the type

  • Does the joint move? Vibration or thermal cycling points to a flexible laminate; a fixed run points to rigid bar.
  • Is the limit weight or current? Aluminum for weight, copper where cross-section is the constraint.
  • Is the part a conductor or an assembly? If sensing and insulation are laminated in, it is a CCS and needs a drawing.
  • What voltage must the covering withstand? The insulation class, not the bar thickness, sets this.
  • Who terminates it? A connector-rated link is only as good as the connector specified with it.

Frequently asked questions

Is a flexible busbar just a bent solid bar?

No. A flexible busbar is a stack of thin foils joined solid at the ends. Bending a solid bar work-hardens it and reduces the section at the bend, while a laminate is designed to flex repeatedly without that loss.

Can aluminum busbar replace copper directly?

Only with the cross-section increased for conductivity and the joint designed for the metal pair. A direct swap at the same dimensions loses capacity and creates a galvanic joint at the terminal.

What is the difference between laminated and composite busbar?

Laminated describes the construction: thin layers bonded together. Composite specifically means copper and aluminum bonded into one laminate, so composite is a subset of laminated construction.

Do I need a drawing to order a CCS?

Yes. Cell pitch, sensing routing, film outline and fixture datums are part-specific, so a CCS is quoted from a drawing rather than chosen from a list.

Sourcing busbar to a drawing or a current rating?

Send the current, the voltage class, whether the joint moves and the terminal style. We will come back with the bar construction that fits, in copper, aluminum or a copper-aluminum laminate.
