Plastic Security Seals: Body, Locking Insert and Tamper Evidence

ET
ET Engineering Team November 13, 2026 8 min read

A plastic security seal is a single-use fastener with a one-way locking mechanism: a moulded plastic body closes onto a wire, strap or hasp, and the only way to open it again is to break it. That is the whole product. There is no key, no combination, and no way to reclose it invisibly — which is precisely why utilities, logistics operators and tanker fleets still specify them instead of padlocks.

The buying decision, however, is rarely about the body. Two seals that look identical on a sample board can sit at opposite ends of the security range, because the part that decides whether a seal can be defeated without leaving evidence is the locking insert hidden inside the head. This guide covers what actually separates a good plastic seal from a decorative one, and which questions to put to a supplier before you order a few hundred thousand of them.

Where plastic seals sit among seal families

The word “seal” covers four products that are sold to the same buyers but do different jobs. Mixing them up is the most common specification error in a metering or cargo project.

FamilyBody and lockingRemoval evidenceTypical duty
Plastic seal (wire or strap)Moulded PP or nylon body, one-way insert, integral wire or strapBody must be cut or the insert torn outMeter covers, terminal enclosures, light cargo, tanker valves
Cable sealPlastic or metal head on a steel cable of fixed diameterCable cut; head cannot be reopenedContainer doors, trailers, rail wagons
Bolt sealSteel bolt through a plastic or metal bush, snap-fit lockBolt must be cut with a toolISO containers, high-value and cross-border cargo
Padlock sealMoulded body with a metal shackle, keyed or one-wayDepends on the locking typeReusable access points, cash bags

Plastic seals win on unit cost, weight and the fact that a serial number can be printed directly onto the body. They lose on tensile strength and on resistance to a determined attack. Deciding between a plastic seal and a bolt seal is a risk decision, not a price decision, and it should be made before the purchase order rather than after the first loss.

The locking insert decides the security level

Inside the head of every plastic seal there is a one-way mechanism. In the inexpensive build it is a moulded plastic pawl that engages a ratchet on the wire or strap. In the security build it is a small metal spring insert — the two bright steel leaves visible through the window on a meter seal are exactly this part.

  • Plastic pawl. Adequate against casual interference. It can sometimes be released by inserting a shim beside the wire and backing the pawl off, and it may not show a clear witness mark afterwards.
  • Metal spring insert. The wire is gripped by hardened steel leaves that bite in one direction only. Attempts to withdraw the wire deform it, and the deformation stays visible after the seal is reinserted.

This is the single specification point worth arguing about. A thicker body, a brighter colour or a longer wire does not change how the seal behaves under attack; the insert does. Ask for the insert material and for the failure mode in writing, and treat “high security” in a product title as a marketing word until the insert is described.

Body material: polypropylene, nylon and sunlight

Most plastic seals are moulded in polypropylene or in a nylon grade. Polypropylene is cheaper, moulds cleanly and is stiff enough to protect the insert; it is also the material that suffers most when a seal spends years on a pole-mounted metering cabinet.

UV exposure is the failure mode that surprises buyers. An unstabilised body chalky after two summers will crack along the head when it is twisted, and a seal that breaks in service is worse than no seal: it forces a re-seal visit and destroys the audit trail for that meter. Three questions cover it:

  • Is the grade UV-stabilised, and is that stated on the datasheet rather than in conversation?
  • What is the service temperature range, including the cold end where polypropylene becomes brittle?
  • Does the colour fade matter to the customer, or is the colour part of the tamper scheme?

For indoor terminal enclosures and short-life cargo, none of this is critical. For outdoor metering, it is the difference between a five-year seal and a one-year seal.

Wire, strap or twist fit

The tail has to match the hardware it passes through, and the three common formats are not interchangeable.

FormatHow it closesBest fitWhat to measure before ordering
Wire-lead sealWire loop through the hasp, head pushed on and lockedMeter covers, small enclosures, instrument casesHasp hole diameter, required loop length, wire gauge
Pull-tight strapSerrated strap pulled through the headBags, valves, light cartons, truck curtainsStrap width and length, break strength required
Twist-tite wire sealWire twisted and captured in the head, then lockedMeter terminals where a short, tight loop is neededWire length, terminal screw spacing, access angle

Order the loop length from the hardware, not from habit. A wire that is too short cannot be fitted without leaving the terminal exposed, and a wire that is too long can be looped twice and slipped off. Measuring one installed meter and one spare is usually enough to fix the specification for a whole fleet.

Marking, barcodes and serial control

A seal without a unique identity is only a lock. The value comes from being able to reconcile the number on the seal against the number recorded at installation.

  • Laser marking gives a permanent, high-contrast serial and a scannable barcode on the same face, and does not wear off in service.
  • Hot-stamp or pad printing is the lower-cost route and is fine for a logo, but a printed serial can be abraded on a seal that rubs against a cabinet door.
  • Sequential ranges should be reserved per site or per crew, so a missing number is immediately visible in the reconciliation log.

If the seals will be scanned, ask for a barcode symbology and a print area drawing before production, and test one printed sample with the actual handheld scanner. Barcode quality problems are almost always a print-size decision, and they are cheap to fix before a run and expensive to fix afterwards.

When a plastic seal is the wrong choice

If the consignment value or the regulatory exposure is high, a plastic seal is the wrong product. ISO container doors, cross-border trailers and high-value electronics shipments call for a bolt seal that cannot be removed without a cutting tool, and customs regimes in many markets expect that standard. A plastic seal on a container door is not a cheaper version of the same protection; it is a different, lower class of protection, and it will be argued about after a claim rather than before one.

Where the requirement is revenue protection on a metering point rather than cargo security, the plastic seal is usually the right call: the attacker is a consumer with limited tools, the evidence requirement is a visible break, and the unit cost has to stay low enough to seal every point in the network.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a plastic security seal and a cable seal?

A plastic seal closes with an integral wire or moulded strap and is removed by cutting the body or the tail. A cable seal uses a steel cable of fixed diameter through a locking head, so it resists cutting far better and is used on container doors and trailers where a plastic tail would be too easy to sever.

Can a plastic security seal be reopened without showing it?

With a moulded plastic pawl, sometimes yes: a shim beside the wire can release the pawl and leave little evidence. With a metal spring insert the wire is gripped by hardened leaves that deform it on any attempt to pull it back, so the seal shows tampering. That insert is the part to specify.

How long does a plastic seal last outdoors?

It depends almost entirely on the UV stabilisation of the moulding grade and the local sunlight dose, not on the wall thickness. Ask for a UV-stabilised grade with a stated service temperature range, and expect an unstabilised polypropylene body to chalk and crack within a couple of summers.

Should the serial number be laser marked or printed?

Laser marking is permanent and survives abrasion, which matters on a seal that rubs against a cabinet door. Printed marking is cheaper and adequate for a logo, but a printed serial can wear off exactly when you need to reconcile it.
