Fuse Time-Current Curve: Reading gG, aM and gPV Before You Order

ET
ET Engineering Team November 16, 2026 11 min read

A fuse time-current curve is the only document that tells you how a fuse will actually behave. The rated current printed on the barrel is a single number that says almost nothing about the two things that decide whether an installation works: how long the fuse takes to open at a given overload, and how much fault energy it lets through before it does.

The practical answer is that you read the curve in three separate bands, and you must know which band your protection decision lives in. Cable protection lives in the overload band. Motor starting lives in the transition band, where a fuse must not open. Short-circuit protection lives in the steep band at the right, where the fuse must open faster than the cable is damaged. A fuse selected on rated current alone usually gets one of those three right and the other two wrong.

What the curve actually plots

Both axes are logarithmic. The horizontal axis is prospective current in amperes, spanning roughly a decade either side of the rated current. The vertical axis is time in seconds, running from about ten thousand seconds down to a few milliseconds. Because both axes are logarithmic, a straight line on the page is a constant I²t relationship, which is why curves look like a shallow S rather than a smooth slope.

Two curves are normally plotted for the same fuse. The lower one is the pre-arcing, or melting, time: the moment the element melts. The upper one is the total clearing time: the moment the arc is finally extinguished and the circuit is genuinely dead. The vertical gap between them is the arcing time, and on a low fault current that gap can be a meaningful fraction of the total.

The area between the two curves is the band of legitimate manufacturing spread. A fuse is not a precision instrument, so the standard allows tolerance on both the current and the time axis. Reading the curve means reading the band, not a single hairline.

The three bands you have to read separately

Every fuse curve divides into the same three regions, and the boundaries matter more than the shape.

BandWhat the fuse is doingWhat you check there
Overload, long timesElement heating slowly, no arc yetThat the cable is protected, and that normal load does not sit inside the fusing band
Transition, seconds to minutesMelting times fall steeply as current risesMotor inrush and transformer energisation, which must pass without opening
Short circuit, millisecondsCurrent-limited operation, element vaporisesTotal clearing I²t against the cable withstand, and the breaking capacity

Most misapplied fuses are correct in the short-circuit band and wrong somewhere else. A fuse that clears a fault beautifully but opens on every compressor start is a fuse chosen by looking only at the right-hand end of the graph.

gG, aM and gPV are not interchangeable

The first letter of an IEC fuse class describes what the fuse is permitted to break; the second describes what it is intended to protect. In low-voltage distribution the three families below account for nearly every enquiry, and they differ in a way that no amount of derating can fix.

ClassProtectsOverload dutyTypical fit
gGCables and general circuitsFull-range, overload and short circuitDistribution boards, feeder and final circuits
aMMotor circuits, short circuit onlyNone by design, overload is left to the overload relayMotor branch, paired with a thermal or electronic overload
gPVPhotovoltaic strings and arraysFull-range, at the DC rating of the arrayString and array protection, DC combiner boxes

An aM fuse is deliberately not an overload device. Its curve is shifted so that motor starting current passes easily, which means a stalled motor is protected by the overload relay and not by the fuse. Fitting an aM fuse where a gG is required removes overload protection from the cable entirely, and no inspection will catch it because the barrel fits and the circuit works.

gPV is a separate case again. Photovoltaic source circuits have a defined maximum current close to their operating current, and the fuse has to survive that current continuously at elevated enclosure temperature while still opening on a reverse or fault current. A gG fuse of the same nominal rating is not a substitute, because its curve and its DC behaviour were never specified for that duty.

Melting time is not clearing time

When someone says a fuse opens in five milliseconds, they are quoting one of two different numbers. Melting time is when the element breaks. Clearing time is when the current actually stops, which includes the arcing period after the element has vaporised. On high fault currents the arc is quenched quickly by the filler and the two numbers are close. On lower fault currents they diverge, and the divergence is exactly where cable damage calculations are decided.

This matters because the published I²t figures come in the same two flavours. Pre-arcing I²t describes the element alone; total clearing I²t describes the whole event including the arc. Any coordination study that mixes the two is comparing quantities that are not the same thing, and it will usually overstate how well the cable is protected.

Checking the curve against the cable

Cable protection is an energy comparison, not a current comparison. The adiabatic withstand of a conductor is k²S², where k is a material constant and S is the cross-section in square millimetres. The fuse passes I²t. The check is simply that the total clearing I²t at the prospective fault current stays below k²S² for that conductor.

Two consequences follow that surprise buyers. First, a larger fuse is not automatically worse for the cable at high fault levels, because current limiting cuts the let-through energy. Second, a smaller fuse is not automatically safer, because it may sit inside the overload band of normal load and open in service. The curve is the only place both effects can be seen together.

Where the installation has a long cable run, the prospective fault current at the far end is much lower than at the board. The fuse then operates in its transition band rather than its current-limiting band, clearing times lengthen, and the I²t let through rises sharply. This is the single most common reason a cable that looked protected in the panel schedule is not protected at the end of the run.

Why fuses nuisance-trip on motor and transformer circuits

Five causes account for almost every case we are asked about.

  • A gG fuse on a motor branch. The starting current passes through the transition band for longer than the curve allows. The correct answer is aM, or a larger gG with the cable re-checked.
  • Transformer energisation inrush. Switching a transformer draws a decaying inrush well above rated current for a few cycles to a few seconds. A fuse sized to the transformer rating sees that as a fault.
  • Enclosure derating ignored. A fuse inside a sealed box runs hotter than the curve assumes, so it opens earlier than the published line. Dense banks of fuses side by side make this worse.
  • Harmonic content. Nonlinear loads add high-frequency current that heats the element without contributing to the fundamental reading on a clamp meter.
  • Repeated overload cycling. A fuse that has run hot for months has a shortened life, and the eventual failure looks spontaneous.

Direct current changes the curve

A DC arc does not cross zero, so it does not extinguish itself the way an AC arc does at each half cycle. A fuse intended for DC duty is built and tested for that, with a DC voltage rating that is usually lower than its AC rating and a specified time constant for the test circuit. Putting an AC-rated fuse on a photovoltaic string is not a derating question; it is a device that has never been qualified to interrupt the fault it will see.

Checks before you send an RFQ

  • System voltage and frequency, and for DC the array voltage and expected time constant.
  • Prospective fault current at the point of installation, so the breaking capacity can be verified rather than assumed.
  • The load type, because it decides whether you need overload duty at all. Motor branch means aM plus an overload relay, not a bigger gG.
  • Cable size and material, so the total clearing I²t can be checked against k²S².
  • Ambient and enclosure, since a sealed box changes the effective rating of every fuse inside it.
  • The class marking you need, written as gG, aM or gPV on the order. Suppliers will otherwise default to whichever barrel is in stock.

Send the curve reference you are working to as well, not only the rated current. Two suppliers quoting the same amperage against different classes is the most expensive kind of price comparison.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a fuse time-current curve and its rated current?

The rated current is one point on the curve, nothing more. The curve gives the melting and clearing time across the whole current range, which is what decides whether the fuse holds on motor starting and whether it clears a fault before the cable is damaged.

Can I use a gG fuse instead of an aM fuse on a motor?

No. An aM fuse is deliberately built without overload protection so that starting current passes; a gG fuse carries overload duty and will open on a long or repeated start. Replacing aM with gG either trips the motor or forces an oversized fuse that no longer protects the cable.

Why does a fuse open faster than the curve suggests?

Because the published curve assumes defined reference conditions. A fuse in a sealed enclosure, in a hot panel, or packed tightly against other fuses runs above the reference ambient and opens earlier. Harmonic current has the same effect without appearing on a fundamental-frequency measurement.

Is an AC fuse acceptable on a photovoltaic DC circuit?

No. A DC arc does not self-extinguish at a zero crossing, so a fuse used on a PV string must be rated and tested for DC duty at the array voltage, with gPV characteristics. An AC-rated fuse has never been qualified to interrupt that fault.
