Grounding Terminal Block: The PE Path From Conductor to Rail

ET
ET Engineering Team November 15, 2026 9 min read

A grounding terminal block is not a green version of a feed-through terminal. Its mounting foot is metal and clamps directly onto the DIN rail, so the rail itself becomes the protective earth bus for the enclosure. That single design decision is what makes the block useful, and it is also what makes it easy to install in a way that looks correct, passes a visual inspection, and still leaves a high-resistance path between a metal enclosure and the earth bar.

This guide covers what separates a protective conductor terminal from the rest of the rail, how the fault path is actually completed, and the handful of checks that catch the mistakes most often found during commissioning.

What makes a grounding block different from a feed-through block

Put a standard feed-through terminal and a PE terminal side by side and the differences are all at the bottom of the moulding.

  • The foot is conductive and spring-loaded. A feed-through block sits on an insulating foot or carries an insulating tab precisely so that it does not connect to the rail. A PE block does the opposite: its metal foot bites into the rail and creates a permanent low-resistance contact.
  • The current path is short and wide. In a feed-through block the conductor path runs terminal to terminal. In a PE block it runs conductor to rail, and the cross-section of that path has to carry fault current, not just signal current.
  • The marking is mandatory. Green and yellow is reserved for protective conductors. Using that colour on a neutral or a live terminal is a defect, not a preference.
  • The product standard is different. Protective conductor terminal blocks are covered by their own part of the terminal block standard rather than the general requirements. Our IEC 60947-7-1 explainer covers the general family; the PE variant adds the rail-contact and current-carrying requirements on top.

The rail is the PE bus — treat it like one

The moment you install a PE terminal, the DIN rail stops being a mounting accessory and becomes part of the earthing system. Three consequences follow immediately.

First, the rail must be continuously bonded along its whole length. A rail made up of two sections joined by a plastic splice, or two rails in adjacent enclosure sections with no bonding jumper between them, gives you two separate earth systems that happen to share a colour code.

Second, the rail must be bonded to the enclosure and to the main earth bar. If the rail is mounted on plastic stand-offs, is painted, or sits on anodised aluminium, the contact is not there however tight the screws feel.

Third, the rail must be sized for fault current, not for the weight of the terminals on it. Thin rail in a long row of PE terminals is a common weak point in otherwise well-built panels.

The comparison explains a confusion that shows up in nearly every panel review: a green feed-through block looks like a PE terminal and is wired like one, but it does not touch the rail, so every conductor landed on it is floating with respect to earth until someone adds a separate bond.

Contact resistance and fault-loop impedance

A protective conductor is only as good as its worst joint. A PE terminal adds two joints to the path — conductor to clamp, and clamp to rail — and both are measurable.

  • Measure, do not assume. A low-resistance ohmmeter between the incoming protective conductor and the enclosure metalwork will show whether the path is real. A multimeter on the resistance range will not resolve it usefully.
  • Check the rail joint first. On a suspect installation the highest reading is almost always at a rail splice or at the point where the rail is fixed to a painted panel.
  • Clean the contact area. Paint, powder coat, anodising and oxidation all insulate. Masking the rail contact area before painting is cheaper than discovering the problem at commissioning.
  • Use the correct torque. An under-torqued clamp heats under fault current; an over-torqued one damages the thread and the connection loosens later. Follow the manufacturer's table rather than feel.

Loop impedance testing then confirms the whole path from the supply through the fault and back. That test cannot compensate for a PE terminal that never touched the rail, which is why the two checks belong together.

The adiabatic check on the protective conductor

Protective conductors are sized by fault energy rather than by load current, because they carry current only while a fault is being cleared. The standard relationship is the adiabatic equation:

S = √(I²t) ÷ k

where S is the required cross-sectional area, I²t is the prospective fault energy let through by the protective device, and k is a coefficient that depends on conductor material and insulation. The tabulated values live in the installation standard, not on a terminal datasheet, so the sizing decision belongs to the panel designer. What the terminal has to do is not become the bottleneck: a PE terminal rated for a smaller conductor than the protective conductor it is asked to carry is a specification error even if it fits.

Colour, marking and the PEN trap

Green and yellow means protective conductor, and nothing else. Two related rules catch most of the remaining mistakes.

A combined protective and neutral conductor — a PEN — is not the same thing as a PE. It carries operating current, so it has minimum cross-section requirements that a small PE terminal cannot satisfy, and it is not permitted on the load side of the point where the installation separates into PE and N. Terminating a PEN on a green-yellow terminal is a wiring error regardless of how the panel is labelled.

One protective conductor per terminal is the default. Doubling two conductors into one PE clamp is only acceptable where the terminal is explicitly designed and rated for it, and the second conductor still has to be independently secure.

Installation faults that fail an inspection

  • Green feed-through blocks used as PE. The most common single defect, and invisible once the cover is on.
  • Rail not bonded to the enclosure. Every PE terminal on the rail is then decorative.
  • PE row broken by an insulating end stop or a plastic spacer placed to line up the row neatly.
  • Mixed metals without thought. Copper conductor in an aluminium terminal, or an aluminium rail under humid conditions, needs a joint that accounts for the galvanic couple rather than one that merely tightens.
  • Neutral and earth bars bonded together downstream of the separation point, which creates parallel return paths and unwanted residual current.
  • No spare PE terminals. A row built exactly to the current drawing has nowhere to land the next cable, and the next cable always arrives.

What to put on the RFQ

  • Conductor range in mm², including the largest protective conductor the row must accept.
  • Rail type — 35 mm top-hat or another profile — and whether the rail is steel, aluminium or copper.
  • Number of PE ways plus the spare allowance your standard requires.
  • Fault current and clearing time the row must survive, if it has been calculated.
  • Colour and marking, and whether labelling or printing is needed.
  • Environment — indoor panel, humid plant room, or outdoor enclosure with condensation.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use a green feed-through terminal block as a grounding terminal?

No, not on its own. A feed-through block is insulated from the DIN rail, so the conductor landed on it is not connected to earth. Either use a protective conductor terminal with a metal foot, or add an explicit bond from the terminal to the earth bar.

Does the DIN rail itself count as the protective earth conductor?

It can serve as the PE bus inside an enclosure, but only if the rail is continuously bonded along its length and bonded to the enclosure and the main earth bar. A rail on plastic stand-offs or a painted rail is a mounting surface, not an earth path.

How do I check that a grounding terminal is actually connected?

Use a low-resistance ohmmeter between the incoming protective conductor and clean metalwork on the enclosure, and repeat the measurement across rail joints. A conventional multimeter does not have the resolution to prove a low-resistance bond.

Can two protective conductors share one grounding terminal?

Only where the terminal is designed and rated for two conductors. In the default case each protective conductor gets its own terminal, because a shared clamp gives no independent assurance that both conductors are secure.

Building a PE row for a panel?

Send the conductor range, rail profile, number of ways and the environment. We will confirm which terminal body and bar arrangement fits the row instead of leaving you to guess at the rail contact.
