Three Phase Current Transformer: How Three CTs Meter a 3-Phase Supply

ET
ET Engineering Team November 24, 2026 10 min read

A three phase current transformer is not one device. It is a set of transformers — almost always three, occasionally two — one per conductor, feeding a meter that sums them into a single three-phase reading. The transformers may be three separate low voltage units clamped around cables, or one cast body containing three cores for medium voltage switchgear, but the measuring principle is identical: each phase is scaled down, and the meter reconstructs the total.

Because the answer is a sum, the failure modes are different from a single-phase installation. One reversed unit, one mismatched ratio, or one loose secondary turns a correct total into a plausible-looking wrong one. This guide covers the arrangements, the matching rules and the commissioning checks that keep the number defensible.

Why one transformer per phase

A current transformer measures the current in the conductor that passes through it, and nothing else. On a three-phase circuit the three conductor currents are not equal, so a single CT on one phase reports one third of the story. Put it on the phase that happens to carry the lightest load and the meter will under-read the whole installation while looking perfectly healthy.

The meter, for its part, needs all three currents simultaneously to compute power correctly. It multiplies each phase current by its own phase voltage and adds the three results, which is why the CT set and the voltage inputs have to be matched phase to phase, not merely present.

The two-element arrangement works because in a three-wire system the three currents sum to zero, so the third can be derived. It is a legitimate saving on a motor feeder and a bad idea on a four-wire board with single-phase loads, where the neutral current carries information the meter needs and the derived third phase is simply wrong.

Matched ratios are not optional

Three CTs on one meter must share the same ratio and the same class. The meter applies one ratio setting to all three inputs, so a 400/5 A unit fitted alongside two 200/5 A units does not produce a small error — it produces a total that is wrong by a factor on one phase and impossible to reconcile afterwards.

The same rule applies to class. Mixing a Class 0.5 with Class 1.0 units in one set means the total inherits the worst member. Where a set is extended later, replace all three or accept that the installation is now a Class 1.0 system with a Class 0.5 label on two of the boxes.

Ratio selection follows the heaviest phase, not the average. On a board with single-phase loads the phases can differ substantially, and a ratio chosen from the arithmetic mean will saturate the loaded phase while the other two read comfortably.

Polarity and phase identity

Every unit in the set is wired the same way: P1 towards the supply, P2 towards the load, S1 to the meter's I+ terminal and S2 to I−. When one unit is reversed, its phase current arrives at the meter inverted. On a lightly loaded, near-balanced circuit the total then looks almost right, which is exactly why the fault survives commissioning and is found months later during a billing query.

Two practical defences. First, keep the secondary cores identified at both ends with the phase letter, not with a colour convention that nobody wrote down. Second, after wiring, compare each phase current against a clamp meter at the same moment — if one phase disagrees in sign or magnitude, stop and fix it before the meter is sealed.

On a low voltage board the three separate units are almost always the right answer, because they can be added to an existing panel without cutting cables. On medium voltage switchgear the single cast body wins, because the phase spacing is a dielectric requirement rather than a convenience and the primary bar has to be insulated for the system voltage.

Ordering the wrong one of those two is not a specification detail, it is a different installation.

Commissioning checks for a three-phase set

  • Ratio and class on all units — read the labels, do not trust the packing list. One substituted unit invalidates the whole set.
  • Consistent orientation — P1 to supply on every phase. Photograph the three before closing the panel.
  • Secondary continuity and polarity — test each core to the meter terminal, then compare phase currents against a clamp meter under load.
  • Single earth point — one earth on the secondary star point, not three scattered through the panel.
  • Shorting terminals fitted — so the next person can work on the meter without opening a live secondary.
  • Neutral CT where the tariff requires it — a four-wire board with single-phase loads and no neutral measurement will under-report.

What a wrong set looks like from the data

Three symptoms cover most field faults. A total that is roughly two thirds of the clamp-meter sum usually means one phase is missing or one CT secondary is open. A total that is close but never quite reconciles, with one phase reading low under light load, usually means a split-core unit that is not fully closed. A phase that reads negative while the others read positive is a polarity reversal, and it is the easiest of the three to fix and the most expensive to leave.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use two CTs on a three-phase four-wire system?

No. Two-element metering depends on the three currents summing to zero, which is only true in a three-wire system. On a four-wire board with single-phase loads the neutral carries current, and a derived third phase will misstate both the total and the phase balance.

Do all three CTs have to be the same ratio?

Yes, when they feed one meter. The meter applies a single ratio setting across its inputs, so a mismatched unit produces an error that cannot be corrected afterwards without changing hardware. Ratio selection should follow the heaviest phase.

What happens if one CT is installed backwards?

That phase current arrives inverted, so the meter's total is wrong even though all three phases appear to be reading. On a near-balanced circuit the error can look small enough to pass commissioning, which is why a phase-by-phase comparison against a clamp meter is worth the ten minutes.

Is a three-phase cast resin CT the same as three separate CTs?

No. A cast resin three-phase unit is one body with three cores at a fixed phase spacing, built for medium voltage switchgear. Three separate low voltage CTs are independent units clamped around individual cables. They serve the same measuring purpose at completely different voltage levels and cannot be substituted for one another.

What causes a three-phase meter to under-read?

The common causes are a missing phase CT, an open secondary, a split-core unit that is not fully latched, an undersized ratio saturating on the loaded phase, or a burden that exceeds the CT rating on a long secondary run. Each shows a different pattern in the phase-by-phase data, which is why that data is worth capturing at commissioning.

Specifying a three-phase CT set?

Send the system voltage, the current per phase, the number of wires and the meter input. We will confirm the set: three matched units, or a single three-phase transformer where the switchgear calls for one body.
