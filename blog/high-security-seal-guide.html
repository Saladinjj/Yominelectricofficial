High Security Seals: Grading, Marking and Inspection

ET
ET Engineering Team November 5, 2026 11 min read

A high security seal is a single-use locking device built so that any attempt to open it either destroys the seal or leaves evidence that cannot be reversed. The defining property is not strength on its own. It is the combination of a one-way mechanism, a tamper-evident body, and a unique identifier that can be reconciled against a record.

That definition matters commercially, because "high security" is often bought as an adjective rather than a specification. Two seals that look identical in a photograph can behave completely differently when a door is forced, when a customs officer asks for the seal number, or when a claim is disputed six weeks later. This guide covers what the grade actually describes, how marking and numbering work as evidence, and the inspection routine that makes the seal worth paying for.

What "high security" is really describing

Seals sit on a spectrum defined by what they can survive and what they leave behind. At the low end, a seal is a marker: it shows that a closure was made, and it may not survive a determined attack cleanly. At the high end, a seal is designed so that the attack itself is recorded in the physical object.

Three properties separate the two ends of that spectrum:

  • Irreversibility. Once closed, the mechanism cannot be released. Removal requires cutting the pin or breaking the body.
  • Evidence retention. The body deforms, cracks or discolours in a way that a trained inspector can read, rather than simply coming apart.
  • Unique identity. Each seal carries a number that exists once in the world, and that number is recorded against a specific closure.

A seal can be heavy and still fail the third test. If the number is not recorded, the seal tells you that a door was opened but not which shipment, which day or which carrier was responsible.

Indicative seals and high security seals

The practical split buyers need is between a seal that indicates a closure and a seal that supports a claim. They are bought for different reasons and should not be substituted for each other to save a few cents per unit.

Primary purposeShows a closure was madeSupports an investigation or a claim
MechanismMay be released with a tool or by designOne-way; removal destroys the seal

Where a contract, an insurer or a customs procedure specifies a security grade, confirm the classification the supplier is actually claiming and ask for the supporting documentation. A photograph and a colour swatch are not a grade.

How a bolt seal closes a container

The container bolt seal is the workhorse of the high security category because it matches the hardware it protects. A steel pin passes through the aligned holes of the door locking bars, and a body containing a spring clip or lock ring captures the pin and holds it in the locked position.

Application is by hand: the pin is pushed home until the clip seats, and the seal is then self-sealed. There is no tool to lose and no torque setting to get wrong. Removal is by bolt cutters, which is exactly the point — the seal cannot be quietly undone.

The YEM-S038 uses a low carbon steel pin, zinc galvanised and wrapped in ABS plastic. The galvanising handles the corrosion question on a steel part that spends weeks at sea, and the ABS wrap gives the body the deformation behaviour that makes interference visible. The construction is single-use and tamper-evident by design.

Material, weather and colour

Two environmental factors decide whether a seal still reads correctly at the destination: moisture and handling. Moisture attacks the steel; handling attacks the body.

Zinc galvanising on the pin is the standard answer to moisture, and the ABS wrap keeps water and dust out of the space between the pin and the body. On a route that crosses several climates, that combination is what prevents a rusted seal being mistaken for a tampered one — a false positive that costs a real delay at the gate.

Colour is an operational control rather than decoration. The YEM-S038 is supplied in red, yellow, blue and white, with additional colours available. Consignments can be colour-coded by lane, by carrier or by month, so an inspector can see at a glance that the seal on the door belongs to this movement and not to the previous one.

Marking, numbering and the seal register

This is where most sealing programmes succeed or fail, and it has nothing to do with the seal's mechanical strength.

YOMIN marks the YEM-S038 by thermal stamping or laser printing. That covers a company name or logo, sequential numbers up to six digits, and a barcode. The marking is applied to the seal itself, so it travels with the physical evidence rather than in a separate document that can be lost.

The register is the other half. At minimum it records the number range issued, the date, the container or vehicle, the destination and the person who applied the seal. At destination, the receiving officer compares the number on the door with the number on the paperwork before breaking the seal. Without that comparison, the number is decoration.

Two failure modes are worth naming. A register kept in a spreadsheet that is not reconciled against physical stock will drift, and the drift is usually discovered during a dispute, which is the worst possible moment. A register with duplicate numbers is worse than no register, because it makes every seal on that range arguable.

Inspection at three points

A seal is only evidence if it is examined more than once. Treat the checks as three separate events with different questions.

StageCheckWhat it catches
On receipt from the supplierNumber sequence is unbroken; marking matches the approved proof; a sample seal locks and will not releaseProduction or packing errors before stock enters the system
At loadingNumber is recorded against the container and the document; seal is applied and photographedWrong seal on the wrong door; disputes about when a seal was fitted
At destinationNumber matches the paperwork; body and pin show no deformation, cutting or discolouration; seal is photographed before removalInterference in transit; false claims of tampering

Photograph the seal at loading and before removal. A timestamped image resolves most arguments without needing to establish who is telling the truth.

Specifying a high security seal

Send these six items and the quotation will be comparable with the next one you receive:

  • Application — container door, trailer, tanker hatch or bonded store, and the hasp hole diameter if it is unusual.
  • Grade required — the classification named in your contract or procedure, so the supplier can confirm or decline rather than guess.
  • Marking — logo or company name, sequential number, barcode, and the digit count of the sequence.
  • Colour — and whether colours must rotate by lane or by month.
  • Quantity and call-off pattern — annual volume and whether deliveries are split.
  • Documentation — what the receiving site will ask for at the gate.

Ask for a sample before the first production run and destroy it deliberately. Cut the pin, try to reseat it, and see what the body looks like afterwards. That single test tells you more about a seal than any datasheet.

The YEM-S038 container bolt seal

The YEM-S038 container bolt seal is a low carbon steel, zinc galvanised bolt seal wrapped in ABS plastic, marked by thermal stamping or laser printing with a logo, up to six sequential digits and a barcode. It is CE and RoHS certified, self-sealing by hand and removed with bolt cutters.

Frequently asked questions

What makes a seal high security rather than just a seal?

Three properties together: a one-way mechanism that cannot be released once closed, a body that visibly retains evidence of interference, and a unique number that is recorded against the specific closure. Strength alone is not the test. A heavy seal with no reconciled number cannot support a claim.

How is a container bolt seal removed?

With bolt cutters. The YEM-S038 is a single-use seal, so removal destroys it by design. That is the intended behaviour: a seal that could be opened and re-closed without trace would provide no evidence at all.

Why does sequential numbering matter?

Because the number is what ties a physical seal to a shipment, a date and a person. Without a recorded number, a broken seal proves that something happened but not when, where or to which consignment, which is not enough to support an insurance or contractual claim.

Can one seal range be used across several routes?

It can, but it is easier to control if number ranges or colours are allocated per route, carrier or month. Separate ranges let an inspector spot a seal from the wrong movement immediately, and they make a reconciliation error obvious rather than invisible.

What should I check when the seals arrive?

Check that the number sequence is unbroken across the cartons, that the marking matches the approved proof, and that a sample seal locks and will not release. Keep that test seal and its number as the reference for any later dispute.

Sourcing high security seals for container or utility use?

Send the application, required grade, marking and numbering format, and annual quantity. We will confirm the seal against your hasp and your documentation requirements.
