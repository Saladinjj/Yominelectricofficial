Distribution Boards & Voltage Protectors / Voltage Protection Devices

Distribution Boards: What Is an Over and Under Voltage Protector?

ET
ET Engineering Team October 22, 2026 10 min read

The Critical Challenge of Grid Voltage Instability in Distribution Systems

Electrical equipment and home appliances are engineered to operate within strict voltage tolerances, typically $\pm 10\%$ of nominal utility voltage (e.g., $207\, ext{V}$ to $253\, ext{V}$ for a standard $230\, ext{V}$ grid). Across rapid-growth industrial corridors, rural distribution feeders, and developing utility networks, supply voltages routinely violate these limits:

  • Voltage Swells and Overvoltage: Caused by sudden load shedding, capacitor bank switching, or distribution transformer tap mismatches. Sustained overvoltage forces excessive magnetizing current through iron cores, punctures dielectric insulation, and destroys power supply capacitors.
  • Voltage Sags and Brownouts: Caused by overloaded distribution feeders and high-current motor starts. When supply voltage drops, induction motors and refrigeration compressors draw excessive current to satisfy their shaft torque demands, leading to overheating and thermal burnout.
  • The Floating Neutral Catastrophe: When a shared utility three-phase neutral conductor is severed or disconnected, single-phase 230V residential sockets can suddenly see up to $400\, ext{V}$ phase-to-phase voltage, causing widespread appliance destruction and domestic fires.

The DIN rail self-restoring over and under voltage protector provides an automated, autonomous defense system installed directly inside the consumer distribution board.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an over and under voltage protector and how does it differ from a surge arrester (SPD)?

An over and under voltage protector (often designated as Model VAP or self-restoring voltage relay) is an active digital protection switch that continuously monitors RMS grid voltage and disconnects downstream power whenever the voltage climbs above a safe ceiling or plunges below a safe floor. <strong>Crucial difference from a Surge Protective Device (SPD):</strong> <ul><li><strong>Surge Protective Device (SPD / Type 2):</strong> Diverts microsecond-duration high-voltage transient spikes (such as indirect lightning strikes or utility switching transients of $1\, ext{kV}$ to $6\, ext{kV}$) to earth using metal oxide varistors (MOVs). SPDs cannot protect against sustained multi-second overvoltages or low-voltage brownouts.</li><li><strong>Over/Under Voltage Protector (VAP):</strong> Handles sustained power-frequency abnormal voltages (sags, swells, brownouts, and neutral line disconnection) lasting from tens of milliseconds to hours. When grid voltage deviates, it opens its internal contactor, completely isolating appliances until voltage normalizes.</li></ul>For complete consumer unit protection, engineering best practice places a Type 2 SPD in parallel, followed by a series-installed self-restoring voltage protector.

Why is the auto-reclosing recovery delay timer essential for air conditioners and refrigerators?

When utility grid power fluctuates or sags momentarily, power may bounce off and on within a few seconds. In refrigeration compressors, air conditioner heat pumps, and electric motors, the refrigerant gas remains under high head pressure immediately after shut-off. If voltage is reapplied instantly, the compressor motor attempts to start against this locked hydraulic pressure, drawing locked-rotor current ($I_{ ext{LRA}}$), tripping motor overloads, and burning out motor stator windings. The adjustable <strong>auto-reclosing timer delay ($1\, ext{s}$ to $500\, ext{s}$, typically set to $180\, ext{s}$)</strong> ensures the protector waits until refrigerant pressures equalize before automatically restoring electricity.

What happens if the utility neutral conductor breaks (floating neutral condition)?

A broken neutral line on a three-phase distribution transformer (often caused by storms, poor connections, or copper theft) causes the neutral point to float. Consequently, phase-to-neutral voltages in single-phase homes become unbalanced: one phase can collapse to $100\, ext{V}$ while another phase surges to $380\, ext{V}$ or $400\, ext{V}$! A standard miniature circuit breaker (MCB) will NOT trip during a floating neutral because current draw may still be within nominal limits. A digital over and under voltage protector senses the $380\, ext{V}$ swell within $< 0.1\, ext{seconds}$ and snaps open, saving connected household appliances, televisions, and electronics from catastrophic fire.
