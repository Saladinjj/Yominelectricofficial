Low Voltage Current Transformer: Selection Guide for 0.66 kV Panels

ET
ET Engineering Team November 24, 2026 11 min read

A low voltage current transformer is a measuring transformer that steps a large alternating current down to 5 A for a meter, installed inside low voltage distribution equipment rather than in a substation. The practical definition is the insulation class: the transformer is built for a system voltage of 0.66 kV or a rated voltage of 720 V AC, it clamps around a cable or busbar, and its secondary feeds a kWh meter, a power analyser or a building management input.

That sounds simple until you try to order one. Two transformers with the same 200/5 A ratio can differ in accuracy class, burden, window shape and whether they can be opened around a live cable. This guide covers the decisions that actually change the part number, and the checks that decide whether the meter reading is worth trusting afterwards.

What makes a CT "low voltage" rather than medium voltage

The dividing line is the insulation system, not the current. A low voltage CT is a moulded plastic case with a magnetic core and a copper secondary wound around it. It has no epoxy cast body, no oil, no primary bar insulated for 12 kV, and no provision for mounting on a post insulator. You will see the class in the model name: a Bh-0.66 or MSQ-0.66 unit is a 0.66 kV transformer, and a 720 V AC marking on the label describes the same class of duty.

Medium voltage and high voltage transformers are a different product family entirely, cast in cycloaliphatic epoxy and specified by rated voltage, creepage distance and lightning impulse level. If your panel is a 400 V or 690 V switchboard, you want the low voltage family, and the design questions become mechanical and metrological rather than dielectric.

Where the transformer sits in a metering chain

The CT is the first element of a chain: primary conductor, current transformer, secondary wiring, meter, and then whatever consumes the number. Each element adds error, and the class you buy only describes the transformer at reference conditions. A Class 0.5 CT connected with 2.5 mm² secondary cable over 15 m, with the meter at the far end, is not a Class 0.5 measuring system.

This is why the burden figure matters as much as the ratio. Burden is the volt-ampere load the secondary can drive while staying inside its accuracy limits, and the meter plus the cable resistance together must stay below it. A split-core unit with a small window often has less margin here than a solid-core unit of the same ratio, because the core has to be split to open around the cable.

For retrofit work the split-core form usually decides itself: nothing has to be disconnected. For a new board, a solid window unit is cheaper for the same accuracy and is fixed before the cables arrive. The mistake to avoid is ordering a window CT after the cable terminations are made.

Choosing the ratio

The ratio is written as primary over secondary, for example 2000/5 A. Pick the primary rating just above the maximum current the circuit will actually carry, so the meter spends its life in the upper part of its scale where resolution is best. Oversizing to the breaker rating instead of the load is the most common cause of a meter that reads almost nothing on a graph.

Maximum load currentSensible primary ratingTypical use
Up to 150 A150/5 A or 200/5 ASmall commercial boards, single tenant metering
150–400 A400/5 AShop units, small plant rooms
400–800 A800/5 ABuilding incomers, feeder pillars
800–2000 A2000/5 AMain distribution boards, factory feeders
2000–3000 A3000/5 ATransformer incomers, heavy process lines

Two cautions. First, a ratio is not a licence to exceed it: sustained primary current above the rating pushes the core towards saturation and the secondary waveform distorts before the number on the display goes obviously wrong. Second, where a circuit runs at a steady low fraction of its breaker rating, a smaller CT with a longer secondary run is usually a better engineering answer than a big CT with a short one.

Accuracy class: 0.5 or 1.0

Class 0.5 means the ratio and phase error stay inside a 0.5 % band at the specified burden and current; Class 1.0 allows twice that. For revenue metering, tenant billing and anything that ends up on an invoice, Class 0.5 is the sensible floor. For trend monitoring, energy reporting and fault investigation where the absolute number matters less than the shape of the curve, Class 1.0 is often enough and costs less.

Whichever you buy, the class only holds at the stated burden and at currents between roughly 5 % and 120 % of rating. Below that, phase error grows quickly and a small load can be misreported by a wide margin. If a site has a very light overnight load and the bill depends on it, a higher class is worth more than a bigger ratio.

Installation checks that protect the reading

  • Direction. P1 faces the supply, P2 faces the load. Reverse one CT in a three-phase set and that phase reads negative while the others read positive.
  • Secondary polarity. S1 goes to the meter's I+ terminal and S2 to I−, consistently across every phase. Mixed polarity inside one meter is a data fault that survives commissioning because the total still looks plausible.
  • One earth point only. The secondary circuit is earthed at one point. A second earth creates a parallel path and an error you cannot find with a clamp meter.
  • Short before you open. Any CT secondary that is opened under primary current will try to generate a dangerous voltage. Fit a shorting terminal block and use it before breaking the circuit.
  • Burden budget. Add the meter burden to the cable loop resistance and compare with the CT rating. Long runs with thin cable are the usual failure.

When a low voltage CT is the wrong answer

Three cases are worth naming. If the circuit is a 6.6 kV or 11 kV feeder, you need a medium or high voltage transformer, not a bigger low voltage one. If the current is under roughly 5 A, a direct-connected meter removes the transformer and its error entirely. And if the only purpose is to know whether a machine is running, a current switch or a transducer is cheaper than a metering-class CT plus a meter.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between 0.66 kV and 720 V on a low voltage CT?

They describe the same class of duty from two directions. 0.66 kV is the insulation class used in model designations, and 720 V AC is the rated voltage printed on the label. Both mean the transformer is intended for low voltage distribution equipment, not for a substation.

Can a split-core CT be as accurate as a solid one?

Yes, at the same class and burden, provided the closing faces are clean and fully latched. The split introduces a small air gap, so the practical risk is mechanical rather than inherent: a unit that is not properly closed, or that has dirt on the faces, will read low and drift.

Do I need Class 0.5 for tenant billing?

If the number reaches an invoice or a dispute, yes. Class 0.5 keeps ratio and phase error inside a 0.5 % band at rated burden, which is the usual floor for billing. Class 1.0 is acceptable for internal reporting where a consistent trend matters more than absolute accuracy.

What secondary cable size should I use?

Size it for burden, not habit. Work out the loop resistance of the run, add the meter burden, and keep the total below the CT's rated VA. As a starting point, 2.5 mm² is common for short panel runs and 4 mm² or 6 mm² for long ones, but the calculation decides.

Can one low voltage CT meter a three-phase circuit?

No. A single CT measures one conductor. A three-phase circuit needs one transformer per phase, with matched ratios, feeding a three-phase meter that sums them. Mismatched ratios across phases produce a total that is wrong in a way that looks like a load problem.
