Voltage Stabilizer for a CNC Machine: The Sizing Rules That Actually Apply

ET
ET Engineering Team November 10, 2026 10 min read

A CNC machine is not a resistive load with a nameplate, and sizing a stabilizer on the sum of nameplate kVA usually gets the answer wrong in one of two directions. Too small, because the spindle and the servo drives draw peak current the nameplate never mentions. Or needlessly large, because the drive front end already tolerates a good part of the variation you were trying to correct. The stabilizer has to be sized on the current the machine actually draws, over the voltage window the machine actually needs.

Why a CNC load is not a nameplate load

On a machine tool, three things happen that a simple load table does not describe. The spindle accelerates and decelerates constantly rather than running at a steady current. The axis drives regenerate power back into the DC bus when they brake. And the whole cabinet is fed through a variable frequency drive, which draws current in short pulses rather than in a smooth sine.

Each of those changes what the stabilizer sees at its output. A stabilizer sized on the sum of the motor nameplates will be asked for current peaks it cannot supply, and the symptom is not a dramatic failure. It is nuisance tripping, a spindle that faults on acceleration, or a control that reboots during a heavy cut — faults that get blamed on the machine rather than on the supply in front of it.

Four effects that actually decide the kVA

Spindle inrush. A spindle motor starting direct-on-line can draw several times its rated current for a short period. If the machine has a direct-on-line spindle rather than a drive, that surge, not the running current, sets the stabilizer rating.

Drive harmonic current. A VFD front end does not present a clean linear load. The current waveform is rich in harmonics, the RMS current is higher than the fundamental would suggest, and the stabilizer has to carry that current without saturating or overheating. This is where the manufacturer's derating for non-linear load comes from, and it should be read from the datasheet rather than assumed.

Servo regeneration. When an axis decelerates, the drive pushes energy back toward the supply. Some of that is absorbed by a braking resistor, and some appears at the input. A stabilizer that corrects in one direction only, or that has no headroom above nominal, will see its output voltage rise on a fast deceleration. Machines with heavy, fast axes — gantry and high-speed milling — are the ones where this shows up first.

Phase imbalance and phase loss. A three-phase supply with one weak phase produces a stabilizer output that is also unbalanced, unless each phase is regulated independently. CNC controls are less tolerant of imbalance than most industrial loads, and a lost phase is worse than an unbalanced one. Phase-failure protection is a separate requirement from voltage correction, and it should be specified explicitly rather than assumed.

Matching the sizing basis to the load

Load in the cabinetSizing basisWhat to watch
Lighting, heaters, resistive loadsRunning kVANothing beyond the total
Spindle motor, direct-on-lineStarting surgeSurge duration against the stabilizer's overload capability
Spindle motor, drive-fedRunning current plus drive deratingHarmonic current and the datasheet derating figure
Servo axesRunning kVA plus regeneration headroomOutput rise on deceleration; braking resistor arrangement
Coolant pump, chip conveyorRunning kVA, check starting methodMotors started direct-on-line add a surge on top

Add the continuous loads first, then treat the largest surge separately rather than adding every surge together. Machines rarely start every motor at the same instant, and sizing for the sum of all possible surges is how a shop ends up with a stabilizer twice the size it needed.

Working the number

For a three-phase machine the arithmetic is not the difficult part:

  • Continuous kVA = √3 × line voltage × line current, summed over the loads that run together.
  • Surge kVA = the largest single starting or accelerating event, taken from the motor or drive data, not estimated.
  • Regulation window = the input range the stabilizer must accept, taken from the site's actual recordings rather than a remembered nominal.
  • Headroom = the derating the manufacturer requires for the load type, applied after the sum, not instead of it.

The input range is worth measuring rather than guessing. A shop at the end of a feeder may be seeing a sag on every heavy cut that nobody has recorded, and a stabilizer chosen for a nominal ±15% window will sit at its limit exactly when the machine needs it most.

What a stabilizer can and cannot fix

A voltage stabilizer corrects RMS voltage. That is the whole job, and it is worth being precise about the boundary because machine-tool faults are often misdiagnosed as voltage problems.

  • It fixes steady over-voltage and under-voltage, and the slow sag that follows a heavy load elsewhere on the site.
  • It does not fix harmonics. Correcting the voltage of a distorted waveform leaves it distorted, and the drive still sees the distortion.
  • It does not fix imbalance unless every phase is regulated independently — and even then, the correct answer is usually to find the imbalance at the source.
  • It does not fix frequency, transients or earth faults. Those need a different device, and a stabilizer in front of them changes nothing.

This matters commercially. If the real problem is a harmonic resonance with a capacitor bank, or a bad neutral, no amount of stabilizer capacity will solve it, and the shop will have bought a large box that does not address the fault.

Servo AVR, relay type or variac

TypeCorrectionWhere it fits on a machine tool
Servo motor AVRContinuous, motor-driven variable transformerCNC cabinets and mixed machine loads; the usual choice where the load varies all day
Relay or electronic tap changerStep correctionSmaller machines and stable loads; cheaper, but steps are audible in the output
Variac / variable transformerManual or motorised set pointTest benches and fixed processes, not a machine that changes load continuously

For a machine shop the deciding property is usually continuous correction under a load that changes second by second, which points to a servo-driven unit. A tap changer that corrects in 10 V steps is fine for a compressor and wrong for a control that is sensitive to a step appearing mid-cut.

Three-phase or single-phase

Industrial CNC machines are almost always three-phase, and the stabilizer should match the machine's phase configuration rather than the building's convenience. A common error is stabilising one phase and feeding the control from it while the spindle runs on the unstabilised three-phase supply: the control is protected and the spindle is not, which is the opposite of what was intended.

Small benchtop machines, training lathes and single-phase spindles are the exception, and a single-phase unit is correct there. What matters is that the stabilizer covers every load that shares the fault, not just the load that was easiest to wire.

Installation details that cause callbacks

  • Ambient and ventilation. A servo AVR dissipates heat. A cabinet designed for 25 °C and installed in a 40 °C workshop corner will derate, and the derating is not optional.
  • Cable and volt drop. A stabilizer at the far end of a long run corrects voltage at its own terminals, not at the machine. Cable sizing has to follow the same rules as any other feeder.
  • Bypass. Specify a maintenance bypass. Running production on a stabilizer with no bypass means every service visit is a shutdown.
  • Separation from welding and other dirty loads. A stabilizer sharing a board with a welding set will spend its life correcting the welder's dips.
  • Earthing and neutral. Follow the machine builder's requirement. Correcting voltage does not correct a neutral problem, and an undersized neutral shows up as control faults that look like supply faults.
