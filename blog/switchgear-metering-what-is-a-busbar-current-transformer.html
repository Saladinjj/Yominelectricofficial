Switchgear Metering: What Is a Busbar Current Transformer?

ET
ET Engineering Team October 14, 2026 10 min read

The Essential Measurement Interface for Low-Voltage Power Distribution Switchgear

Inside every commercial main distribution board (MDB), industrial motor control center (MCC), generator synchronizing panel, and data center power distribution unit (PDU), accurate current measurement is the foundational prerequisite for power management.

Digital power meters, energy monitors, power factor controllers, and protective relays cannot accept heavy raw currents ranging from hundreds to thousands of amperes directly into their delicate electronic terminals.

To measure these heavy currents safely, electrical switchboard panel builders rely on instrument current transformers. However, traditional wound-primary current transformers require cutting the main power busbar and bolting cumbersome terminal blocks, introducing electrical joint resistance and severe physical bulk.

The Low-Voltage Busbar Window-Type Current Transformer (Model MSQ / BH-0.66 Series) represents the universal, space-saving engineering standard: a compact toroidal measuring transformer featuring a rectangular window aperture that slips directly over the continuous copper busbar, delivering Class 0.5 and Class 0.2 precision with zero busbar cutting.

Engineering Anatomy: Inside a Model MSQ Busbar Window Current Transformer

  1. Continuous Toroidal Low-Loss Silicon Steel Core: Wound from high-permeability, cold-rolled grain-oriented silicon steel sheet ($CRGO$). Its gapless circular magnetic circuit ensures ultra-low excitation current, minimal phase angle error, and high accuracy across the entire load range (5% to 120% of rated current).
  2. Precision Machine-Wound Secondary Copper Coil: Wound uniformly around the toroidal core using high-grade enameled electrolytic copper wire, minimizing leakage flux and maximizing secondary burden capacity (up to 30 VA).
  3. Ultrasonically Welded Flame-Retardant ABS Casing: Impact-resistant polycarbonate/ABS housing certified to UL94 V-0 flame retardancy. Provides 3.0 kV dielectric isolation between the primary live busbar and secondary instrumentation circuits.
  4. Integrated Insulated Busbar Clamping Screws: Heavy-duty plastic-headed clamping screws thread through the housing to lock the CT rigidly onto the copper busbar, preventing mechanical rattling and acoustic humming caused by 50Hz/60Hz electromagnetic vibration.
  5. Sealable Transparent Secondary Terminal Cover: Transparent polycarbonate terminal cover with lead-sealing holes protects secondary brass screw terminals (S1, S2) against accidental contact and prevents unauthorized tampering with utility billing circuits.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a Busbar Current Transformer and how does its window-type design function in low-voltage switchboards?

A Busbar Current Transformer—frequently designated as a window-type current transformer, busbar-mounted CT, or ring-type measuring CT—is a specialized low-voltage instrument transformer designed with a hollow central window aperture that slips directly over a solid copper or aluminum busbar without requiring any physical disconnection, cutting, or bolting of the primary electrical conductor. In modern electrical distribution switchboards and motor control centers (MCC): <ul><li><strong>The Window-Type Operating Principle:</strong> The solid copper busbar itself serves as the single-turn primary winding ($N_1 = 1$). As alternating load current ($I_p$) flows through the busbar, it induces a proportional magnetic flux inside the high-permeability toroidal silicon steel core. The secondary coil, tightly wound with multiple turns of enameled copper wire ($N_2$), produces a stepped-down proportional secondary current ($I_s = 5$A or 1A) governed by the transformer turns ratio: $$I_s = I_p imes rac{N_1}{N_2}$$</li><li><strong>Seamless Panel Assembly:</strong> During switchgear fabrication, panel builders slide the lightweight MSQ series CT over the busbar riser, tighten the integrated insulated clamping screws against the copper bar, and wire the secondary terminals (S1, S2) directly to digital power meters, multimeters, or energy management controllers.</li></ul>

How do switchboard builders select the correct window aperture size for copper busbars?

Selecting the correct window-type CT aperture is essential to avoid mechanical interference and thermal overheating: <ul><li><strong>MSQ-30 (Window 30x10mm or &phi;30mm round):</strong> Ideal for smaller distribution boards with primary currents from 100A to 300A. Accommodates a single 30x10mm copper bar or insulated power cables up to 30mm diameter.</li><li><strong>MSQ-40 (Window 40x10mm):</strong> Used for 300A to 600A main breakers and feeder switches, accepting standard 40x10mm copper busbars.</li><li><strong>MSQ-60 (Window 60x10mm or two 50x10mm bars):</strong> The workhorse for 600A to 1200A main distribution boards, accepting single or double busbars.</li><li><strong>MSQ-100 / MSQ-120 (Window 100x10mm to 120x10mm):</strong> High-capacity switchboards from 1200A to 5000A, fitting multiple parallel busbars.</li></ul>

What is the difference between Class 0.5 and Class 0.2 accuracy, and when should 1A secondary be chosen over 5A?

The accuracy class defines the maximum allowable percentage ratio error at rated current according to IEC 61869-2: <ul><li><strong>Class 0.5 (&plusmn;0.5% Ratio Error):</strong> The universal industry standard for commercial submetering, motor control centers, and industrial power quality monitors.</li><li><strong>Class 0.2 (&plusmn;0.2% Ratio Error):</strong> Required for high-precision utility revenue billing, data center colocation tenant metering, and grid interconnection points.</li><li><strong>5A vs. 1A Secondary Output:</strong> 5A is standard when the CT is mounted inside the same cabinet as the digital meter (cable runs $< 10$ meters). If the meter is located in a remote control room 50 to 200 meters away, a <strong>1A secondary output</strong> must be selected: because cable power loss is proportional to the square of the current ($P = I^2 R$), a 1A secondary reduces cable burden resistance losses by <strong>25 times ($25 imes$)</strong>, preventing CT core saturation and maintaining high measurement accuracy.</li></ul>
