Industrial Automation: What Is an AC Current Transducer?

ET
ET Engineering Team October 17, 2026 10 min read

The Precision Translation Bridge Between High-Power AC Equipment and Microelectronic PLCs

Inside modern industrial manufacturing facilities, chemical process plants, water treatment stations, and automated machinery, Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs) and Distributed Control Systems (DCS) orchestrate thousands of pumps, fans, conveyors, and compressors.

To prevent motor overloads, detect mechanical pump jams, monitor conveyor belt slip, and calculate factory energy consumption, the automation PLC must know the precise electrical current drawn by each three-phase motor.

However, industrial motors operate on 380V, 480V, or 690V AC with phase currents of dozens or hundreds of amperes. Traditional current transformers scale this down to 5A AC, but PLC analog input modules are strictly low-voltage DC electronic devices that cannot accept alternating current directly.

The AC Current Transducer (Model YM-ACT Series) serves as the indispensable translation interface: a solid-state instrument sensor that measures AC conductor current and converts it into a clean, linear, electrically isolated 4–20 mA DC or 0–10 V DC analog signal that wires directly into any standard PLC analog card.

Engineering Anatomy: Inside a Model YM-ACT True-RMS AC Current Transducer

  1. Magnetic Induction Sensing Core (Solid or Split-Core): High-permeability silicon steel or permalloy toroidal core with precision secondary winding. Split-core models feature a spring-loaded hinge and latch for rapid clamp-on installation around existing live conductors without disconnecting power cables.
  2. Integrated True-RMS Signal Processing Circuitry: On-board precision analog computation IC continuously samples the induced AC waveform, executing instantaneous squaring, averaging, and square-root calculations to output true-RMS current values even on distorted VFD inverter outputs.
  3. 3000V AC Galvanic Dielectric Isolation Barrier: High-voltage optocoupler and magnetic isolation barrier separating the high-voltage primary conductor aperture from the low-voltage DC output terminals, protecting delicate PLC microelectronics from destructive power surges.
  4. Precision 4–20 mA DC Loop-Powered Transmitter: Regulated constant-current output driver that powers itself directly from the PLC's 24V DC loop supply (2-wire configuration), eliminating the need for a separate auxiliary power supply brick.
  5. Flame-Retardant DIN-Rail Mounting Enclosure: Compact UL 94 V-0 rated polycarbonate housing that snaps directly onto standard 35mm DIN-rails, saving valuable horizontal space in crowded control cabinets.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an AC Current Transducer and why cannot PLCs read standard current transformers directly?

An AC Current Transducer—frequently designated as an AC current transmitter, 4–20mA current sensor, or current-to-DC converter—is a precision solid-state electronic device that measures alternating current (AC) flowing through a power conductor and converts it into an electrically isolated, standardized low-voltage direct current (DC) analog signal (typically 4–20 mA DC or 0–10 V DC). In industrial PLC and SCADA automation panels: <ul><li><strong>The Incompatibility of Traditional CTs:</strong> Standard current transformers (CTs) output an alternating current of 0–5 A AC. Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs from Siemens, Allen-Bradley, Schneider, Omron) and microcontrollers are strictly DC microelectronic circuits whose analog input cards only accept 4–20 mA DC or 0–10 V DC signals. Connecting 5A AC directly to a PLC analog card will destroy the sensitive analog-to-digital converter (ADC) channels.</li><li><strong>Integrated Conditioning Solution:</strong> Instead of installing a bulky CT plus an external signal conditioning DIN-rail amplifier module and messy inter-wiring, the YOMIN ACT series combines the inductive sensing core and the precision true-RMS 4–20mA transmitter circuit into a single, compact, pre-calibrated housing.</li></ul>

Why is a 4–20 mA DC Current Loop preferred over a 0–10 V DC signal in industrial plants?

While voltage signals (0–10 V DC) are simple, current loops (4–20 mA DC) dominate industrial automation for two critical engineering reasons: <ul><li><strong>Zero Voltage-Drop Signal Degradation:</strong> Electrical cables have intrinsic resistance ($R$). Over cable runs exceeding 50 to 300 meters between factory floor motor drives and central PLC control rooms, voltage signals suffer significant $I imes R$ voltage drop, distorting measurement accuracy. A 4–20 mA current loop delivers identical current regardless of cable length or loop resistance (up to $500\,\Omega$).</li><li><strong>Live Zero (4 mA) Wire-Break Fault Detection:</strong> A 0–10 V signal cannot distinguish between zero measured current and a severed/disconnected sensor wire. In a 4–20 mA loop, <strong>4 mA represents 0% current</strong>, while <strong>0 mA instantly alerts the PLC to a broken wire, blown fuse, or damaged sensor</strong>.</li></ul>

What is the difference between Average-Responding and True-RMS AC Current Transducers?

Basic low-cost transducers utilize simple average-rectifying circuits calibrated to the root-mean-square value of a pure sine wave ($RMS = 1.11 imes ext{Average}$). However, in modern industrial plants with Variable Frequency Drives (VFDs), solar inverters, LED power supplies, and DC rectifiers, AC currents contain severe harmonic distortion and non-sinusoidal chopped waveforms. Average-sensing transducers produce measurement errors exceeding 20% to 40% under harmonic distortion. YOMIN ACT transducers incorporate <strong>True-RMS (TRMS) analog computation chips</strong> that mathematically calculate the exact root-mean-square heating value of the waveform, delivering accurate &plusmn;0.5% measurements on distorted and chopped inverter currents.
