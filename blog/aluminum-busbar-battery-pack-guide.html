Aluminium Busbar for Battery Packs: Grades, Joints and Sizing

ET
ET Engineering Team October 31, 2026 10 min read

An aluminium busbar is the right choice for a battery pack when weight, cost per kilo or the sheer size of the section decides the design, and when the joints can be engineered rather than improvised. It is the wrong choice when it is dropped into a copper design unchanged: the bar will carry the current, but the interface between aluminium and a copper terminal is where packs fail after a year of thermal cycling.

Why packs move to aluminium, and where that stops

A lithium pack is a weight-driven design. Every kilogram of conductor is a kilogram that is not cell, and in a vehicle or a containerised storage system that kilogram is paid for twice: once at purchase and once in the structure that has to carry it. Aluminium is roughly a third of the density of copper, so even after the section is enlarged for conductivity the finished bar is about half the mass of the copper equivalent.

Where that argument stops is at the joint. Aluminium forms an oxide layer within minutes of being cleaned, that oxide is an insulator, and the metal creeps under sustained clamp load. A bolted aluminium joint that is tightened to the right torque on the day of assembly can lose a significant part of its clamp force over a few hundred thermal cycles. The contact area shrinks, resistance rises, the joint runs hotter, and the mechanism feeds itself.

None of that makes aluminium unsuitable. It makes the joint a designed component with its own specification, in the same way the cell is.

The weight and section trade-off, in numbers

The usual summary — aluminium needs more section but weighs less — is true but too vague to design with. The table below applies the reference conductivity ratio to a simple case: the same continuous current, the same allowable temperature rise.

Two consequences deserve attention before the first drawing. The larger section needs space, which in a pack is often scarcer than mass; a design that saves 400 g of conductor but adds 8 mm of stack height may be a net loss. And the higher expansion coefficient means the joint sees more relative movement per thermal cycle, which is precisely why the clamp hardware and the contact plating matter more on aluminium than on copper.

The joint is the design problem

Four measures turn an aluminium joint from a liability into a component.

  • Plate the contact surface. Tinned or nickel-plated aluminium presents a stable surface to the mating terminal and keeps the insulating oxide from forming at the interface. Bare aluminium bolted to bare copper is the configuration that generates field failures.
  • Break the galvanic couple. Where aluminium meets copper, use a bimetallic transition or a plated interface, and keep moisture out with a joint compound. In a pack the electrolyte is never far away, so this is not a theoretical concern.
  • Hold the clamp load. Use hardened washers or disc springs so the joint keeps its force as the stack expands and contracts. Specify the torque, and specify a re-torque after the first formation or thermal cycle.
  • Control the surface preparation at the supplier. Abrasion and plating belong in the process document, not in a fitter's judgement on the line, because two adjacent joints prepared differently will age differently.

This is also why an aluminium busbar should be bought to a drawing that names the plating, the joint face finish and the hardware, rather than to a cross-section alone.

Insulation in a pack: coating, sleeving and clearance

Inside a pack, insulation is doing two jobs at once: electrical isolation and mechanical protection against chafing where the bar passes a cell terminal or a module edge. Three options cover most designs.

OptionTypical useWatch for
Epoxy powder or resin coatingRigid bars crossing modules, high voltageCoating must stop short of the contact face; thickness varies at edges
Heat-shrink sleevingFormed links, tight bendsCut ends can expose a sharp conductor edge
PET film or insulation paperLayer separation between stacked barsAdds stack height; must not trap heat

The recurring specification mistake is a coating that creeps onto the contact face during production. Contact resistance rises, the joint runs warmer, and the fault is invisible until the pack is tested at load. State the masked area on the drawing, with a dimension, and inspect it as a critical characteristic rather than a cosmetic one.

Sizing a pack interconnect

Pack interconnects are short, and short conductors behave differently from long runs. Voltage drop is rarely the constraint; temperature rise at the terminal and fault withstand usually are.

  • Start from the continuous current of the module, not the pack. A pack rated at 200 A may push far more through a parallel group, and the bar between two modules carries a fraction of the total. Size each link for what actually flows through it.
  • Check the terminal, not only the bar. In most packs the limiting component is the cell or module terminal and its welded or bolted interface, not the conductor cross-section.
  • Check fault withstand for the clearing time. A module fuse clears in milliseconds; the bar still has to survive the let-through energy without annealing or distorting. Short, thick links are thermally comfortable but mechanically stiff, so support them.
  • Respect the bending radius. A formed link that is bent too tightly work-hardens at the bend, and that is where a crack starts under vibration.

Apply the same first-pass current density reasoning used for panel busbars and then verify it against the manufacturer's tested data for the actual mounting, because a bar clamped against a plastic module cools very differently from one in free air.

Frequently asked questions

Is aluminium acceptable for a high-current battery pack?

Yes, provided the joint is designed for it. Aluminium carries high current perfectly well at a larger section, and its lower mass is a real advantage in a pack. The failure mode to design out is the aluminium-to-copper interface: plate the contact surfaces, use a bimetallic transition where needed, exclude moisture with a joint compound, and hold the clamp load with hardened washers or disc springs.

Why does an aluminium busbar need a larger cross-section than copper?

Because its conductivity is roughly 61 % of copper's on the IACS reference scale, an aluminium bar needs about 1.6 times the copper cross-section to carry the same current at the same temperature rise. Even so, the finished bar weighs about half as much, because aluminium density is roughly a third of copper's.

Can an aluminium busbar be tinned or plated?

Yes, and for pack work it usually should be plated. Tin or nickel plating gives the mating terminal a stable surface, prevents the insulating aluminium oxide from forming at the interface, and makes contact resistance predictable over thermal cycling. Specify the plating and the masked contact area on the drawing.

Specifying aluminium busbars for a pack?

Send the module layout, the current per link, the plating and the insulation requirement. We manufacture aluminium and copper busbars to drawing from 100 pieces, rigid or flexible, insulated or bare, for ESS, solar and battery-pack assemblies.
