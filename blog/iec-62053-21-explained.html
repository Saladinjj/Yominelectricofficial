IEC 62053-21 Explained: Accuracy Classes for Direct-Connected kWh Meters

ET
ET Engineering Team November 4, 2026 10 min read

IEC 62053-21 is the part of the IEC 62053 suite that sets the accuracy requirements for direct-connected static (electronic) watt-hour meters in classes 1 and 2. The class number is the maximum permitted measurement error as a percentage at the reference conditions the standard defines — class 1 means 1 %, class 2 means 2 %. A meter that carries the marking has been type-tested against those limits, at a defined current range, power factor and temperature. It says nothing about how the meter behaves once the installation pushes it outside those conditions, and that gap is where most metering disputes start.

What IEC 62053-21 covers, and what it leaves to other parts

The IEC 62053 family is split by the accuracy class a meter is built to, not by the product family. Part 21 covers the standard classes used on direct-connected meters — class 1 and class 2. The tighter classes, 0.5S and 0.2S, which are the ones revenue-grade installations specify for transformer-operated metering, sit in a separate part of the same suite (IEC 62053-22). That is why a competent quotation cites the two together, and why our own industrial metering range is listed against “IEC 62053-21/22” rather than a single number.

Two consequences follow for a buyer. First, a meter cannot be “class 1 and 0.5S” at the same time; the accuracy class is a property of the build. Second, if a tender document asks only for “IEC 62053” without naming the part, the supplier is free to offer the cheapest class that satisfies the sentence, and the cheapest class is very often class 2.

Older specifications still reference IEC 61036 for the same class 1 and class 2 static meters, and it is common to see both numbers on a Chinese meter datasheet. For a new project, 62053-21 is the reference that will be accepted.

The class number is a ceiling, not an average

A class 1 meter is not a meter that is typically 1 % out. It is a meter whose error must stay inside ±1 % at every test point the standard defines, across the whole declared current range. The distinction matters because two meters can both be class 1 and still behave very differently on a lightly loaded circuit: one sits at 0.2 % and one sits at 0.95 %, and both are conforming.

The column on the right is engineering judgement, not a clause in the standard. Nothing in IEC 62053-21 forbids a class 2 meter on an industrial feeder, and nothing obliges a utility to accept class 0.5S. What actually decides the matter is who carries the commercial risk of the measurement error.

Why the same meter can pass on the bench and drift on site

The declared class holds at the reference conditions. A meter that leaves the factory at 0.3 % error can read several percent out once the installation stops looking like the test bench, and the causes are predictable:

  • Current range. Accuracy is specified between a stated minimum current and the maximum. Below the minimum — a lightly loaded feeder at night, a tenant with one appliance — the error budget widens sharply. A meter rated 5(80) A is not held to class 1 at 50 mA.
  • Power factor. The error limits are tighter at unity power factor than at 0.5 lagging. A plant running at 0.7 power factor is operating in the part of the envelope where the permitted error is larger.
  • Temperature. The standard defines a reference temperature and a permitted drift away from it. A meter in a sealed outdoor cabinet in a hot climate spends its life at the edge of that band, not in the middle.
  • Waveform. A meter type-tested on a sinusoidal supply is being asked to measure an inverter output, a variable-speed drive or a switched-mode load. Harmonic content shifts the measurement, and a class 1 certificate does not quantify by how much.
  • Wiring and phase sequence. A three-phase meter with one phase reversed, or with the current coil on the wrong conductor, can be perfectly conforming and still bill the wrong number.

The practical lesson is that the class is necessary and not sufficient. On a circuit with heavy harmonic distortion or a very wide load swing, the specification that protects the buyer is not a tighter class but a meter whose declared current range actually brackets the real load.

The direct-connected ceiling

Part 21 applies to meters that carry the load current through their own terminals, and those terminals set a hard physical limit. Our ym-0058 is a three-phase four-wire DIN rail meter rated 3×5(80) A with WiFi communication. At 400 V three-phase that 80 A ceiling corresponds to 1.732 × 400 × 80 ÷ 1000, or about 55 kVA.

Above roughly that point the circuit moves to a current transformer: the meter becomes a 5 A device reading a scaled secondary signal, and the accuracy of the whole measurement is now the accuracy of the transformer plus the meter. At that stage the class that matters is 0.5S, and it comes from the other part of the suite. The reason is not commercial — it is that a direct-connected meter rated for 400 A does not exist in any practical form.

Where a three-phase circuit needs maximum-demand registration as well as energy, the ym-0724 is the direct-connected option in the same family: class 1.0 accuracy, 60 A maximum current, RS485 and far-infrared communication. It is the meter to quote when the load sits under the direct-connection ceiling and the buyer wants demand data without adding a CT set.

Where class 1 is the right answer, and where it is not

Class 1 is the correct specification for the majority of commercial and industrial installations: internal cost allocation between departments, factory energy monitoring, sub-metering of a plant room, and any circuit where a 1 % error changes a decision rather than a payment.

It is the wrong specification in three situations. Where the reading becomes an invoice between two parties, because a 1 % error on a large account is a real sum of money and the party receiving the invoice will eventually ask what standard the meter was built to. Where the circuit is transformer-operated, because the class then belongs to the CT circuit as a whole. And where the load is dominated by harmonics, because the class was never intended to cover that waveform in the first place.

Seven lines, and the quotation stops being ambiguous. The most common failure is a buyer who sends “three-phase meter, class 1, 100 A” and receives a direct-connected meter that cannot legally be installed on the circuit they had in mind.

How to check a certificate before you pay

A type-test certificate is only useful if you read three things on it. The first is the standard and part number, because “IEC 62053” alone does not tell you which class was tested. The second is the current range the test covered — a certificate for 5(60) A says nothing about a 5(100) A variant. The third is the laboratory that issued it, and whether that laboratory is recognised in the market where the meter will bill.

The nameplate should then repeat the same three facts: the class, the standard, and the current range. If the plate says “class 1” and the certificate says class 2, the plate is the one that will be read by the inspector. Where the meter is going into a market with legal metering, MID in Europe for example, the additional approval is a separate document from the type test, and it is the one that decides whether the meter can be used for billing at all.

For the accuracy-class definitions themselves — what 0.5S means, and why the “S” extension exists — the energy meter accuracy class guide works through the class table in more detail than this page needs to. What this page adds is the boundary: which of those classes part 21 governs, and which part governs the rest.

What this means for a specification

Write the part number, not just the class. Write the current range, not just the maximum. Say whether the meter is direct-connected or CT-operated, because that single word decides which half of the IEC 62053 suite applies. And if the answer is “CT-operated”, accept that class 1 no longer describes the measurement — the transformer is now in the error chain, and its class is the one that sets the accuracy of the invoice.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between IEC 62053-21 and IEC 62053-22?

Part 21 covers direct-connected static meters in classes 1 and 2. Part 22 covers the tighter classes, 0.5S and 0.2S, which are used for transformer-operated metering. The part number therefore tells you the accuracy class band, and the class tells you the maximum permitted error.

Does a class 1 meter guarantee 1 % accuracy in service?

No. Class 1 is the maximum permitted error at the reference conditions defined by the standard, across the declared current range. Outside that range, at low power factor, at temperature extremes or on a distorted waveform, the permitted error is wider and the actual error can be several percent.

Can I use a class 1 direct-connected meter for tenant billing?

You can, and many landlords do. The question is who absorbs the error. On a large account a 1 % error is a real sum, so where the reading becomes an invoice between two parties, a class 0.5S CT-operated meter is the specification that will survive being questioned.

What current range should I specify?

The declared range must bracket the real load, not just the maximum. A meter rated 5(80) A is held to its class between the minimum and the maximum, so a circuit that runs most of the time at a few amps is being measured near the edge of the envelope where the error budget is widest.

Specifying meters against IEC 62053-21?

Send the supply voltage, the phase count, the maximum demand and the accuracy class the project requires. We will confirm whether a direct-connected meter in class 1 is enough or whether the circuit belongs on a class 0.5S transformer-operated meter, and quote both.
