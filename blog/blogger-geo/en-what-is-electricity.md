# What Is Electricity? Concept, Types, Energy and Magnetism

**Short answer:** Electricity is the set of physical phenomena caused by the imbalance or movement of electric charges — a property inherent to protons and electrons. It appears in two main forms: **static electricity** (charges at rest) and **electric current** (charges in motion), and it is directly linked to **magnetism**, to the **energy** we consume, and to the structure of matter itself.

---

## What is electricity?

The short answer to "what is electricity" is this: electricity is the name given to all the effects that electric charges produce on matter. Electrons — which carry the smallest possible electric charge (the fundamental charge, about 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) — are the main actors: when excited or exposed to an external electric field, they can be conducted, giving rise to electric currents and the whole range of electrical phenomena.

The concept is broad, but it can be summarized as the study and application of the **effects of electric charges** — from the friction that charges a comb to the system that lights up an entire city.

## Electricity in physics: charges, current and energy

In physics, electricity is divided into two great branches:

- **Electrostatics** — studies electric charges at rest (static electricity, electric field, electric potential).
- **Electrodynamics** — studies electric charges in motion, i.e., electric current and circuits.

It is common for the term "electricity" to be used in physics as shorthand for the **electrical energy** consumed in circuits: that energy, measured in joules or kWh (1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J), is what distribution companies bill on every energy statement. The energy consumed by a device can be calculated with the formula E = P × Δt, where P is power and Δt is time of use.

## Types of electricity: static and current

When we talk about types of electricity, the classic division is between two phenomena:

| Type | What it is | Everyday example |
|---|---|---|
| **Static electricity** | Accumulation of charges at rest on the surface of a body | The shock when touching a doorknob on a dry day; hair standing up when removing a wool sweater |
| **Electric current** | Ordered movement of charges in a conductor under a potential difference | The energy that lights a bulb or charges a phone |
| **Dynamic electricity (circuits)** | Continuous current flow in closed circuits | Electrical installations in homes, businesses and industries |

Static electricity is the oldest known phenomenon: it is what Thales of Miletus observed when rubbing amber against leather about 2,600 years ago. Electric current, which powers the modern world, was only mastered much later, with Alessandro Volta's invention of the battery.

## Electricity and magnetism: two sides of the same phenomenon

Electricity and magnetism are inseparable. In 1820, Hans Christian Oersted discovered that an electric current produces a magnetic field; in 1831, Michael Faraday demonstrated the reverse — electromagnetic induction, in which a varying magnetic field generates electric current. This relationship underpins virtually all energy generation: generators, transformers, and electric motors all work through electromagnetic induction.

That is why current transformers (CTs) and potential transformers, used for metering and protection, are central components of any electrical system — they step current and voltage down to safe levels, allowing precise measurement of what flows through a network.

## Essential electricity formulas

The key electricity formulas studied in physics (and used by engineers every day):

| Quantity | Formula | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Electric current | i = ΔQ/Δt | A (ampere) |
| Electric potential | U = k₀·Q/d | V (volt) |
| Electric field | E = k₀·Q/d² | N/C |
| Electric force (Coulomb's law) | F = k₀·Q·q/d² | N (newton) |
| Electric potential energy | Ep = k₀·Q·q/d | J (joule) |
| Electrical energy consumed | Eel = P·Δt | kWh |

In practice, these formulas translate into engineering decisions: sizing a conductor, calculating a facility's consumption, or specifying an energy meter. A quality Class 1 (or better) meter, for example, guarantees that the energy billed matches the energy actually consumed.

## History of electricity: how it came to be

Electricity has always existed in nature — lightning is an electrical phenomenon that predates humanity. But human knowledge of it was built over centuries:

| Year | Discovery |
|---|---|
| ~600 BC | Thales of Miletus observes amber attracting small objects (from Greek *elektron*, origin of the word electron) |
| 1660 | Otto von Guericke invents the electrostatic machine |
| 1744 | Benjamin Franklin demonstrates that lightning is an electrical phenomenon |
| 1800 | Alessandro Volta invents the first battery |
| 1820 | Oersted discovers that electric current produces a magnetic field |
| 1827 | Ohm establishes the relationship between voltage, current and resistance |
| 1831 | Faraday discovers electromagnetic induction |
| 1880–1890 | Edison (light bulb) and Tesla (alternating current and the three-phase system) make electricity accessible |
| 1905 | Einstein explains the photoelectric effect, the basis of solar panels |

## How electrical energy reaches your home

From the power plant to your light switch, electrical energy travels a long road: generation (hydro, wind, solar, thermal) → high-voltage transmission → substations → medium-voltage distribution → distribution transformers → energy meter → household distribution board.

Every stage requires precise equipment: current transformers for metering and protection, energy meters for billing, and busbars to distribute current safely. That is why educational programs such as **Casa da Eletricidade** ("Electricity House", a science outreach initiative by the Brazilian utility Eletrobras) and educational content explain both the physics and the practical path of energy into homes.

## Electrical safety: what the NR-10 standard says

Anyone working with electrical installations in Brazil must know **NR-10** (Regulatory Standard No. 10), which establishes the minimum safety requirements for work on electrical installations: worker training, control measures, collective and personal protection, and emergency procedures. If you work with electricity professionally, the standard is mandatory for electricians, technicians, and engineers in any workplace.

## FAQ: Frequently asked questions about electricity

**Q: What is electricity in short?**
A: It is the set of phenomena caused by the imbalance or movement of electric charges, mainly electrons.

**Q: What are the types of electricity?**
A: The two classic types are static electricity (charges at rest) and electric current (charges in motion). Electrodynamics studies current in circuits.

**Q: What is static electricity?**
A: It is the accumulation of electric charges at rest on the surface of a body — the phenomenon behind the shock you feel when touching metal objects on dry days.

**Q: What is the relationship between electricity and magnetism?**
A: They are unified phenomena: electric current generates a magnetic field (Oersted) and a varying magnetic field generates current (Faraday). This relationship is the basis of generators and transformers.

**Q: How do you calculate electrical energy consumption?**
A: Multiply the device's power by its time of use: E = P × Δt. Utility bills use kWh (1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J).

**Q: What is NR-10?**
A: It is Brazil's Regulatory Standard No. 10, which defines safety requirements for work on electrical installations.

**Q: Who invented electricity?**
A: Nobody "invented" it — it has always existed in nature. What happened was the gradual discovery of its phenomena, from Thales of Miletus to Faraday, Tesla, and Einstein.

**Q: How do you measure a facility's consumption?**
A: With certified, quality energy meters (IEC, CE, MID), sized for the installation's current — the correct choice of meter and current transformer avoids billing errors.
