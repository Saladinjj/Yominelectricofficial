Fuse Manufacturer Sourcing Guide: NH, PV and Cut-Out Families

ET
ET Engineering Team October 30, 2026 10 min read

Sourcing fuses from a manufacturer looks like a commodity purchase until the first batch arrives with the wrong operating class printed on the label. A fuse is one of the few components where a single wrong character — gG instead of aR, or an AC rating used on a DC circuit — turns a protective device into a fire risk that will pass every incoming visual inspection.

The short version: decide the family first, then the operating class, then the voltage and current, and only then compare suppliers. Ask for the marking that will be printed on the body, the standard it is made to, the breaking capacity, and whether the base, handle and microswitch are supplied with it. A manufacturer who can answer those four questions in writing is usually the one whose second shipment matches the first.

The families, and what each one is actually for

Four families cover most of what an exporter or panel builder buys. They are not interchangeable, and mixing them across a project is the most common sourcing mistake.

Reading the marking before you compare prices

A fuse body carries almost everything you need to know, and the four characters that matter most are usually the smallest on the label.

  • Operating class. gG is a full-range general-purpose link that clears overloads and short circuits. aR is a partial-range link intended for semiconductor and DC protection, and it will not clear a modest overload — it expects something else to do that job.
  • Voltage, with the current type. "DC 48 V" and "500 V AC" are different products. A DC rating is lower because a direct current arc does not self-extinguish at a zero crossing.
  • Standard. NH links are standardised under DIN 43620 and IEC 60269-2; semiconductor-protection links under IEC 60269-4. The standard on the label tells you what the dimensions and characteristics were tested against.
  • Breaking capacity. The fault current the link can interrupt safely. It has to exceed the prospective fault current at the point of installation, not merely match the current rating.

If a supplier quotes a fuse by current rating alone, you are one message away from a mismatch. Ask for a photograph of the actual label on the batch you will receive.

AC and DC are not the same purchase

An AC fuse relies on the current passing through zero twice per cycle to extinguish the arc. A DC circuit has no zero crossing, so the fuse has to be built and rated for it — a longer element, an arc-quenching filler, and a voltage rating that reflects the circuit's time constant. This is why a PV string fuse carries a DC voltage marking that looks low next to the AC rating of a physically similar NH link.

When you specify a DC fuse, give the manufacturer the DC voltage, the expected short-circuit current and the time constant. Those three numbers decide whether the part offered is suitable, and none of them can be inferred from the current rating.

What to put in the enquiry

A fuse enquiry that gets a correct quote on the first reply contains: family and size, operating class, current rating, voltage with AC or DC stated, breaking capacity required, whether the base and handle are needed, quantity per rating, and the standard you want the marking to reference. Add the destination country if labelling or documentation has to change for the market.

If you are replacing a part already in service, send a photograph of the label rather than a description. It resolves the operating class and voltage in one step, and it removes the most common cause of a wrong substitution.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between gG and aR fuses?

gG is a full-range general-purpose link that clears both sustained overloads and short-circuit fault currents. aR is a partial-range link designed for semiconductor and DC protection, which clears high fault currents but relies on other protection for overloads. Substituting one for the other changes the protection scheme, not just the part number.

Can I use an AC fuse on a DC circuit?

No, not unless the fuse is explicitly rated for DC at that voltage. A direct current arc has no zero crossing to help it extinguish, so a DC-rated fuse is built and tested differently. Using an AC-rated link on a DC circuit can leave the arc sustained after the element has melted.

Which standards should the marking reference?

NH knife-blade links are standardised under DIN 43620 and IEC 60269-2; semiconductor and PV protection links such as aR types are marked to IEC 60269-4. The standard tells you which dimensions and characteristics were tested, and it is the quickest way to confirm that two suppliers are offering equivalent parts.

Do I need to order the fuse base separately?

Usually yes. NH links need a matching spring-loaded base, and replacement in service needs an insulated extraction handle. Specify both with the fuse order, together with any microswitch for remote indication, because accessories sourced later are frequently the wrong size for the link.

How do I confirm a replacement fuse is the right one?

Send the manufacturer a photograph of the label on the failed part, plus the circuit voltage, current type and prospective fault current. The label resolves the operating class and voltage rating; the circuit data confirms the breaking capacity is adequate.

Sourcing fuses for a distribution or PV project?

Send the family, operating class, current, voltage with AC or DC, and the quantity per rating — or a photograph of the label you need to match. We supply NH, PV and cut-out fuse families with the matching bases and handles.
