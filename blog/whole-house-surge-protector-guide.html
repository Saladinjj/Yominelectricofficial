Whole House Surge Protector: What It Protects and What It Cannot

ET
ET Engineering Team November 26, 2026 8 min read

The short answer

Direct answer: a whole-house surge protector is a Type 2 surge protection device mounted at the main panel and wired in parallel with the incoming supply. It clamps the short, high-voltage spikes that arrive on the mains and diverts them to earth before they reach the appliances. It does not correct a supply that sits at 150 V for two hours, and it will not stop a direct lightning strike on the roof. Those are different faults, and they need different devices.

Most households that fit one device and still lose equipment have bought the right product for the wrong problem. The useful question is not “which surge protector” but “which of the four faults am I actually exposed to”.

Where the device sits in the installation

An SPD is a parallel component. It draws almost no current at normal voltage, and the moment the voltage crosses its clamping level the internal metal-oxide varistor conducts and dumps the surge energy into the earth conductor. Nothing in the circuit is switched; the appliance simply never sees the spike.

TypeWhere it goesWhat it is for
Type 1Main service entrance, ahead of the meterDirect lightning strikes and high-energy surges (10/350 µs wave)
Type 2Distribution boards and sub-panelsSwitching and induced surges (8/20 µs wave) — this is the whole-house device
Type 3Point of use, next to sensitive equipmentFine protection for electronics (combined wave)

Most residential installations combine a Type 2 device at the panel with Type 3 protection at the socket. A surge-protection power strip is a Type 3 device: useful as a second layer, unable to absorb a large surge on its own.

The four ratings that decide the unit

Catalogues quote a wall of numbers. Four of them decide whether the device does its job, and one decides whether it fits the panel.

RatingWhat it meansWhat to ask for
Uc — maximum continuous voltageThe highest steady voltage the device tolerates without conducting275 V AC on a 230 V mains; 1000 V DC on a solar string
In — nominal discharge currentWhat it can absorb repeatedly without degrading20 kA is the common Type 2 figure
Imax — maximum discharge currentThe one-off limit; the headline number sellers compare40 kA is the usual industrial spec
Up — voltage protection levelThe clamp voltage the appliance actually seesThe lower the better; compare it, do not just compare Imax
ModeHow many poles are protected2P for single phase, 3P or 4P for three phase

A unit with a spectacular Imax and a poor Up protects the panel, not the refrigerator. And a device that is compliant with IEC 61643 and carries a third-party mark such as TÜV or CE comes with documentation that insurers and utilities increasingly ask to see.

The three problems it will not solve

This is where most disappointment comes from, because all three faults are described by homeowners as “surges”.

What the site actually suffersWhat fixes itWhere that device lives
Brief spikes: motors switching, grid transients, induced lightning energyType 2 SPDMain panel, in parallel
Sustained over-voltage above roughly 260 V, or brownouts down to 100–140 V on a 220 V supplyOver/under-voltage protector, or an automatic voltage stabilizer where the sag lastsPanel or appliance inlet
A direct strike on the buildingLightning rod, down conductor and a low-impedance earthRoof and earth electrode
Surges arriving on the DC side of a PV arrayDC-rated SPDCombiner box or inverter DC input

Put differently: an SPD buys you microseconds. A voltage protector buys you minutes. A stabilizer buys you hours. A lightning protection system buys you the building. They are not alternatives to each other.

The four layers, in the order they earn their money

Layer 1 — interception. A copper lightning arrester rod and its down conductor give the strike somewhere to go other than the wiring. YOMIN supplies building lightning rods in copper conductor construction, CE marked to IEC standard, with a 10 kA / 60 kV / 800 A rating in the current range, and a 200 kA stainless steel rod for heavier exposure.

Layer 2 — diversion at the panel. The Type 2 SPD. Our SPD line in this series is the PV DC unit: T2, 2P, 1000 V rated voltage, 40 kA maximum discharge current, TÜV certified — the device that protects the DC side before the inverter. For an AC service-entrance device, send the system voltage, phase configuration and panel schedule and we will confirm exactly what the range covers rather than let you order on assumption.

Layer 3 — the faults an SPD ignores. A digital over/under-voltage protector with self-restoring operation, 40 A or 63 A at 220 V, disconnects the load when the supply leaves its window and reconnects when it returns. That is the device that saves a compressor during a two-hour brownout.

Layer 4 — continuous correction. Where the supply is chronically low rather than occasionally spiking, an automatic voltage stabilizer holds the output steady instead of merely disconnecting. Our single-phase servo units run to 10 kW / 15 kVA on a 110 V / 220 V, 50 Hz supply with a copper servo motor, and the three-phase TNS home range uses an LED display for the same duty on a three-phase service.

Before you order: six checks

  • Voltage and phase count. Single-phase 230 V and three-phase 400 V need different pole configurations, and the device must be ordered for the supply that exists, not the one on the drawing.
  • Panel space and breaker. A parallel device needs a dedicated way and a short, direct connection to the busbar. Confirm both before delivery, not after.
  • What sits downstream. A PV array, an EV charger or a workshop with welders changes the surge profile and may need a second device at that sub-panel.
  • The earth. An SPD diverts energy into the earth electrode. A high-resistance earth turns a well-specified device into a decorative one.
  • Status indication. Ask whether the unit shows end-of-life. A spent SPD looks identical to a healthy one unless it says so.
  • Documentation. Compliance certificate, test report and the marking on the label, kept with the panel schedule.

None of these six items is expensive. Each of them is the reason a protection scheme fails inspection or fails in service.

Frequently asked questions

Does a whole-house surge protector stop a lightning strike?

No. It absorbs the induced energy that a nearby strike pushes onto the wiring. A direct strike on the building needs a lightning rod, a down conductor and a good earth, with the SPD as the second layer.

Will it fix low voltage or brownouts?

No. An SPD only reacts to short spikes. A supply that sits at 100–140 V for hours needs an over/under-voltage protector to disconnect the load, or a voltage stabilizer to hold the output up.

How much discharge capacity does a house need?

On a 230 V single-phase supply the common Type 2 specification is 20 kA nominal and 40 kA maximum discharge current, with Uc rated at 275 V AC. Compare the voltage protection level as well, because that is what the appliance sees.

Do I still need surge-protection power strips?

Yes, as the second layer. A strip is a Type 3 device and cannot absorb a large surge alone; the Type 2 device at the panel is what takes the energy.

Do solar panels need a separate device?

Yes. The DC side of a PV array needs a DC-rated SPD — 1000 V DC is the common rating for modern strings — installed in the combiner box or at the inverter DC input.

Protecting a house, a farm building or a PV array?

Send the system voltage, phase configuration and a photo of the panel. We manufacture PV surge protective devices, lightning arrester rods and over/under-voltage protectors under ISO 9001 quality management, and we will tell you which layer your installation is missing.
