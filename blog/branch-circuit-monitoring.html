Branch Circuit Monitoring: Split-Core CTs and DIN-Rail Meters, Circuit by Circuit

ET
ET Engineering Team November 18, 2026 11 min read

Branch circuit monitoring measures each outgoing circuit separately, instead of measuring only the main incomer. A current transformer goes around every branch conductor, each CT feeds a channel of a meter that accepts CT inputs, and the result is a per-circuit record of current, power and energy. The main meter tells you what the building consumes. Branch monitoring tells you which circuit consumed it, and at what hour.

That difference decides what questions the data can answer. A single main meter supports billing and a monthly total. It cannot tell you that one chiller branch runs at 40 A between two and five in the morning, or that a tenant's sub-board has been creeping upward for six weeks. Those are the findings that pay for the instrumentation, and they need circuit-level resolution.

This guide covers the practical side of panel-level monitoring: what to measure, how a CT-per-branch scheme compares with a meter-per-branch scheme, how to choose the split-core CT that makes installation possible without disconnecting anything, and the mistakes that turn a fully instrumented panel into a set of readings nobody trusts.

What branch level measurement actually gives you

A split-core CT around a branch conductor produces a secondary current proportional to the branch current. The meter converts that into current, and — using the voltage input it already has — into power and, integrated over time, energy. Three things follow from that chain, and each has a practical consequence.

  • Current is measured directly; power and energy are derived. A CT plus a current-only channel gives you load profile and utilisation. Power and energy need the voltage reference, which is why a CT on a branch with no voltage input to the meter gives current data only.
  • The measurement is per phase. A three-phase branch needs three CTs and three channels, or a single-phase reading that tells only part of the story. A three-phase load measured on one phase looks like a balanced load and is not.
  • Small currents are the hard part. A 250/5 CT monitoring a circuit that idles at a few amps is working at the bottom of its range, where the accuracy class is least comfortable. This is the most common reason branch data looks noisier than main-meter data.

CT per branch or meter per branch

Both architectures exist, and the choice is about panel space, wiring volume and how much resolution the analysis needs.

CT per branch, shared meterMeter per branch
Panel spaceA CT around each conductor plus one meter unitA meter module per way, which adds width on every way
WiringOne secondary pair per branch back to the meterNo secondary runs, but a bus and auxiliary supply per module
RetrofitSplit-core CTs clamp on without disconnecting the conductorThe circuit usually has to be interrupted to insert the module
ResolutionSet by the number of channels the meter providesOne circuit per module, so resolution is complete by construction
Failure scopeA meter fault affects every channel it servesA module fault affects one circuit
Best fitDense panels, retrofits, tenant sub-boardsNew build where space is planned and circuits are few

In existing boards the split-core CT route usually wins on the single argument that nothing has to be switched off to install it. That matters more than the wiring volume, because a monitoring project that needs a shutdown on every board tends not to get approved.

Choosing the split-core CT for a branch

The CT is where most branch monitoring projects are decided, because it has to fit around a conductor that is already in place.

DecisionHow to settle itWhat goes wrong otherwise
RatioFrom the branch's design current, not the breaker frame sizeAn oversized CT reads poorly at light load, which is where most branches spend their time
Window sizeMeasure the conductor and its insulation, then leave clearance to close the coreA CT that nearly fits cannot be closed squarely, and an open gap ruins accuracy
Accuracy classClass 0.5 where the data feeds billing or tenant recharge; class 1.0 where it feeds trend analysisPaying for class 0.5 and then burying it in a long secondary run
BurdenTotal the secondary loop and the meter channel, then compare with the CT's VAClass on the label no longer describes the installed measurement
Voltage ratingAt or above the system voltage of the panelA low-voltage body used where it was never qualified

Two of these are worth stating plainly. First, the ratio follows the load, not the switchgear: a 63 A way that actually carries 12 A wants a CT sized for that 12 A, not one chosen to match the breaker label. Second, class 0.5 is only meaningful if the secondary run respects the burden — otherwise the money spent on the class is spent on a number that does not reach the meter.

Where the CTs go, and keeping the panel serviceable

Physical layout decides whether the installation survives its first maintenance visit. The CTs go on the branch conductors downstream of the protective device, oriented so that P1 faces the supply. Group the secondary pairs, label each one at both ends with the circuit it belongs to, and route them away from contactor and drive cabling.

Leave the CTs accessible. A split-core CT buried behind a loom cannot be re-opened for a ratio change or a polarity correction without dismantling the panel, and the correction will be needed — branch circuits get reassigned, and a monitoring scheme that assumes the panel will never change is a scheme that ages badly. Label the secondary at the CT end as well as at the meter end: the person diagnosing a wrong reading is usually standing at one end with the drawing for the other.

From readings to decisions

Branch data earns its keep in four places, and all four need circuit-level resolution rather than a building total.

  • Base load. The overnight floor of each branch shows what runs when the site is empty. A branch that never drops is either a genuine continuous load or something that should not be running.
  • Load shifting. Branches with high daytime peaks and low night demand are the candidates for moving duty cycles, and the data shows which ones are worth moving.
  • Tenant and departmental recharge. Sub-boards with their own CTs turn a shared bill into an allocation with an evidence trail, which is why class 0.5 matters in that application.
  • Capacity and maintenance. A branch trending upward against its rating is a decision waiting to be made, and a branch whose current profile changed shape often has a mechanical problem behind it.

The failure modes that make branch data useless

SymptomCauseFix
All branches read low and similarCTs oversized for the actual branch currentsRe-specify ratios against measured current, not breaker rating
One channel reads zeroSecondary pair not landed, or landed on the wrong channelCheck continuity and channel mapping at the meter
Readings jitter on long runsUnscreened secondary picking up switching noiseTwist or screen the pair, and shorten where possible
Branch totals do not add up to the mainMissing branches, or a CT closed on the wrong conductorReconcile circuit by circuit with the panel schedule
Consistent negative valuesCT reversed on the conductorRe-clamp with P1 facing the supply

The reconciliation test is the one to run first. If the sum of the branch readings does not track the incomer within a sensible margin, the problem is in the mapping, not in the meter, and no amount of analysis on the branch data will fix it.

Frequently asked questions

What is branch circuit monitoring?

It is the measurement of each outgoing circuit in a distribution board separately, using a current transformer around every branch conductor and a meter that accepts those CT inputs. It produces per-circuit current, power and energy instead of a single building total.

Can I add branch monitoring to an existing panel without switching it off?

Usually yes, with split-core CTs. They open, clamp around an installed conductor and close again, so nothing has to be disconnected. The meter still needs a mounting position, an auxiliary supply and its voltage reference, which may require a short outage for that part of the work.

Why does a branch reading look noisy compared with the main meter?

Because branch currents are small relative to the CT fitted. A CT sized for a much larger current operates at the bottom of its range on a lightly loaded branch, where the accuracy class is least comfortable. Matching the ratio to the measured current fixes most of it.

Do the branch readings have to add up to the main meter?

They should track it within a sensible margin once every circuit is instrumented. A large gap usually means a branch is missing from the scheme, a CT is closed on the wrong conductor, or a channel is mapped to the wrong circuit — mapping problems, not meter problems.
