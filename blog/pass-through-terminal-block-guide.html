Pass-Through Terminal Block: Conductor Path, Bridging and Accessories

ET
ET Engineering Team November 14, 2026 9 min read

A pass-through terminal block connects the conductor that enters one side of the body to the conductor that leaves the other. There is no switching element and no fuse inside it: the two sides are permanently joined through a metal bar, and the block exists to provide a fixed, testable, re-terminable joint in the middle of a run.

The name is often used loosely, so it is worth stating what it does not mean. A pass-through block is not a barrier strip, it is not a disconnect terminal, and it is not a multi-level block that happens to carry a conductor across. Those are three different parts with three different failure modes, and specifying the wrong one is the quiet cause of a panel that cannot be isolated for maintenance.

The conductor path, and why it matters

The defining feature is a continuous conductive path from the inlet clamp to the outlet clamp. On a rail-mounted block that path is a shaped copper or brass element inside the moulding, with a screw or spring clamp at each end. The conductor does not terminate at the block and restart on the other side: it passes through, electrically speaking, and the block is the joint.

Two practical consequences follow. The first is that the block introduces resistance and heat into a run that would otherwise be one continuous conductor, so a long chain of pass-through blocks is not electrically free even though it is mechanically convenient. The second is that the joint is now a serviceable point: a technician can open one side, test, and re-terminate without disturbing the rest of the run. That is the real reason the part exists.

Inlet and outlet are a specification item

When a datasheet says a block has copper inlet and outlet, it is telling you the current path is metal at both ends rather than relying on a plated steel screw to carry current through the body. That distinction matters at higher currents and in installations where the block will run warm, because the screw is a clamping element, not a conductor.

It also matters for the drawing. A block rated for a conductor range at one inlet size and a different outlet size is doing real work: it lets a larger incoming feeder be reduced to a smaller outgoing conductor at a defined point, with a clamp sized for each. Specifying a single wire range for both sides throws that capability away and forces a separate reducer.

Pass-through, barrier, disconnect and multi-level compared

These four are the family that buyers most often substitute for one another, and the substitution usually happens at the quotation stage rather than on the drawing.

Pass-throughPermanently joined inlet to outletMaking a fixed, testable joint in a runOverheats when a long chain is loaded without derating

The substitution that causes most trouble is using a pass-through block where a disconnect was intended. The two look almost identical on a rail, the terminal count is the same, and the difference only appears when someone tries to isolate a circuit and finds there is nothing to open. If the maintenance procedure requires a visible break, the part must be a disconnect, and that requirement belongs on the drawing rather than in a verbal instruction.

Bridging: jumpers and cross-connections

Because a pass-through block is a joint, a row of them can be made into a common node by bridging. A jumper inserted across adjacent blocks ties their inlet sides together, which is how a single feed is distributed to several outgoing circuits without a separate busbar.

Bridging is convenient and it is also where current ratings quietly break. A jumper rated for the current of one block is not necessarily rated for the sum of the circuits it now feeds, and the first block in a bridged row carries the total. Two habits keep this honest: size the first position for the total load rather than the per-circuit load, and write the bridge on the drawing so the next person to modify the panel knows which positions are already commoned. An undocumented bridge is indistinguishable from a wiring error.

Test access and maintainability

A pass-through block is a natural test point, and the family has evolved parts specifically for that: test terminal blocks that accept a probe or a plug through the body, and disconnect variants that open the circuit for a measurement. If the commissioning procedure involves measuring at the terminal rather than at the instrument, the test access should be a specified feature rather than something improvised with a probe against a live screw head.

The test terminal block guide covers the probe-access variants; the point here is that test access is a specification decision taken with the block, not an accessory added afterwards.

The accessories that decide whether the row is serviceable

A rail-mounted row is a system, and the blocks are only part of it. Four accessories separate a row that can be maintained from one that has to be rebuilt.

  • End stops. They stop the row sliding along the rail when a conductor is pushed in. Without them the row migrates and the jumpers work loose.
  • End plates. They close the open side of the first and last block, which is where fingers and tools reach the live parts.
  • Marker strips and terminal markers. The row is only maintainable if each position can be identified from the drawing. Marker provision should be checked before the blocks are ordered, because not every body accepts a marker.
  • Jumper bars and partition plates. Jumpers make common nodes; partitions restore separation between groups that must not share a node.

Ordering the accessories with the blocks is cheaper than ordering them after the panel is built, and it avoids the common outcome where a row is commissioned with end stops borrowed from another project.

Selection checklist

  • Function — pass-through, disconnect, test or multi-level. Decide this from the maintenance procedure, not from what is in stock.
  • Conductor range at each side, separately, if the run reduces in size at the block.
  • Current per block and the total for the first position of any bridged group.
  • Rail standard and the mounting depth available in the enclosure.
  • Accessories — end stops, end plates, markers, jumpers, partitions.
  • Marking scheme, taken from the drawing so the row can be traced in service.

The rail standard and the accessory set are covered in more depth in the DIN rail terminal block guide, and the terminal blocks identification chart is a faster route if you are identifying an existing row rather than specifying a new one.

The last row is the one that is hardest to notice, because a missing partition does not fail at commissioning. It reduces the distance between two groups that the design assumed were separated, and the installation keeps working until the day it does not.

Frequently asked questions

What is a pass-through terminal block used for?

It makes a fixed, testable joint in the middle of a conductor run. The conductor entering one side is permanently joined to the conductor leaving the other, so the block provides a re-terminable connection point rather than a switching or protecting function.

What is the difference between a pass-through and a disconnect terminal block?

A pass-through block joins the two sides permanently. A disconnect block joins them through a removable link or a sliding blade, so the circuit can be opened for test or maintenance. They look almost identical on a rail, and the difference only becomes visible when someone tries to isolate the circuit.

Can pass-through blocks be bridged to share one feed?

Yes. A jumper across adjacent blocks makes them a common node, which is how one feed is distributed to several outgoing circuits. The first block in the bridged group then carries the total current, so it has to be sized for the sum of the circuits rather than for one circuit.

Which accessories does a rail row need?

End stops to stop the row sliding, end plates to close the open sides, marker strips so each position can be traced from the drawing, and jumpers or partitions depending on whether groups must share a node or be kept separate. Ordering them with the blocks is cheaper than adding them after the panel is built.
