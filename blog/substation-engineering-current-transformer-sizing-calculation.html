Substation Engineering: Current Transformer Sizing Calculation Guide

ET
ET Engineering Team October 20, 2026 11 min read

The Critical Role of Precise Current Transformer Sizing in Electrical Engineering

In electrical substations, industrial power plants, and utility switchgear, current transformers (CTs) serve as the sensory nervous system: stepping down thousands of amperes of primary line current into safe, standardized secondary currents (5A or 1A) for revenue meters and protection relays.

However, an improperly sized current transformer represents a catastrophic engineering hazard:

  • Under-sizing the CT burden: Connecting a 15VA metering circuit to a 5VA CT drives the magnetic core into continuous saturation, introducing massive ratio and phase errors that cause tens of thousands of dollars in commercial unmetered billing losses.
  • Under-sizing protection CT knee-point voltage ($V_k$): During an external through-fault, an under-sized protection CT saturates within milliseconds, producing distorted, zeroed secondary currents that cause differential protection relays to trip falsely or overcurrent breakers to fail to operate, resulting in switchgear arc-flash destruction.

This comprehensive engineering guide outlines the exact step-by-step mathematical calculations required to size primary ratings, secondary outputs, total circuit burdens, and knee-point voltages under international standards IEC 61869-2 and IEEE C57.13.

Step-by-Step Sizing Methodology

  1. Step 1: Primary Current Calculation ($I_{pn}$): Calculate full-load current from maximum kVA rating, apply a continuous $1.25 imes$ safety factor for future load expansion, and select the next standard commercial primary rating.
  2. Step 2: Secondary Current Selection (5A vs. 1A): Evaluate the secondary wire loop distance from the switchgear to the control room. If the distance exceeds 30 meters, mandate a 1A secondary to reduce $I^2R$ lead burden by 96%.
  3. Step 3: Total Secondary Burden Calculation ($S_b$ in VA): Sum the wire lead resistance ($R_{ ext{lead}} = ho \cdot rac{2L}{A}$), digital meter impedance, and terminal contact resistances. Ensure total connected VA is between 25% and 100% of the CT's rated burden to maintain class accuracy.
  4. Step 4: Verification of Accuracy Limit Factor (ALF) and Knee-Point Voltage ($V_k$): For protection CTs, ensure the core can develop sufficient secondary electromotive force ($V_k$) under maximum prospective fault current ($I_{ ext{sc}}$) without entering magnetic saturation before the protection relay trips.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the step-by-step formula to calculate the primary current rating ($I_{pn}$) of a Current Transformer?

To size the primary current rating ($I_{pn}$), follow these standard engineering steps: <ol><li><strong>Calculate the Full Load Current ($I_{ ext{FLC}}$):</strong> For a 3-phase system: $I_{ ext{FLC}} = rac{P_{ ext{kVA}} imes 1000}{\sqrt{3} imes V_{ ext{LL}}}$. For example, on an $11\, ext{kV}$ system with a $2000\, ext{kVA}$ transformer: $I_{ ext{FLC}} = rac{2000 imes 1000}{1.732 imes 11000} pprox 105\, ext{A}$.</li><li><strong>Apply the Continuous Overload Sizing Factor ($1.25 imes$):</strong> $I_{ ext{target}} = 1.25 imes 105\, ext{A} = 131.2\, ext{A}$.</li><li><strong>Select the Next Standard Commercial Primary Rating:</strong> Standard IEC ratings are 100A, 150A, 200A, 250A, 300A, 400A, 500A, 600A, 800A, 1000A. In this case, select a <strong>150/5A</strong> or <strong>150/1A</strong> CT. Selecting 150A ensures the CT operates in its linear, high-accuracy zone (between 50% and 100% of rating) during normal operation without saturating during peak overloads.</li></ol>

Why should you choose a 1A secondary instead of a 5A secondary for long cable runs?

The secondary lead burden loss is proportional to the square of the current ($P = I^2 R$). <ul><li><strong>With a 5A secondary:</strong> $I^2 = 5^2 = 25$. A $1\,\Omega$ lead resistance imposes a massive $25 imes 1 = 25\, ext{VA}$ burden on the CT secondary! This exceeds the rated burden of most standard CTs (5VA, 10VA, or 15VA), causing severe measurement ratio errors and core saturation.</li><li><strong>With a 1A secondary:</strong> $I^2 = 1^2 = 1$. The identical $1\,\Omega$ lead resistance imposes only $1 imes 1 = 1\, ext{VA}$ burden!</li></ul>Choosing a 1A secondary reduces secondary wiring losses by <strong>96%</strong>, allowing smaller cable cross-sections ($2.5\, ext{mm}^2$ vs $6\, ext{mm}^2$) and permitting CTs to be located hundreds of meters away in outdoor substation switchyards without overloading the secondary core.

How do you calculate the Knee-Point Voltage ($V_k$) for a protection class Current Transformer?

For protection CTs (such as differential and distance protection under Class PX / Class 5P), the knee-point voltage $V_k$ is the point on the excitation curve where a $10\%$ increase in secondary voltage increases the excitation current by $50\%$. The sizing formula is: <p style="font-family:monospace;font-weight:bold;margin:10px 0;">V_k \ge 2 imes I_{f( ext{max})} imes rac{I_{sn}}{I_{pn}} imes (R_{ct} + 2R_{ ext{lead}} + R_{ ext{relay}})</p>Where: <ul><li>$I_{f( ext{max})}$ = Maximum prospective symmetrical primary short-circuit fault current (e.g. 25,000A).</li><li>$ rac{I_{sn}}{I_{pn}}$ = CT transformation turns ratio (e.g. 5/1000 = 1/200).</li><li>$R_{ct}$ = Internal secondary winding DC resistance at $75^\circ ext{C}$ (e.g. $0.5\,\Omega$).</li><li>$2R_{ ext{lead}}$ = Loop resistance of the secondary cables out and back (e.g. $0.4\,\Omega$).</li><li>$R_{ ext{relay}}$ = Internal impedance of the protection relay (typically $< 0.1\,\Omega$ for digital microprocessors).</li><li>The safety multiplier of 2 ensures that the CT core does not saturate during the initial fully asymmetrical DC offset component of the fault current.</li></ul>
