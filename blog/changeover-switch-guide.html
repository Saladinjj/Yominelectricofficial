Changeover Switches and Manual Transfer Switches: Choosing Safe Source Switching

ET
ET Engineering Team 8 min read September 8, 2026

A changeover switch — called a manual transfer switch in North America — is the device that decides which source feeds an installation: the utility, or a standby generator. It sounds trivial. It is the single component that, if specified wrong, can back-feed a generator into a dead grid and endanger line crews, or connect two sources in parallel and destroy both. This guide covers how they work, the variants that matter, and how to specify one correctly.

The one rule: break-before-make

A proper changeover switch never connects both sources at once. Its mechanism passes through an OFF position between the two ON positions — "break-before-make". This guarantees a dead interval during switching, which is what prevents parallel connection of utility and generator. Any device marketed as a changeover switch without a genuine centre-off or mechanically interlocked position is not fit for source switching. Verify the interlock physically before accepting a product.

Where each type belongs

  • Modular DIN-rail transfer switches (1P/2P, e.g. SF219G style, 63A): compact source selection inside a distribution board — utility vs generator for a single circuit or a small board, or switching between two feeders in a rental or commercial panel.
  • Rotary or lever changeover switches (3P/4P, 63-160A and above): whole-installation switching for homes, shops and small facilities with three-phase supplies.
  • PV changeover/isolation switches (DC or AC side): isolating or selecting between solar strings, inverters and the grid — a different duty (DC arc extinction) but the same interlock discipline.

Generator applications: the interlock question

For standby generators the switch must do three jobs: select the source, isolate the unused source completely, and make back-feed impossible by construction. In many markets the installation also requires that the generator neutral be handled per local code — some systems bond neutral at the generator, others at the service entrance; a 4-pole changeover that switches neutral is often the correct choice to avoid parallel neutral paths. State the pole count deliberately: 2-pole for single-phase without neutral switching, 4-pole where the neutral must transfer.

Sizing and ratings that matter

  • Current rating: at least the main protective device rating of the circuits being switched; for motor-heavy loads consider the starting current the contacts must make.
  • Voltage and utilization category: AC-23A or equivalent for mixed resistive/inductive loads; do not accept a device rated only for resistive duty on a motor load.
  • Making/breaking capacity: the switch must withstand the inrush of the largest load started through it.
  • Enclosure: IP rating for the mounting location; a board-mounted modular device differs from a wall-mounted enclosed switch.

Manual vs automatic: an honest boundary

A manual changeover requires a person to throw it — acceptable where someone is present when the grid fails, and the cheapest safe option. An automatic transfer switch (ATS) senses the failure and switches alone; it costs more and needs commissioning. The middle ground people ask about — "semi-automatic" — usually means a manual switch plus a generator with auto-start; that combination is safe only if the generator cannot energise the installation until the switch is thrown, so the interlock discipline still rests on the switch itself.

Installation and maintenance discipline

  • Mount upstream of the circuits to be backed up, downstream of the meter and main protection.
  • Label both positions and the sources permanently; ambiguity during an outage causes mistakes.
  • Exercise the mechanism quarterly — a changeover that has not moved in two years is a changeover that will stick during the one outage that matters.
  • Check terminal torque annually; thermal cycling loosens connections on switched loads.

Specification checklist

  • Pole count and whether neutral switches (2P / 3P / 4P).
  • Rated current and utilization category (AC-23A or stated equivalent).
  • Break-before-make interlock confirmed by construction.
  • Mounting: DIN rail modular, panel, or enclosed wall mount; IP rating.
  • Terminal type and conductor size range.
  • Certification marks for the destination market.
