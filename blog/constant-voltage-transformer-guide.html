Constant Voltage Transformer: How Ferroresonance Holds the Output Steady

ET
ET Engineering Team November 15, 2026 10 min read

A constant voltage transformer is a transformer that regulates by saturating its own magnetic core. There is no motor, no tap changer, no control board and nothing to calibrate. You feed it a ragged mains supply and it hands back an output that stays within about one percent, cycle after cycle, for as long as the input stays inside its designed window. That single property is why the device survives in laboratories, telecom huts and instrument cabinets long after cheaper electronic regulators have replaced it everywhere else.

The catch is equally simple: the window is narrow, the output waveform is not a clean sine wave, and the whole thing runs hot and hums. Understanding which of those three constraints your load can live with is the entire selection exercise.

What a constant voltage transformer actually is

The proper name is a ferroresonant transformer, and outside North America it is usually sold as a CVT, a constant voltage stabiliser, or simply a ferro-resonant supply. Physically it looks like an ordinary dry-type transformer with an unusually bulky core. Electrically it has three parts doing three different jobs:

  • A saturating primary winding that is deliberately designed to run into magnetic saturation on every half cycle, so the flux in the core is clipped rather than following the input voltage.
  • A resonant winding with a capacitor connected across it, forming a parallel tank tuned close to the supply frequency. This is what supplies the magnetising current the saturated core needs and keeps the resonance stable.
  • An output winding, magnetically coupled to the saturated section, that sees a nearly constant flux swing regardless of small changes at the input.

Because the output is taken from a separate winding, a CVT is also an isolation transformer by construction. That is a genuine side benefit in instrument work, and one reason the device keeps being specified even where a modern AVR would regulate better.

How ferroresonance produces a flat output

The mechanism is easiest to follow as a sequence rather than a formula.

  1. The core saturates early. As the input voltage rises through a half cycle, the core reaches saturation well before the peak. Beyond that point the flux can barely increase, so the output winding stops following the input and starts following the resonant tank instead.
  2. The tank stores the difference. The capacitor and the resonant winding exchange energy at supply frequency. Extra input voltage is absorbed as reactive energy in the tank rather than passed to the load; a dip in input voltage is made up from the same store.
  3. The result is a flat-topped wave. The output emerges as a quasi-square wave with a controlled rms value. Regulation is excellent, but the waveform carries third and fifth harmonic content that a pure sine wave does not.

That third point is where most real projects run into trouble. A CVT feeding a resistive heater or a linear power supply is fine. A CVT feeding a load that is sensitive to harmonic distortion, or a load whose own switch-mode supply interacts with the resonant tank, is where the datasheet and the site start to disagree.

Read the table as a trade, not a ranking. The CVT wins on speed and on having nothing to wear out. The servo AVR wins on input range, waveform quality and running cost. For most industrial buyers the second column is the one that matters.

Where a constant voltage transformer is genuinely the right answer

  • Frequency is stable but voltage is not. A CVT is at its best on a supply where the hertz are rock steady and only the volts wander, which describes most utility-fed instrument rooms.
  • The load cannot tolerate a correction gap. Because there is no motor to chase the error, the output is corrected inside the same cycle. A servo AVR takes seconds to travel, and during that travel the load sees the fault.
  • Nothing may be maintained. A sealed CVT in a remote cabinet has no brushes to inspect and no control card to fail. For a site visited twice a year, that is worth more than two points of efficiency.
  • Isolation is wanted anyway. If the design already calls for a double-wound isolating transformer ahead of sensitive equipment, a CVT folds that requirement and the regulation into one box.

Where it is the wrong answer

Four situations account for nearly every misapplication we see quoted.

  • Generator-fed sites. A standby generator rarely holds frequency inside the band a CVT is tuned to. As the hertz drift, the resonant tank detunes and the output either collapses or climbs. A servo AVR rides through frequency wander without complaint.
  • Deep-sag markets. Where the incoming supply routinely sits well below nominal, the CVT input window is exhausted before the load is. A wide-range servo stabiliser is the correct device, which is why our sizing guide starts from the measured input range rather than the load.
  • Motor starting and high inrush. A saturated core has little margin to supply a starting current several times rated. Either the CVT is oversized substantially, or the load trips it.
  • Switch-mode power supplies in bulk. Rows of electronic drivers present a nonlinear load whose current waveform interacts with the resonant tank. Voltage regulation can look fine on a meter while the load misbehaves.

Sizing: kVA, power factor and inrush

A CVT is rated in volt-amperes, and the rating assumes a load power factor in the ordinary lagging range. The relationship that matters is definitional rather than proprietary:

required VA = load watts ÷ load power factor

Then apply three margins in order. Add headroom for inrush if the load has motors or large capacitors. Add headroom if the load is nonlinear, because the harmonic current heats the winding without contributing to useful output. Add headroom for ambient, since a saturated core already runs warm and a hot enclosure shortens insulation life. Only after those three steps does the nameplate number mean anything.

The same arithmetic applies to any stabiliser, which is why our AVR guide uses an identical worksheet for servo and relay types.

Specification checklist before you send an RFQ

Seven lines turn a vague enquiry into a quotation that can be checked:

  • Measured input range, not the nominal voltage. Log it for a week if the site is remote.
  • Supply frequency and how stable it is, especially whether a generator can be the source.
  • Load list with watts and power factor, separated into resistive, motor and electronic loads.
  • Largest single inrush and whether loads start simultaneously.
  • Output waveform sensitivity — does anything downstream care about harmonic distortion?
  • Phase configuration and whether the neutral must be switched.
  • Ambient temperature and enclosure type, since a saturated core dissipates continuously.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a constant voltage transformer and an AVR?

A CVT regulates by driving its core into saturation and drawing on a resonant tank, so it has no moving parts and corrects within a cycle or two but produces a harmonic-rich output. An AVR corrects by changing transformer ratio, either with a servo motor or with relay-switched taps, so it accepts a much wider input range and passes a cleaner waveform but takes longer to respond.

Why does a constant voltage transformer hum and run warm?

Because the core is saturated on every half cycle by design. Saturation produces magnetostriction at twice the supply frequency, which is the hum, and continuous magnetising losses, which are the heat. Neither is a fault, but both must be allowed for in the enclosure and the ambient rating.

Can a constant voltage transformer run from a generator?

Only if the generator holds frequency closely. The resonant tank is tuned to 50 or 60 Hz, so drifting hertz detunes it and the output moves with it. Where a generator is a possible source, a servo-motor AVR is the safer specification.

Does a CVT give a clean sine wave output?

No. The output is a quasi-square wave with significant third and fifth harmonic content. It is stable in rms value, which is what most loads need, but it is not a low-distortion sine wave and should not be specified as one.
