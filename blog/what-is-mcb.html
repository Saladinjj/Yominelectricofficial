What Is MCB: Miniature Circuit Breaker Ratings and Curves

ET
ET Engineering Team November 22, 2026 9 min read

An MCB, or miniature circuit breaker, is a resettable automatic switch that mounts on a DIN rail and opens a circuit when current exceeds its rating for long enough to overheat the wiring, or when a short circuit drives current far above it. It replaces the fuse in almost every modern distribution board because it can be reset by hand instead of replaced, and because its trip behaviour is repeatable enough to be relied on in a protection study.

That definition is short, but most of the specification work hides inside it. A 32 A device with a B curve, a C curve and a 6 kA rating are three different products even though they look identical from the front, and choosing the wrong one produces either nuisance tripping or a device that cannot clear the fault it is installed to clear.

What the M in MCB actually means

The word miniature describes the form factor, not the duty. An MCB is built into a modular moulding a few tens of millimetres wide that clips onto a 35 mm DIN rail beside the terminals and contactors it protects. The whole device is sealed at the factory: there is no trip unit to swap, no setting dial and no serviceable arc chamber.

That is the practical line between an MCB and a moulded case circuit breaker. An MCCB also has a moulded case, but it is bolted rather than clipped, it opens to accept a different trip unit, and its settings can be adjusted on site. Above roughly 125 A the MCB form factor stops being available and the MCCB takes over.

DeviceTypical current rangeResets after a tripWhere it belongs
FuseUp to a few hundred ampsNo — element replacedWherever the highest breaking capacity per unit cost is needed
MCBUp to about 125 AYes — lever resetFinal circuits inside a distribution board
MCCBAbout 16 A to 1600 AYes — lever resetSub-mains, feeders, motor circuits
ACBAbout 630 A to 6300 AYes — often motor chargedMain incoming and bus-tie positions

Fuses have not disappeared, and the reason is worth knowing before specifying: a fuse is a single fusible element with nothing mechanical to fail, so a small fuse can interrupt a fault current that would destroy a small breaker. Where the prospective fault current at the terminals is higher than any MCB rating available, a fuse or a back-up arrangement is the honest answer.

Two trips inside one moulding

Every thermal-magnetic MCB contains two independent mechanisms in series, and understanding which one is operating explains almost every nuisance trip you will ever diagnose.

  • Thermal element. A bimetal strip heated by the load current bends gradually. Its job is overload protection, and because it is thermal it responds to duration, ambient temperature and how the device is enclosed.
  • Magnetic element. A solenoid that trips almost instantly once current passes a multiple of the rating. Its job is short-circuit protection, and it operates in milliseconds.

The consequences are practical rather than theoretical. A board full of breakers in a hot enclosure trips earlier than the same breakers on a bench, because the bimetal starts from a higher temperature. A load with a high inrush, such as a motor or a transformer, can cross the magnetic threshold on start even though its running current is well inside the rating. Both are selection problems, not product faults.

Trip curves: B, C and D

The curve letter describes where the magnetic trip operates, and it is the single most useful field on the device label after the current rating. The ranges below are the standardised bands under IEC 60898-1 for domestic and similar installations.

CurveMagnetic trip bandTypical loadWhat it tolerates poorly
B3 to 5 times rated currentResistive loads, lighting, socket outlets in domestic workMotor and transformer inrush
C5 to 10 times rated currentGeneral commercial and light industrial circuits, small motorsVery high inrush equipment
D10 to 20 times rated currentWelding sets, large transformers, compressors, X-ray equipmentLong cable runs where the earth loop is weak

The trade-off runs in both directions. A higher curve letter means fewer nuisance trips on start-up, but it also means the breaker needs a larger fault current before it trips magnetically, which can leave a long cable run protected only by the slower thermal element. That is why the curve cannot be selected from the load type alone: the earth fault loop impedance at the far end of the circuit has to be able to deliver the magnetic trip current.

Breaking capacity, and why the number on the front matters

The figure printed in a rectangle on the front of the device, typically 3, 4.5, 6 or 10 kA, is the rated short-circuit capacity. It states the largest prospective fault current the device can interrupt safely and still be fit to use afterwards. A device asked to clear more than that may still open, but it is not required to survive doing so, and the enclosure around it may not survive either.

Rated capacityWhere it is normally adequateWhere it is not
3 kAFinal circuits far from the transformer, domestic boardsBoards close to a distribution transformer
6 kAMost commercial distribution boardsMain switchboards with a high fault level
10 kABoards fed directly from a transformer or a large sub-mainAnything needing a cascaded back-up calculation

Two related figures appear in IEC 60898-1: Icn, the rated short-circuit capacity, and Ics, the service short-circuit capacity. The second is the current the device can interrupt and then continue to carry load. Where a datasheet quotes only one number, treat the higher one as the one-time figure and confirm the service value before comparing prices.

Poles and the neutral question

Pole count is where residential and industrial practice diverge most often, and it changes what the enclosure has to provide.

ConfigurationWhat opensTypical use
1PPhase onlySingle-phase final circuits where the neutral is solidly linked at the board
1P+NPhase, with the neutral followingSingle-phase circuits where the neutral must be isolated with the phase
2PBoth poles protectedSingle-phase circuits requiring protection in both conductors
3PThree phasesThree-phase loads without a neutral
3P+N, 4PThree phases plus neutralThree-phase circuits with a distributed neutral, or where full isolation is required

An MCB only protects what passes through it. A 1P device on a circuit whose neutral is switched elsewhere leaves the neutral unprotected, and a device whose neutral pole is a solid link rather than a protected pole will not clear a neutral-to-earth fault. Writing the pole configuration on the order is as important as writing the current.

A DC MCB is not an AC MCB

Direct current has no natural zero crossing, so an arc drawn when the contacts open does not extinguish itself the way it does on alternating current. A DC-rated miniature circuit breaker solves this with a magnetic blow-out arrangement and, in most designs, by asking several poles to break the arc in series. That is why a DC device carries a voltage rating that depends on how the poles are connected.

YOMIN's photovoltaic range reflects the two common cases: a 500 V DC two-pole breaker for smaller strings, and a 1000 V DC device offered from one to four poles for larger arrays. Polarity matters on these devices. Reversing the supply and load terminals on a polarised DC breaker compromises the arc extinction path, which is why the markings have to be followed rather than assumed. The same range is listed under solar and PV products.

Selection checklist

  • Rated current from the cable it protects, not from the appliance nameplate. The cable is what overheats.
  • Curve from the load's inrush behaviour, then verified against the earth fault loop at the far end of the circuit.
  • Breaking capacity from the prospective fault current at that point in the installation, not from the board's main device.
  • Poles from what must be isolated: phase only, or phase and neutral.
  • Ambient and enclosure, because a bank of breakers side by side in a sealed box derates.
  • Standard — IEC 60898-1 for household and similar installations, IEC 60947-2 where the device is applied industrially.

Where an MCB stops being the right answer

Three situations come up repeatedly. Where the prospective fault current exceeds the highest capacity available in the form factor, a back-up fuse or a higher-rated upstream device is needed and the combination has to be checked as a pair rather than assumed. Where selectivity between an upstream and downstream device matters, two MCBs of adjacent ratings rarely coordinate and the manufacturer's tables decide the question. And where a motor starts under heavy load, the thermal element of a standard MCB may not ride through the start, which is what motor protection breakers exist for.

If you are comparing device families rather than a single rating, the air circuit breaker guide covers the top of the same hierarchy, and what is fuse covers the alternative at the bottom.

For a specification question, send the system voltage, the cable size, the load type and the prospective fault current. Those four figures decide the current rating, the curve and the breaking capacity, and they are the difference between a board that trips for a reason and one that trips for no reason at all.

Frequently asked questions

What does MCB stand for?

MCB stands for miniature circuit breaker: a resettable automatic switch in a modular DIN rail housing that opens a circuit on overload or short circuit. The word miniature describes the form factor, not the duty.

What is the difference between a B and a C curve MCB?

The curve letter sets where the magnetic trip operates. Under IEC 60898-1 a B curve trips magnetically between three and five times its rating, and a C curve between five and ten times, so C tolerates more inrush from motors and transformers but needs a stronger fault current to trip instantly.

Do I need an MCB or a fuse?

An MCB resets by hand and is repeatable, which suits final circuits in a distribution board. A fuse is a single fusible element with no mechanism to fail and can interrupt a larger fault current for its size, so it stays the right answer where the prospective fault current exceeds the available MCB breaking capacity.

Can an MCB be used on DC?

Only if it is rated for direct current. DC has no natural zero crossing, so the arc does not self-extinguish; a DC-rated device uses magnetic blow-out and usually several poles in series, which is why its voltage rating depends on the pole connection. YOMIN builds 500 V DC two-pole and 1000 V DC one to four-pole versions.
