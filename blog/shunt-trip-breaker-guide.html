Shunt Trip Breaker Guide: Wiring, Trip Sources and Selection

ET
ET Engineering Team October 26, 2026 9 min read

A shunt trip breaker is a circuit breaker fitted with a small solenoid coil that opens the breaker when a control voltage is applied to it. The coil does the mechanical work of pressing the trip bar, so a fire alarm panel, an emergency stop button or a building management system can disconnect a circuit without anyone standing at the switchboard. The breaker keeps its normal overcurrent protection; the shunt trip simply adds a second, remote way to open it.

That definition is easy. Getting one installed so that it works on the day it is needed is harder, because most shunt trip failures are wiring failures rather than device failures. The coil is energised from a control circuit that somebody else designs, and the two designs are rarely checked against each other.

What the coil actually does

The shunt trip release, sometimes called a trip coil or shunt release, is an accessory that bolts onto the breaker mechanism. When the control voltage reaches the coil, the plunger strikes the trip latch and the breaker opens through its normal mechanism. Because it uses the mechanism rather than forcing the contacts apart, the breaker still opens at the speed its design allows and still clears any current that happens to be flowing.

Two consequences follow. First, the shunt trip can open a breaker carrying load, which is why it is acceptable for emergency disconnection and not merely for isolation. Second, the coil is a short-duty component. It is built to deliver one decisive stroke, not to sit energised for hours.

Shunt trip or undervoltage release?

These two accessories are constantly confused, and choosing the wrong one produces a breaker that behaves in exactly the opposite way to what the designer intended.

PropertyShunt trip releaseUndervoltage release
Signal that opens the breakerVoltage applied to the coilVoltage lost from the coil
Coil state in normal serviceDe-energised, waitingContinuously energised, holding
Behaviour on control supply failureNo trip — the breaker stays closedTrips — fails safe
Typical useFire alarm interlock, remote e-stop, load sheddingMotor circuits, safety circuits, supply monitoring
Can the breaker be closed without the coil circuit?YesNo — the release must be energised first

The practical rule: if you want the breaker to open because something happened, specify a shunt trip. If you want the breaker to open because something stopped happening, specify an undervoltage release. A fire alarm interlock is the first case. A conveyor safety circuit that must drop out when its monitoring relay loses power is the second.

Where a shunt trip earns its cost

  • Fire alarm interlock. The most common application by a wide margin. On alarm, the shunt trip opens the incoming or distribution device so that firefighters are not working around live conductors.
  • Remote emergency stop. A stop button at a workstation or a loading dock that must isolate a remote panel.
  • Load shedding and peak control. A controller opens selected non-essential feeders when demand approaches a contracted maximum.
  • Generator and changeover interlocks. Preventing a breaker from being closed onto a source that is not available.
  • Commercial kitchen and workshop equipment. A single gas or fire detection signal that must kill power to a group of cooking or machining circuits.

In each case the value is the same: the person who needs the power off is not the person standing at the panel, and the delay of walking to the panel is the risk being designed out.

Wiring the coil so it survives

This is where most installations go wrong. The coil circuit is a small control circuit and it is easy to treat as an afterthought, but it carries the whole reliability of the function.

  1. Confirm the coil voltage before anything else. Releases are made for different control voltages, and a 24 V coil connected to a 230 V control circuit is destroyed on the first test. Read the label on the release itself, not the breaker nameplate.
  2. Feed the coil from a dedicated circuit with its own protection. A small fuse or miniature breaker in the control circuit limits the damage if the coil or its wiring fails. Taking the feed from an unrelated circuit means a fault in the coil can take out something more important.
  3. Use a momentary contact, not a maintained one. The trip command should be a pulse. A switch that stays closed keeps the coil energised after the breaker has already opened.
  4. Interrupt the coil with an auxiliary contact. Fit a normally-closed auxiliary contact in series with the coil so that the circuit is broken the moment the breaker opens. This single detail is what stops coils burning out, and it also prevents the coil from holding the mechanism in a state that blocks reclosing.
  5. Keep control wiring out of power conduits. Induced voltages on a long control run can be enough to nuisance-trip a sensitive release. Use screened cable where the run is long or passes close to variable-speed drives.
  6. Label the function at both ends. A breaker that opens for no apparent reason costs hours of fault-finding. A label reading "shunt trip — fire alarm zone 3" costs nothing.

Specifying the accessory with the breaker

Shunt trips are accessories, and accessories ordered later cost more, arrive later, and occasionally cannot be fitted to the breaker already on site. The cheapest moment to specify one is on the original order.

AccessoryWhat it gives youOrder with the breaker?
Shunt trip releaseRemote electrical opening on commandYes — field fitting is possible but not always economic
Undervoltage releaseOpening on loss of control supplyYes — same reason
Auxiliary contactsStatus signalling and coil interlockingYes — needed for the coil circuit itself
Motor operatorRemote electrical closing and resettingYes — determines the front-frame variant

When you send an enquiry, state the coil voltage and whether it is AC or DC, the number of auxiliary contacts, and the control source that will drive the release. Those three answers remove almost all of the ambiguity, and they are the three things a supplier cannot infer from the breaker rating alone.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a shunt trip and an undervoltage release?

A shunt trip releases the breaker when voltage is applied to its coil, so it is a deliberate command from an operator or a detection system. An undervoltage release releases the breaker when its coil loses voltage, so it is a continuous monitoring function that trips on supply failure.

Can a shunt trip coil stay energised after the breaker opens?

It should not. Use an auxiliary contact on the breaker to cut the coil circuit as soon as the main contacts open. Leaving the coil energised burns it out and can leave the breaker unable to be reclosed until the control circuit is repaired.

Does a shunt trip breaker still protect against overload?

Yes. The shunt trip is an additional release mechanism added to a breaker that already has its own overcurrent protection. It does not replace the thermal-magnetic or electronic trip unit, it adds a remote command path on top of it.
