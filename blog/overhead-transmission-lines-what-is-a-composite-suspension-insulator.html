Overhead Transmission & Distribution: What Is a Composite Suspension Insulator?

ET
ET Engineering Team October 12, 2026 10 min read

The Modern Technological Revolution in High-Voltage Overhead Transmission Line Insulation

Across national electrical power transmission grids, regional sub-transmission networks, and renewable wind and solar interconnection corridors operating at 33kV, 66kV, and 110kV, high-voltage overhead lines transport bulk electrical energy over hundreds of kilometers of rugged terrain.

Along these transmission corridors, line insulators must perform two demanding, simultaneous functions: mechanically supporting heavy multi-ton spans of conductor wire under gale-force winds and heavy ice loading, while providing flawless dielectric electrical insulation against grounded steel towers.

For more than a century, utilities relied on heavy strings of porcelain or toughened glass disc insulators. However, porcelain strings are heavy, fragile, and vulnerable to catastrophic electrical flashover outages when coastal salt fog or desert sandstorm dust accumulates on their hydrophilic surfaces.

The Composite Polymer Suspension & Tension Long-Rod Insulator (Model FXBW Series) represents the definitive modern standard: lightweight, high-strength insulators built from aerospace-grade fiberglass core rods and hydrophobic silicone rubber weather housings that eliminate pollution flashovers and reduce installation labor by over 70%.

Engineering Anatomy: Inside a Model FXBW Composite Suspension Long-Rod Insulator

  1. Corrosion-Resistant ECR Fiberglass Core Rod: Pultruded from electrical-grade, boron-free glass fibers impregnated with high-grade epoxy resin. Delivers tensile strength exceeding 1,000 MPa—stronger than structural steel—while providing superior resistance to brittle fracture and acid attack.
  2. High-Temperature Vulcanized (HTV) Silicone Rubber Housing: Injection-molded seamlessly over the core rod under high pressure. Exhibits natural hydrophobicity (water-repellency) and unique hydrophobic transfer, migrating silicone oils into dust and salt layers to prevent conductive water film formation.
  3. Aerodynamic Alternating Shed Profile: Alternating larger and smaller shed diameters optimize creepage distance (≥ 31 mm/kV) while allowing self-cleaning by natural wind and rain, preventing ice-bridging and dust build-up.
  4. Hot-Dip Galvanized Forged Steel End Fittings: Heavy-duty forged steel ball-and-socket (IEC 60120) or clevis-and-tongue fittings hot-dip galvanized to ASTM A153 for maximum corrosion resistance, swaged to the rod via computerized radial crimping.
  5. Aluminum Alloy Corona Grading Rings: Installed on insulators operating at 66kV and 110kV to smooth electrical field gradients, prevent surface corona discharge, and protect silicone sheds from electrical degradation.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a Composite Suspension Insulator and where is it used on overhead transmission grids?

A Composite Suspension Insulator—commonly known as a polymer suspension insulator, silicone long-rod insulator, or non-ceramic insulator (NCI)—is a specialized high-voltage insulating tension device designed to mechanically suspend energized transmission conductors while electrically insulating them from grounded steel lattice towers, wooden poles, or substation crossarms. It is utilized extensively across utility power grids: <ul><li><strong>1. Suspension Towers (Straight Runs):</strong> Hung vertically from tower crossarms, supporting the massive vertical weight and wind lateral loads of bare aluminum conductor steel reinforced (ACSR) or all-aluminum alloy conductors (AAAC).</li><li><strong>2. Tension &amp; Dead-End Towers:</strong> Mounted horizontally or obliquely in dead-end tension assemblies, resisting full longitudinal line tension (70kN to 160kN) where the transmission line terminates, crosses highways or rivers, or navigates sharp directional angles.</li><li><strong>3. Coastal, Desert &amp; Heavy Industrial Corridors:</strong> Deployed in high-salinity coastal areas, desert sandstorm zones, and chemical industrial corridors where traditional ceramic insulators suffer catastrophic pollution flashover outages.</li></ul>

Why are composite long-rod insulators replacing traditional porcelain and glass disc insulator strings?

For over a century, high-voltage lines relied on strings of porcelain or toughened glass cap-and-pin disc insulators. However, utilities worldwide are rapidly migrating to composite silicone long-rod insulators for four decisive engineering reasons: <ul><li><strong>1. Dramatic Weight Reduction (Up to 80% Lighter):</strong> A 110kV porcelain disc string weighs 40 to 60 kg, requiring heavy cranes and strenuous lineman handling. An equivalent 110kV composite insulator weighs only 5 to 8 kg, drastically reducing tower structural steel weight and accelerating helicopter or bucket truck installation.</li><li><strong>2. Superior Hydrophobic Flashover Immunity:</strong> Ceramic surfaces become hydrophilic when wet, allowing conductive water film and salt/dust layers to cause violent electrical flashovers. Silicone rubber exhibits permanent <strong>hydrophobicity (water-repellency)</strong> and transfers hydrophobic low-molecular-weight silicone oils into the accumulated pollution layer, causing moisture to bead into isolated droplets and preventing leakage current flow.</li><li><strong>3. High Vandalism &amp; Shatter Resistance:</strong> Porcelain and glass insulators are brittle and easily shattered by gunshots, vandalism, flying rocks, or mechanical impacts during transport. Composite insulators absorb shock without cracking or shattering.</li><li><strong>4. Zero Zero-Value Insulator Degradation:</strong> Porcelain discs suffer from hidden microscopic internal puncture cracks that necessitate regular, costly on-line voltage distribution testing. Composite rods are solid and immune to internal punctures.</li></ul>

How does the swaged forged steel end fitting transfer massive tensile forces without slipping off the fiberglass core rod?

The interface between the steel end fitting and the fiberglass rod is the critical mechanical load-bearing point. YOMIN FXBW Composite Insulators utilize high-precision <strong>multi-directional radial swaging (crimping) technology</strong>: A computerized 24-point circumferential swaging press compresses the hot-dip galvanized forged steel sleeve radially into the fiberglass rod. Precision acoustic emission monitoring sensors verify that the crimping force permanently locks the steel into the rod surface without crushing the internal glass fibers. This creates a mechanical grip that reliably exceeds the Specified Mechanical Load (e.g., 100kN or 160kN) without slippage across decades of thermal cycling.
