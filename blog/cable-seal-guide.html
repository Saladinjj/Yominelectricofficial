Cable Seal Guide: Pull-Tight, Twist-Tite and Wire-Lead Seals

ET
ET Engineering Team November 5, 2026 10 min read

A cable seal is a single-use locking device that captures a length of wire or cable, so the loop cannot be opened and re-closed without destroying the seal or leaving visible damage. That is the whole job: not to stop a determined attacker, but to make any interference obvious to the next person who looks at it.

Buyers usually arrive at this decision with a price per thousand and a photograph of the seal they used last time. That is enough to reorder, but not enough to specify. The mechanism inside the seal body decides how it behaves in service, and three families are routinely confused with each other: pull-tight, twist-tite and wire-lead. This guide separates them, then sets out what to put in an enquiry so the quotation matches the application.

What a cable seal actually does

Every cable seal has two parts. A moulded body holds a one-way locking mechanism, and a length of wire or cable is anchored at one end. The free end passes through the item being protected, then into the body. The mechanism grips and does not release.

Because the grip is one-way, the loop can be shortened but never lengthened. Opening it requires cutting the wire or breaking the body. Either action leaves a seal that no longer matches its recorded number, which is precisely the evidence a utility, a warehouse or a customs officer is looking for.

This is why the seal number matters as much as the seal itself. A seal without a recorded number proves that something was opened, not when or by whom. The record is what turns a plastic part into an audit trail.

The three mechanisms buyers confuse

The difference is in how the wire is gripped. It is worth understanding because the wrong mechanism turns a routine inspection into a broken seal and a written explanation.

MechanismHow it locksBest suited toWhat to check before ordering
Pull-tightWire is pulled through a ratchet or collet and cannot be drawn backHigh-volume sealing where speed of application matters, such as meter covers and cabinetsThat the wire tail can be tucked or trimmed so it cannot be used to lever the body
Twist-titeThe body is twisted onto the wire, deforming it into a locking coil inside the housingMetering and revenue protection, where the seal must sit flush and resist casual probingThat the twist is fully seated; a half-twisted seal looks closed but is not locked
Wire-leadA lead or soft-metal ferrule is crimped over the wire endsLegacy utility installations and where a metal seal is specified by procedureCrimp tooling and operator training, because an under-crimped ferrule pulls free

None of these is universally better. A twist-tite body that sits flat against a meter terminal cover is a poor choice for a container door where the seal must hang free and survive handling. A pull-tight seal that applies in two seconds is the wrong answer where the procedure demands a crimped metal ferrule.

Where cable seals are used

The applications share one property: someone other than the owner has access to the asset, and the owner needs to know whether that access happened.

  • Electricity and water meters. The seal passes through the terminal cover screw, so removing the cover to bypass the meter breaks the seal.
  • Containers and trailers. A cable seal through the door locking bars gives a number that can be quoted on the shipping document.
  • Vehicle and tanker hatches. Where a bolt seal will not fit the hasp, a cable seal follows the geometry.
  • Warehouse cages and high-value storage. Short-life seals replaced on each shift, with the number logged.

A cable seal is the right family whenever the hasp is irregular, the run is long, or the seal must flex with the fitting. Where the hasp is a clean pair of aligned holes and the load is heavy, a bolt seal is the stronger mechanical answer.

Tamper-evident is not tamper-proof

These two words get used interchangeably in tenders, and they should not be. A cable seal cannot stop a person with cutters. What it can do is make the intervention permanent and visible: the wire is cut, the body is destroyed, or the number no longer matches the register.

That distinction changes how you buy. If the requirement is to deter, the seal needs to look like a seal and carry a number that is checked. If the requirement is to detect, the seal needs a mechanism that cannot be defeated and re-closed, plus a register that is actually reconciled. Buying a heavier seal without the register gives you the cost of control and none of the evidence.

The same logic explains why colour matters operationally rather than cosmetically. A site that changes seal colour by month or by route can spot last month's seal on a door from a distance, without reading a number.

What to specify before you request a quote

Seal quotations go wrong for boring reasons. The two most common are an unstated wire length and an unstated marking requirement, both of which are fixed at the moulding or printing stage and cannot be added later without a new run.

  • Application and hasp geometry — what the wire passes through, and the maximum and minimum loop the fitting allows.
  • Wire diameter and length — stated as a number, not as "standard".
  • Marking — logo only, logo plus sequential number, or logo plus number and barcode. Confirm how many digits the sequence needs.
  • Colour — and whether colour must be consistent across repeat orders.
  • Environment — indoor meter cupboard, outdoor cabinet, or unheated container. UV exposure and low temperature are the two conditions that age a plastic body.
  • Quantity and reorder pattern — a single large run and a monthly call-off are priced differently, so say which you need.

Ask for the marking proof before production. A logo that reads correctly on a screen can lose its fine detail at seal scale, and correcting it after the run is expensive.

Marking and an auditable seal register

A sequential number is only useful if it is written down. The practical minimum is a register that records the number range issued, the date, the location or asset sealed, and the person who applied it. At the next inspection, the number on the seal is compared with the number in the register.

Barcodes make that comparison cheaper. A scan at the door or at the meter replaces a manual read and removes transcription errors, which are the main reason registers drift out of agreement with reality.

Two design details protect the number itself. Marking should be moulded or laser-applied rather than printed on a label that can be peeled and moved, and the number should sit where it cannot be obscured by the wire once the seal is closed.

Receiving inspection

Check a sample from each carton before the batch goes into stores. Four checks catch almost everything:

  • The number sequence runs without gaps or duplicates across the cartons received.
  • The marking is legible at arm's length, and the logo matches the approved proof.
  • The wire is anchored in the body and does not pull out under a firm hand pull before first use.
  • A test seal, once closed, cannot be reopened without visible damage.

Keep the test seal and its number. It is the reference against which a disputed seal is later compared.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a cable seal and a bolt seal?

A cable seal uses a flexible wire or cable loop, so it follows an irregular hasp and can seal a meter cover screw or a door bar. A bolt seal uses a rigid steel pin through two aligned holes and is the stronger choice for container doors. Cable seals suit geometry; bolt seals suit heavy, well-aligned hasps.

Are cable seals reusable?

No. The locking mechanism is one-way by design, so a closed seal must be cut or destroyed to open it. That single-use property is what makes the seal evidence: a seal that could be reopened and re-closed would prove nothing.

Should the seal number be printed or moulded into the body?

Moulded or laser-applied marking is preferred, because a printed label can be peeled off and moved to another seal. Whatever method is used, the number must be recorded in a register at the point of application, otherwise it cannot be reconciled later.

How do I choose the wire length?

Measure the longest and shortest loop the fitting will accept, then specify a wire length that closes comfortably at both. A wire that is too short cannot be twisted or pulled tight; one that is far too long leaves a tail that can be used as a lever against the body.

Can YOMIN print our logo and a sequential number on the seal?

Yes. Custom logo marking is part of the standard specification for the twist-tite wire meter seal, and sequential numbering and barcoding are available on the container bolt seal. Confirm the digit count and send artwork before the production run begins.
