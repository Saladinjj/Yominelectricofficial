Terminal Block Size Chart: Matching Wire Range to Current and Pitch

ET
ET Engineering Team November 16, 2026 10 min read

A terminal block is sized by the conductor it has to accept, not by the width of the rail it sits on. The single number buyers reach for, the rated current, is the least reliable of the four figures printed on the body, because it is quoted at a reference ambient that no real panel ever reaches. This chart starts from the wire range, adds the current, and then applies the two checks that actually decide whether the part is right: derating inside the enclosure, and pitch against the system voltage.

Read it as a first filter, not a final answer. Every block carries its own marking, and where the marking disagrees with a generic chart, the marking wins. The purpose of the chart is to get you into the correct family and body size in one step, so that the quote you request is for something that exists rather than something that would have to be invented.

The four numbers on a terminal block

Every DIN rail or panel block carries the same four specifications, and they answer four different questions.

  • Rated voltage — the insulation class, which sets the pitch and the creepage distance. It is a safety number, not a performance number.
  • Rated current — the continuous current at a defined reference ambient, usually 40 °C, for a single block in free air.
  • Wire range — the smallest and largest conductor the clamp will accept, given separately for solid, stranded and ferruled conductors.
  • Pitch — the centre-to-centre width on the rail, which decides how many ways fit in a given duct and how much clearance exists between adjacent circuits.

Buyers routinely quote the current and ignore the wire range, then discover at installation that the 6 mm² cable will not enter a block rated for the current. The clamp opening, not the amp figure, is the hard constraint.

Two rows deserve a warning. The 16 mm² row is where screw clamps start to struggle with fine-stranded cable and stud terminals take over, and the 35 mm² row is where a DIN rail block stops being the sensible answer at all and a busbar or a link bar becomes cheaper and better. If you are working in AWG rather than square millimetres, convert first: a 6 AWG conductor is 13.3 mm², which sits between two rows and must go to the larger one.

Current rating is a derated number

The rated current printed on the block assumes a single block, in free air, at the reference ambient. A real panel violates all three assumptions at once, and three corrections apply in combination.

  • Ambient. A panel beside a furnace, in direct sun, or in an unventilated plant room runs well above 40 °C. The correction is applied per the manufacturer's table, not guessed.
  • Block density. Fifty blocks packed side by side heat each other. This is the factor most often ignored, and it is the one that fails first in a dense marshalling row.
  • Enclosure. A sealed IP66 box behaves very differently from a louvred cabinet. The block is the same; the current it can carry is not.

The practical consequence is that a block chosen exactly at its nameplate current in a dense, sealed row is already overloaded. Leave genuine margin rather than specifying to the printed figure and hoping the panel runs cool.

Pitch is a voltage decision, not a spacing preference

Two blocks with the same wire range can have different pitches because they are rated for different voltages. Pitch sets the creepage distance along the surface and the clearance through air between adjacent circuits, and those distances are what the insulation standard actually governs.

This is why a 5.2 mm block rated for a low-voltage control circuit must not be substituted for a wider block on a 690 V feeder, even though the cable fits perfectly. The substitution will look correct, work correctly, and fail an inspection — or worse, track across the surface after a few years of dust and moisture.

Bars, not blocks, once the count rises

Above roughly eight conductors to a single common point, a terminal bar is cheaper, cooler and easier to inspect than a chain of blocks with jumpers. A bar also removes the jumper as a failure point, which matters in a neutral or earth application where a loose jumper is a safety issue rather than an inconvenience.

When you specify a bar, give the number of holes, the hole diameter, the conductor range per hole and the mounting centres. Bars are made to a drawing, so a bar quoted without a hole schedule is a bar that will arrive wrong.

Checklist before you order a row

  • Conductor range, given separately for solid and fine-stranded, because the clamp opening differs.
  • Current per way after ambient and density derating, not the nameplate figure.
  • System voltage, which fixes the pitch and therefore the number of ways that fit the duct.
  • Number of ways plus spares, and the accessories: end stops, end plates, jumper bars, marking tags.
  • Mounting — rail profile for blocks, hole schedule for bars.
  • Environment, since a sealed enclosure changes the current the same block can carry.

Send the conductor range and the number of ways together. A row quoted on current alone will arrive with the right amp figure and the wrong clamp.

Frequently asked questions

How do I choose a terminal block size?

Start from the conductor range, not the current. Find the block family that accepts your largest conductor, check that its rated current after derating covers the load, then confirm the pitch suits the system voltage. Current alone will give you a block that cannot accept the cable.

Does a higher rated current always mean a better terminal block?

No. The rated current is quoted for a single block in free air at a reference ambient. In a dense, sealed row the effective rating falls, so a nominally larger block may run no cooler than a smaller one carrying less current. Density and ambient decide more than the nameplate figure.

Why is the same wire range sold at different pitches?

Because pitch is set by the voltage rating, not the conductor. Two blocks accepting the same cable can be rated for different system voltages, and the wider one carries more creepage and clearance. Substituting the narrow block on a higher-voltage circuit is a safety error even though the cable fits.

When should I use a terminal bar instead of blocks?

Once more than roughly eight conductors land on one common point. A bar removes the jumpers, runs cooler, is easier to inspect, and is cheaper per way. Specify it with the hole count, hole diameter, conductor range per hole and mounting centres.

Building a terminal row for a panel?

Send the conductor range, the number of ways and the system voltage. We will confirm the body size and pitch that fit the row, and flag where the row should become a bar instead.
