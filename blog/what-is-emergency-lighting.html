What Is Emergency Lighting: Systems, Standards and Backup Power

ET
ET Engineering Team November 21, 2026 12 min read

Emergency lighting is the part of a building's lighting installation that is designed to work when the normal supply does not. It provides enough light for people to see an escape route, find a exit sign and leave without injury, and it keeps lighting on in areas where a sudden loss of light would itself create danger. The defining feature is not the lamp: it is the independent source of energy behind it, and the fact that the installation is tested on a schedule rather than trusted.

The question usually comes up at specification stage, when someone has to choose between self-contained luminaires and a central battery system. The two are not rivals so much as different answers to the same constraint: how much energy has to be stored, where it is stored, and who is going to maintain it. This guide covers what emergency lighting has to do, how the main architectures differ, what a central power supply contains, and how to specify one without buying capacity you cannot maintain.

The three jobs emergency lighting has to do

Emergency lighting is specified against tasks, not against rooms, and the tasks have different light levels and different durations. Mixing them up is the most common reason a system passes its first test and fails its first inspection.

  • Escape route lighting: keeps the route to an exit visible, including changes of direction, steps and doorways, at a level that lets people move at walking pace.
  • Open area / anti-panic lighting: prevents the disorientation that follows a sudden blackout in a large space, so people can find a route rather than converge on one point.
  • High-risk task area lighting: keeps a dangerous activity — a machine, a process, a switching operation — lit long enough to shut it down safely.
  • Exit signage: identifies the route and the final exit, and in most jurisdictions is treated as part of the same emergency lighting system.

Maintained vs non-maintained

This is the first decision, and it is about the luminaire's normal state, not about the backup. A maintained luminaire is lit all the time and stays lit when the supply fails, so the change is invisible to the occupant. A non-maintained luminaire is dark in normal service and only comes on when the supply fails or a test is triggered. The choice follows the space: a cinema or a plant room is usually maintained, a normally lit corridor is usually non-maintained because the general lighting already does the job.

CharacteristicMaintainedNon-maintained
Normal stateLit continuouslyOff until the supply fails
Failure behaviourStays lit; no visible changeSwitches on immediately
Typical locationsCinemas, plant rooms, assembly areas, switching roomsCorridors, stairwells and rooms with general lighting
Maintenance signalA failed lamp is obvious during the dayA failed lamp is invisible until a test
Test implicationsFunction test confirms the battery path onlyFunction test is the only routine proof the luminaire works

The maintenance point is the one that surprises people. A non-maintained system hides its own faults: if a battery has failed, nobody knows until the monthly function test or the annual duration test. That is not an argument against non-maintained luminaires — it is an argument for a system that reports its own status, which is where controllers and monitored central supplies earn their cost.

Self-contained vs central battery

The second decision is where the energy is stored. A self-contained luminaire carries its own battery, charger and changeover inside the housing. A central battery system stores energy in one place — usually a cabinet in a plant room — and distributes it to luminaires over dedicated final circuits.

CriterionSelf-containedCentral battery
Where energy is storedInside each luminaireOne cabinet, shared across the building
Failure scopeOne luminaire, one faultA circuit or the whole system if the common plant fails
Maintenance accessAt the ceiling, per fittingAt one location, on one battery set
Battery life managementSpread across many small batteriesOne bank, replaced on a planned cycle
Best fitSmall buildings, retrofit, scattered exitsLarge or complex buildings, high-risk areas, many fittings
Typical constraintCeiling access and per-unit costDedicated distribution, cabinet space and ventilation

What a central battery system actually contains

A central emergency power supply is a small UPS with a different duty: it may sit idle for years and then be expected to deliver its full rated duration on the first attempt. That duty shapes the design more than efficiency does.

  • A charger sized to recharge the bank within the recovery time the specification allows, not merely to float it.
  • A battery bank, either maintenance-free lead-acid or a lithium iron phosphate assembly, sized on load and duration together.
  • An inverter or DC output stage that produces the voltage the luminaires expect — safety extra-low voltage DC in Type A systems.
  • A changeover device that detects loss of the normal supply and transfers without a gap that resets the luminaires.
  • A controller with status indication, test scheduling and fault reporting, so a failed battery is a message and not a discovery.
  • Distribution and protection for the emergency final circuits, kept separate from normal lighting so a normal-circuit fault cannot take the emergency circuits with it.

Type A output and why the voltage class matters

Fire emergency lighting systems are commonly classified by what they deliver to the luminaire. Type A systems distribute a safety extra-low voltage DC output; Type B systems distribute at mains level. The distinction is practical rather than academic: a Type A output keeps the voltage at the fitting low, which changes the wiring rules, the luminaire construction and the shock risk during maintenance. YOMIN's Type A centralised power supplies are built around that low-voltage DC output, which is why the cabinet, the distribution and the luminaire set have to be specified as one system rather than assembled from unrelated parts.

The trade-off is distribution loss. Low-voltage DC over long runs carries more current for the same power, so voltage drop becomes the limiting calculation and conductor size grows faster than the load does. In a large building that is what pushes the cabinet closer to the load, or splits one large system into two smaller ones.

Duration: the number that sizes everything

Rated duration is the headline figure of any emergency lighting system, and it is also the figure most often assumed rather than calculated. Duration is set by the applicable code and by the evacuation strategy of the building, not by the battery catalogue. Whatever figure applies, the battery has to deliver it at end of life, in the ambient temperature of the cabinet, with the actual connected load — not at the beginning of life on a bench.

Requirement bandWhat it is usually forWhat it changes in the design
Short duration (around 60 minutes)Small premises with a simple, short escape routeSmaller bank; recovery time dominates the charger sizing
Standard duration (around 90 minutes)Most commercial and institutional buildingsBank sized with margin for ageing and cabinet temperature
Extended duration (120 minutes and above)Large, tall or complex buildings; staged evacuationLarger bank, more cabinet volume, ventilation and floor loading
High-risk task durationAreas where shutdown takes timeDuration matched to the shutdown procedure, not to the escape route

Always confirm the applicable figure with the local authority having jurisdiction and the project specification. The table above describes how the requirement bands change the hardware; it is not a substitute for the code that governs the building.

Battery chemistry: lead-acid vs lithium iron phosphate

Both chemistries appear in central emergency supplies, and the choice is about maintenance model and space as much as about cost per watt-hour.

AspectMaintenance-free lead-acidLithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4)
Footprint and weightLarger and heavier for the same energySmaller and lighter for the same energy
Replacement cycleShorter service life; planned replacement is normalLonger service life and more usable capacity over it
Temperature sensitivityCapacity falls noticeably in a cold or hot cabinetMore tolerant, but still needs a controlled cabinet
MonitoringBlock voltage and impedance checksCell-level monitoring with a battery management system
FamiliarityWell understood by most maintenance teamsNeeds a team comfortable with BMS fault codes
Best fitCost-sensitive projects with an established battery regimeSpace-constrained rooms and long-life specifications

Whichever chemistry is chosen, the cabinet is part of the battery specification. Ventilation, ambient temperature and access for replacement decide whether the bank delivers its rated duration in year five. A fire protection EPS cabinet is built with that in mind: lockable double doors, louvred ventilation and a layout that lets a technician reach the battery set without dismantling the cabinet.

Control, testing and the paper trail

An emergency lighting system is only as good as its test record. Manual testing means someone walks the building with a key and a clipboard; automatic testing means the controller schedules function and duration tests, records the results and reports failures. The second is not a luxury in a large building — it is the only way the record stays true, because a manual regime decays the moment the building gets busy.

A wall-mounted emergency lighting controller acts as the central host: it holds the circuit map, triggers tests, logs results and shows faults at a point someone actually walks past. Combined with a monitored central supply, that turns 'we think the system works' into a dated record per circuit, which is exactly what an inspection asks for.

Specifying a system: the checklist that prevents rework

  • Establish the required duration and the escape strategy before choosing any hardware; both come from the code and the building, not the catalogue.
  • Decide self-contained or central battery building-wide, not room by room, because the maintenance model has to be one model.
  • Calculate the connected load at the luminaire level, then add the distribution loss — especially on low-voltage DC circuits.
  • Confirm the output class the luminaires need (Type A extra-low voltage DC or mains-level) before ordering cabinets.
  • Size the charger on recovery time, not only on float current; a bank that cannot recharge between outages is not a backup.
  • Plan cabinet location for cable run, floor loading, ventilation and battery replacement access.
  • Agree the test and reporting regime at handover, including who reads the controller and what happens when it reports a fault.
  • Keep emergency circuits separate from normal lighting so a normal-side fault cannot disable the emergency side.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between emergency lighting and escape lighting?

Escape lighting is one function of emergency lighting: it lights the route out. Emergency lighting is the wider term and also covers anti-panic lighting in open areas, high-risk task lighting and illuminated exit signage. A building's emergency lighting design normally contains all of these, at different light levels and sometimes different durations.

Is emergency lighting the same as a UPS?

No. A UPS is built to ride through short interruptions and protect loads continuously, usually with a short autonomy. An emergency lighting supply may sit idle for years and then be required to deliver its full rated duration on demand, with the battery at end of life. The duty cycle, the testing regime and the code requirements are different, which is why fire protection EPS cabinets are specified separately from general UPS equipment.

What does Type A mean in emergency lighting?

Type A describes a system whose output to the luminaires is a safety extra-low voltage DC supply. Type B systems distribute at mains level. Type A keeps the voltage at the fitting low, which affects wiring rules, luminaire construction and maintenance safety, and makes voltage drop on long DC runs the key distribution calculation.

How long should emergency lighting last?

Duration is set by the code that governs the building and by its evacuation strategy, and it typically falls in bands around 60, 90 or 120 minutes, with longer durations for high-risk tasks. Confirm the figure with the local authority and the project specification. Whatever the figure, the battery must deliver it at end of life with the real connected load, in the real cabinet temperature.

How often is emergency lighting tested?

The testing interval is a code matter, but the usual pattern is a short function test at frequent intervals and a full-duration test less often. What matters practically is that the test is recorded per circuit and that failures are actioned. Automatic testing through a controller exists precisely because manual regimes decay.

Can I add luminaires to an existing central battery system?

Only within the spare capacity of the battery bank, the charger and the distribution, and within the voltage-drop limit of the circuit. Adding fittings to a system that was sized exactly for its original load shortens the duration for everyone on it. Check spare capacity before adding, and re-run the duration calculation if the load changes materially.

Why does cabinet temperature matter so much?

Battery capacity falls as temperature moves away from its optimum, and a cabinet in an unventilated plant room in summer is a real derating case. Since duration is what the system is bought for, a warm cabinet effectively means a shorter rated duration at the moment it is needed. Ventilation and location are part of the specification, not an installation detail.
