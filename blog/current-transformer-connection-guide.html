Current Transformer Connection: P1/P2, S1/S2 and Secondary Wiring

ET
ET Engineering Team November 18, 2026 11 min read

Connect a current transformer by its markings, not by its position. P1 goes to the side facing the supply, P2 to the side facing the load, S1 to the meter terminal that corresponds to P1, and S2 back to the meter's common. The secondary is a current source: it must never be open-circuited while the primary carries current, and it must be shorted before you break any secondary connection.

Almost every complaint about a CT-operated meter traces back to those four terminals rather than to the meter. A reading that is negative, a reading that is three times too high on one phase, a meter that is accurate at light load and drifts at full load — each of these has a wiring cause, and each is cheaper to fix before the panel is closed than after the installation has been billed against it.

This guide covers the connection itself: what the markings mean, which way the polarity points, how far the secondary can run, and the checks worth doing at commissioning. Choosing the ratio, class and window is a separate decision, covered in the current transformer selection guide.

The four markings that decide the connection

A metering CT is a directional device. It has a primary side and a secondary side, and each side has a marked start. The whole connection follows from those markings.

MarkingWhat it isWhere it connects
P1Primary start, the side that faces the supplyThe incoming conductor, upstream of the load
P2Primary finish, the side that faces the loadThe conductor continuing to the load or the next device
S1Secondary start, in phase with P1The meter current input that the meter expects to be in phase with its voltage input
S2Secondary finish, in phase with P2The meter current common, or the return of that current channel

On some bodies the same terminals are marked K and L instead of P1 and P2. The function does not change: K is the start, L is the finish. What matters is that you record the convention on the drawing, because a panel wired to one convention and documented in the other is a panel that will be "corrected" into a fault by the next electrician.

Polarity: why a reversed CT reads negative

A watt-hour meter multiplies voltage by current and integrates the product. The multiplication is only signed correctly when the current sample arrives in the phase relationship the meter expects. Turn the CT around on the conductor, or swap S1 and S2 at the meter, and the meter sees the current inverted. The result is a negative power reading on that phase — or, on an import-only register, a reading that runs backwards or refuses to accumulate at all.

Two situations make this more common than it should be. The first is a retrofit where the conductors are already terminated and the CT has to be opened to fit around a busbar: it is easy to close a split-core CT with P1 on the load side. The second is a multi-ratio or wound-primary CT where the secondary terminals are not arranged in the order the drawing implies. In both cases the fix is the same and takes minutes: reverse the CT on the conductor rather than reversing the secondary, so the physical installation keeps matching its label.

Directional and four-quadrant meters are the exception that proves the rule. They are designed to measure both import and export, so a reversed CT does not produce an obviously broken reading — it produces a plausible reading with the sign of the energy flow inverted. That is worse, because it survives commissioning and only surfaces when a bill is disputed.

Secondary wiring: lead length, cross-section and burden

The secondary is a current loop, so its resistance adds directly to the burden the CT has to drive. A CT rated for a given VA figure is rated at a stated accuracy class; exceed the VA and the accuracy is no longer the class on the label, even though nothing looks wrong.

PracticeReasonWhat goes wrong otherwise
Use the largest practical secondary cross-sectionLoop resistance falls with copper areaBurden rises with run length, and the class slips
Keep the run as short as the panel allowsEvery extra metre adds both wires to the loopA CT specified for 5 VA behaves like a 2.5 VA unit
Twist or screen the pairRejects switching noise picked up on a long loopJitter on low-load readings and unstable demand values
Terminate in one continuous loop per channelPreserves the marked direction of the channelCirculating currents and readings that disagree between meters
Never fit a fuse in the secondaryAn open secondary is the fault, not the protectionDangerous voltage at the terminals and possible CT insulation failure

When the run is long, the honest options are to move the meter closer to the CTs, to increase the secondary cross-section, or to specify a higher VA rating at the same class. Software scaling cannot recover a class that the wiring has already destroyed.

Earthing and shorting the secondary

One point of the secondary circuit is earthed, normally at the CT terminal block or at the meter, so that a primary-to-secondary insulation failure cannot put dangerous voltage on the metering wiring. Bond it once. A second earth on the same loop creates a parallel path that carries current around the measurement and quietly destroys accuracy.

Shorting is a different operation and is often confused with earthing. Before any secondary connection is opened — during testing, during a meter change, during a terminal check — the secondary must be shorted with a proper shorting link or shorting terminal. With the primary energised, an open secondary has no path for the magnetising current, so flux collapses and a very high voltage appears across the open terminals. That voltage is a hazard to the person working and to the CT's own insulation.

Three-phase connection: the faults that account for most callouts

SymptomUsual causeCorrection
One phase reads negativeCT reversed on that conductor, or S1/S2 swapped at the meterReverse the CT on the conductor; check the phase assignment of the voltage input
All three phases read about one third of expectedTwo CTs share a return, or the return is looped through a second channelGive each channel its own return pair
Readings drift upward at high loadSecondary burden above the CT's VA ratingShorten the run, increase cross-section, or re-specify the CT
Meter accurate on one meter, wrong on anotherCT ratio programmed into the meter does not match the CT fittedConfirm the ratio at both ends before blaming the hardware
No reading at all on one channelSecondary left shorted after commissioning, or a link left in placeRemove the shorting link and re-check

Commissioning checks before you close the panel

  • Markings recorded. P1/P2 orientation and the S1/S2 landing points written onto the panel drawing, not left in someone's memory.
  • Ratio confirmed twice. Once on the CT label and once against the ratio programmed into the meter. A 1600/5 CT on a meter set to 1000/5 gives a reading that is wrong by a fixed factor and looks entirely plausible.
  • Direction verified under load. With a known load, every phase should read positive and in proportion. A phase that reads negative is a wiring fault, not a tariff setting.
  • Burden checked. Secondary loop resistance measured, or at least the run length and cross-section compared against the CT's VA rating.
  • Single earth confirmed. One bond per secondary loop, and no accidental second path through a screen or a conduit.
  • Shorting links removed. Every link fitted for testing is out, and the fact is recorded on the commissioning sheet.

Frequently asked questions

Which terminal of a current transformer goes to the meter?

S1 goes to the meter's current input for that phase and S2 to the common of that channel. S1 is the terminal in phase with P1, the primary start that faces the supply.

What happens if a current transformer secondary is left open?

With the primary energised, an open secondary leaves the CT with no path for its magnetising current. Flux collapses, a very high voltage appears across the open terminals, and both the person working and the CT insulation are at risk. Short the secondary before opening any connection.

Why does my meter show a negative reading on one phase?

The current sample is inverted on that channel. Usually the CT was closed with P1 facing the load instead of the supply, or S1 and S2 were swapped at the meter. Reverse the CT on the conductor so the installation keeps matching its label.

How far can I run current transformer secondary cable?

There is no single distance, because the limit is burden rather than length. The loop resistance of both wires plus the meter's own burden must stay inside the CT's VA rating at the accuracy class you need. Longer runs need a larger cross-section, a higher VA rating or a shorter path.

Connecting CTs in a panel or retrofitting a metering point?

Send the ratio, the accuracy class, the conductor size and the length of the secondary run. We will confirm the body and the burden that fit the position.
